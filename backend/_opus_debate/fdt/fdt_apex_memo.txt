FUTURE DISRUPTIVE TECH - LANE A APEX MEMO, as of 11 August 2026.

Rubric And Weighting
Four pillars carry roughly 25 points each, applied only to names that clear the gates: pricing power
and margin direction; the order book and its dated milestones; capital discipline and the balance
sheet; and a valuation guard. The guard is a guard only. It can cut a position's size or veto a name
outright, but it was never allowed to lift one name above another that passed the first three pillars
cleanly. Two seats are held despite the shares trading above our own reconciled value (GE Vernova
about 16% above, Intuitive Surgical about 18% above); in both cases the guard took its deduction in
the size, not in the ranking. There is no commodity tilt in this book and none was applied: where a
commodity appears - copper into connectors and grid gear, natural gas into power economics - it is an
input cost and therefore a margin headwind, never a revenue tailwind.

Data Quality - A Parsing Error You Should Know About
The input file's margin-of-safety number is wrong for six names, and the error is systematic rather
than random. The builder reads the fair value out of the analyst's prose before it reads the typed
number, and the text pattern it uses grabs whatever digits follow the words "base case". So a fair
value written as "$370 per share (base case, 24-month horizon)" is read as $24, and the file then
reports a margin of safety of -92.6% for a name that is genuinely about 14% below our reconciled
value. The six affected names in this file are Huntington Ingalls (file says -92.6%, true +14.1%),
Keysight (-94.7%, true -11.8%), General Dynamics (-95.3%, true -16.0%), Woodward (-95.2%, true
-6.6%), Crane (-92.0%, true -2.8%) and Leonardo DRS (+4,380.5%, true -16.0%); four more are distorted
more mildly where the parser averaged two unrelated numbers (Flowserve, Corning, Teradyne, Powell).
Every guard reading in this memo and in the published rows uses the typed reconciled fair value
against the traded price, which is the number the analysts actually reached. Taken at face value the
file would have vetoed our best-value seat, so the correction matters: uncorrected, six of thirty-six
names would have been sent through a veto that none of them earns.

Shared-Axis Stress, Argued Against The Measured Correlations
The measured two-year weekly correlations for this slate average 0.36 across all pairs, with three
pairs at or above 0.60: Vertiv and GE Vernova at 0.67, TE Connectivity and Emerson at 0.61, and
Keysight and Emerson at 0.61. Those are real numbers and the stress test is argued against them
rather than asserted around them.
- Data-centre power and hyperscaler contracts (three names: Constellation Energy, Vertiv, GE Vernova).
  This is the book's genuine concentration and the measured tape agrees: Vertiv and GE Vernova are the
  most correlated pair in the slate at 0.67. We kept all three rather than diversifying, because each
  sits on a different rung - one sells the electrons under twenty-year contract, one sells the room
  hardware, one sells the turbines and the grid gear - and because the alternates that would replace a
  leg (Comfort Systems, Sterling Infrastructure) sit on the same rung as one we already hold and would
  not reduce the exposure. Resolution: kept with a written cap of 1.55 combined units across the three,
  which is where they are sized today and which the deterministic layer will hold.
- The cross-chain pair the rules require us to check explicitly - power for artificial intelligence
  together with nuclear and small modular reactors, which are the same bet on data-centre power demand
  - is seated, and it meets inside a single name: Constellation Energy rides both chains. Huntington
  Ingalls is the other nuclear seat, and it is deliberately not a data-centre bet at all: its revenue
  is a multi-year defence appropriation. So the pair is flagged, and it is contained by the fact that
  only one of the two nuclear seats touches data-centre demand. The 1.50-unit cap across Constellation
  Energy and Huntington Ingalls records the chain concentration in numbers.
- Global growth and industrial capital spending (two names: TE Connectivity, Emerson). Measured at
  0.61, the second-highest pair in the book. Kept rather than diversified because roughly two thirds
  of Emerson's revenue is maintenance on an installed base and TE Connectivity's order book already
  covers the next fiscal year, so the two do not depend on the same incremental order. Resolution:
  kept with a cap of 1.45 combined units.
- Long-duration multiples and the rate axis (two names: Keysight, Intuitive Surgical). No measured
  pair correlation is flagged between them; the interesting fact is that Keysight's strongest measured
  link is to Emerson at 0.61, an industrial, not to any long-duration name. That is evidence Keysight
  trades as an instrument maker levered to capital spending rather than as a rate-sensitive growth
  multiple, which weakens rather than strengthens the case for treating this as a tight cluster. Kept
  with a cap of 1.45 combined units on the grounds that a sharp rate move would still hit both.
- Single-customer concentration: only Huntington Ingalls carries it, selling almost entirely to the US
  Navy. This is exactly why General Dynamics stays an alternate: it shares the same 29 July 2026
  submarine award, so seating both would have turned a one-name exposure into a two-name one.

Bear Rebuttal - The Strongest Case Against Each Seat
- Constellation Energy: if the capacity auction closing 15 December 2026 clears under its
  administrative price ceiling and the Crane restart slips past 2027, the two nearest proof points
  both fail and the shares re-rate toward the adverse case of $180.
- Vertiv: the company stopped publishing its order book quarterly in February 2026, so a buyer today
  is trusting a $15.0 billion figure that is six months old in the fastest-moving spending cycle in
  the market.
- GE Vernova: we would be paying about 16% more than our own reconciled value for an order book that
  is already signed at fixed prices, so any slippage in delivery or margin conversion comes straight
  out of the buyer's pocket.
- TE Connectivity: roughly half the revenue still rides vehicle production, and a car cycle that rolls
  over would swamp the data-centre and grid growth long before the fiscal-2027 outlook arrives.
- Huntington Ingalls: cash conversion is the thinnest in the book at under 3% of revenue, and a
  shipbuilder with a record order book that cannot turn it into cash is a company that has sold a
  great deal of work at the wrong price.
- Keysight: the order book is measured in months rather than years and carries no dated contractual
  milestone, so one quarter of artificial-intelligence test spending digesting removes the whole
  reason to own it.
- Intuitive Surgical: the competitive shelter is wide but thinning, and rival platforms that lock
  instrument choice at the point of purchase attack precisely the recurring revenue that is the entire
  investment case.
- Emerson: gross margin has stopped expanding, which means the toll collector is not taking price, and
  if orders normalise from five quarters above sales the seat has neither growth nor pricing power to
  fall back on.

A Note On How Weight Is Published
This book publishes equal-weighted, so a chain's published share is simply its seat count: three of
eight is 37.5%, which is why power for artificial intelligence and electrification and grid both show
above the 30% guideline in the exposure table. That is arithmetic under equal weighting, not
concentration drift, and the constraint that actually binds the published book is the limit of three
names per chain, which is respected. Measured on the sizes recorded here - 5.20 units in total - every
chain sits inside 30%: power for artificial intelligence 29.8%, electrification and grid 29.8%,
nuclear 28.8%, robotics 26.9%, quantum 14.4%. Those sizes are the audit trail that would decide the
book if equal weighting were ever switched off.
