# Forensic dossier — Power Solutions International, Inc. (Nasdaq: PSIX)

*Prepared 4 August 2026. Live price used throughout: $34.24 per share (Nasdaq, US dollars), an
intraday quote that was up about 19% on the day from a prior close of $28.78. That move was not
company news: Caterpillar rose about 6% and Generac about 8% in the same session, so the day's
gain is sector beta in the power-equipment complex, not a Power Solutions development.*

## 1. Narrative arc

Power Solutions International builds emission-certified engines and packaged power systems —
generator sets and, increasingly, the steel enclosures those generators sit inside. It is
controlled by China's Weichai Group, which held about 46% of the shares as of May 2026 and, with
founder voting agreements, more than half the votes for director elections. Nasdaq classifies it
as a controlled company.

For roughly two years the story was one of the cleanest small-cap turnarounds in industrials. The
company lost about $48M in 2021 on $456M of sales with a 9.0% gross margin. By 2024 sales were
$476M, the gross margin had climbed to 29.5%, and net income was $69M. Then the data-center theme
arrived. Full-year 2025 sales jumped about 52% to $722M, and management framed the business as a
supplier of behind-the-meter power to the artificial-intelligence build-out. The shares reached
$121.78 within the last twelve months.

That arc broke in two steps, and both steps are now the subject of a federal securities-fraud
complaint (Dishion v. Power Solutions International, N.D. Ill., case 26-cv-03149) covering
purchases between 8 May 2025 and 2 March 2026.

- **6 November 2025 (third-quarter 2025 results).** Gross margin fell about five percentage
  points year over year, attributed to inefficiencies from an accelerated production ramp for
  data-center product lines, and full-year growth was framed at roughly 45% — a sharp step down
  from the 74% and 65% growth rates the company had been printing. The shares fell more than 19%.
- **2 March 2026 (fourth-quarter and full-year 2025 results).** Gross margin fell about eight
  percentage points year over year on the same explanation, and management described itself as
  only beginning to see measurable improvement from its supply-chain and cost actions. The shares
  fell $24.84, or about 29%, to close at $60.91 on 3 March 2026.

The story today is a third thing again: not growth, and not yet a demonstrated recovery. In the
first quarter of 2026 sales fell about 5% year over year and management withdrew from formal
full-year guidance entirely. The pitch has narrowed to a single testable promise — that
second-half 2026 sales will land roughly where second-half 2025 landed.

**Claim: 2025 was a record year on every line.** Half true, and the half that is not true matters.
Sales genuinely grew about 52%. Net income of $114M, however, includes a large non-cash tax
benefit — the company booked a negative income-tax line of $10.6M for the full year, driven by a
roughly $20M credit in the second quarter that looks like the release of a valuation allowance on
deferred tax assets. Taxed at a normal rate, 2025 pre-tax income of $103M would have produced
about $78M of net income, or roughly $3.35 per share rather than $4.94. Roughly a third of the
headline earnings growth was an accounting event, not an operating one.

**Claim: gross-margin pressure is temporary ramp-up inefficiency.** This is the load-bearing claim
and the one the lawsuit attacks. The chief financial officer told the first-quarter 2026 call that
the margin decline reflected a weaker product mix, lower oil-and-gas contribution, and elevated
production costs from capacity ramp-up at the Wisconsin operations. The problem is the shape of
the curve. Gross margin has now fallen in four of the last five quarters — 29.5%, 28.0%, 23.7%,
21.7%, 22.7% — a cumulative decline of roughly seven percentage points. A genuine ramp cost is a
step down followed by recovery. Five quarters is not a step; it is a level change. The one point
of support for management is real: the first quarter of 2026 improved about 100 basis points
sequentially from the fourth quarter of 2025, the first improvement in the sequence.

**Claim: data-center demand remains solid.** Unverifiable from the outside, and management has
made it deliberately so. On the first-quarter 2026 call the company declined to give segment
mix within end markets, declined to give backlog figures, and, when an analyst pressed on
hyperscaler orders, said plainly that it does not name individual customers and never has. A
March 2026 investor question-and-answer session was reported to describe firm orders planned out
at least twelve months, and enclosures in the 3-to-4.5 megawatt range with plans up to 5 megawatts,
but no dollar backlog, no book-to-bill, and no named counterparty has been published. For a lane
where the whole question is whether announced demand converts into contracted volume, this is a
complete evidentiary blank.

**Claim: the business is converting profit into cash.** Weakly. Trailing-twelve-month operating
cash flow is $34.4M against $102.7M of trailing EBITDA — a conversion rate of about a third,
because inventory sits at $129M against a quarterly sales run rate of $129M. Free cash flow of
$26.0M on $716M of sales is a 3.6% margin. Capital spending of $8.5M against $5.4M of depreciation
is modest, which raises its own question: the company says it is expanding capacity, and it bought
MTL Manufacturing & Equipment in Beloit, Wisconsin (about 185,000 square feet, announced 2 March
2026), but organic capital spending is barely above maintenance. The capacity story is being bought,
not built.

**Where the vendor data disagrees with the company.** Two disagreements are worth flagging for the
committee. First, the screener's revenue-growth input of +18.2% is a trailing-twelve-month blend
that masks the fact that the most recent quarter turned negative; the company reported about +52%
for full-year 2025 and about −5% for the first quarter of 2026. Second, the vendor's quarterly
revenue for the third quarter of 2024 ($215.3M) does not reconcile with the filed full-year 2024
total — the four vendor quarters sum to $565M against a reported $476M. Any per-quarter comparison
against that period should be treated as unreliable.

## 3. Tone and confidence trajectory

**Declining, and sharply.** The trajectory is legible even without a full transcript archive.

- Through mid-2025 the company was giving growth percentages for the full year and expanding the
  narrative into a new end market.
- By November 2025 the growth number was being revised down inside the year.
- By March 2026 the language had turned to supply-chain performance and manufacturing cost
  structure — defensive vocabulary, not offensive.
- By May 2026 the company had stopped giving formal full-year guidance and reduced its outlook to
  a sequential statement — that the second quarter would look like the first — plus a hope that the
  second half of 2026 matches the second half of 2025. Management explicitly hedged even that,
  attaching it to customer scheduling, parts availability, and supply-chain factors.

That is a two-year slide from specific, forward, quantified guidance to a qualitative,
heavily-caveated, backward-looking comparison.

## 4. Guidance credibility

**Low.** The company set a growth expectation for 2025, cut it inside the year (November 2025),
missed on margin twice in a row on the same stated cause, and has now withdrawn from formal
guidance altogether. First-quarter 2026 revenue of $128.6M came in far below the roughly $161M the
street was carrying. There is no sandbagging pattern here; there is the opposite — a pattern of
guidance that proved too optimistic and was then withdrawn rather than reset.

The one credibility-positive item: the sequential gross-margin improvement of about 100 basis
points in the first quarter of 2026 is the first thing management promised and then delivered in
over a year.

## 5. Analyst pressure points

Three questions recur, and management has answered none of them.

1. **Backlog and order coverage.** Analysts have asked directly for order-book figures. The company
   describes orders it has already received as supporting the second-half expectation, but publishes
   no number. Without a backlog figure and a book-to-bill direction, the second-half claim cannot be
   tested until it either happens or does not.
2. **Customer concentration.** Jefferies' analyst asked for capacity figures and customer names on
   the first-quarter 2026 call. Management refused both. In a lane where a single hyperscaler order
   can be half a year's revenue, refusing to name counterparties leaves outside investors unable to
   distinguish a durable programme from one large order.
3. **Oil and gas.** Craig-Hallum's analyst pushed on why oil-and-gas orders are weak while oil prices
   are high. The chief financial officer confirmed the disconnect but offered no mechanism. That
   matters, because oil and gas was the high-margin part of the mix; its absence is the stated reason
   the margin fell, and nobody has explained when or whether it returns.

## 6. Red flags and green flags

**Red**

- 🔴 **An active securities-fraud class action** over the exact operating metric that decides the
  thesis — whether the company could convert data-center demand without destroying its margin.
  Lead-plaintiff deadline passed 19 May 2026; the case is live.
- 🔴 **The controlling shareholder sold into the top.** Weichai sold 232,683 shares between 22 and 26
  August 2025 at weighted-average prices between roughly $83 and $91, reducing its stake toward
  46%. Those sales occurred inside the class period and roughly ten weeks before the first negative
  margin disclosure. The stock is now $34.24.
- 🔴 **Insider selling is one-way and enormous.** Open-market activity over the past year runs one
  purchase of 300 shares against 86 sales totalling roughly 897,000 shares; press coverage puts the
  total value of insider sales over the last year at more than $106M against a current market
  capitalisation of about $789M.
- 🔴 **Chief executive resigned one day after the quarterly filing.** The first-quarter 10-Q was filed
  11 May 2026; Constantine "Dino" Xykis resigned 12 May 2026. The chief financial officer, Xun
  "Kenneth" Li, was made interim chief executive while continuing as chief financial officer —
  meaning one person held both roles at a company already facing fraud allegations about its
  reported margins. A permanent chief executive, Richard Hu, was named only on 27 July 2026.
- 🔴 **Separate board-level fiduciary-duty investigations** by at least five plaintiffs' firms,
  concerning whether directors permitted the alleged overstatement of demand and understatement of
  ramp costs.
- 🔴 **Margin compression on falling volume.** This is the worst of the two possible bad outcomes.
  Losing margin while gaining volume is commoditisation, which at least buys share. Losing margin
  while volume falls buys nothing.
- 🔴 **A history of accounting failure.** Retained earnings stand at just $29.8M despite $114M of
  reported 2025 net income, a reminder that this company spent years destroying capital and went
  through a restatement and delisting episode last decade. The institutional memory is not clean.

**Green**

- 🟢 **The balance sheet is genuinely sound.** Cash of $49.5M, net debt of $117.6M including $56.9M of
  lease obligations (about $61M excluding them), and net debt of roughly 1.15 times trailing EBITDA.
  There is no maturity wall and no financing gun to the head. This is not a serial diluter: the
  share count has moved from 22.9M to 23.1M over five years. That is unusual and creditable in this
  sector, and it removes the most common way small-cap power stories destroy shareholders.
- 🟢 **The first sequential margin improvement in a year** arrived in the first quarter of 2026,
  about 100 basis points better than the fourth quarter of 2025.
- 🟢 **Vertical integration is being executed, not announced.** The MTL Manufacturing acquisition
  brings fabrication and welding for enclosures in-house in Beloit, Wisconsin. Enclosures are the
  labour-intensive bottleneck; owning that step is the right structural response to the cost problem.
- 🟢 **The end-market demand is real.** Data-centre power is not a promoted theme; it is a physical
  shortage. The company is engineering a larger diesel engine above 3 megawatts for that market.

## 7. Hidden signals

The most telling shift is in what management stopped saying. Through 2025 the company put a growth
percentage on the year. In 2026 it stopped. The replacement formulation — that the second half of
2026 should look roughly like the second half of 2025 — is a comparison against a period the market
already knows was margin-impaired, dressed as an aspiration. A company confident in its order book
publishes the order book.

The second signal is the refusal to disclose end-market product mix. The company will tell you
that data-centre demand is solid and that oil and gas is soft, but will not tell you the split. That
combination lets the same sentence be true whether data-centre revenue is growing 50% or flat.

The third signal is the shape of the leadership change. An engineer chief executive left the day
after a filing; the chief financial officer doubled as interim chief executive for eleven weeks; and
the permanent replacement was recruited through an outside search rather than promoted from a bench.
That is a board reaching outside because it does not trust what is inside.

## 8. Capital-allocation verdict

**Classification: DETERIORATING.**

The single most important thing to weigh before sizing: *this company will not tell you the size,
duration, or ownership of its order book, and the one number it does control — gross margin — has
gone the wrong way for five quarters.* Everything bullish here is an inference from an end market
that is genuinely booming; everything verifiable about the operator is going backwards. In a lane
where the whole discipline is refusing to pay for undated backlog language, Power Solutions has
published nothing that can be dated.

**Moat.** The moat type is a narrow regulatory-licence-plus-scale hybrid: emissions certification
for engines across US and international standards is a genuine barrier, and packaging engines into
certified, thermally-managed enclosures is a real engineering capability. But the evidence says the
moat is eroding, not wide. Gross margin peaked at 29.5% in the first quarter of 2025 and has fallen
to 22.7%, and operating income has fallen from $32.5M in the second quarter of 2025 to $11.4M in the
first quarter of 2026 — high returns that are falling. The customer set is a handful of
data-centre developers with the option to buy from Caterpillar, Cummins, Generac or Rolls-Royce
instead, and the company's own refusal to name a customer suggests the relationship is
transactional rather than designed-in.

**Secular threat.** Manageable, not terminal. The structural force here — electricity demand from
computing — runs in the company's favour, not against it. The threat is competitive, not secular:
larger, better-capitalised engine makers can and will take the same orders, and rising steel and
copper costs are a direct margin headwind for a business whose growth product is a welded steel
box. Note the direction carefully — in this chain a commodity price increase is an input cost, not
a revenue tailwind, and the company's negative measured sensitivity to natural gas confirms it.

**Is the cheapness a fear-print or a value trap?** At $34.24 the shares trade at roughly 8.3 times
trailing EBITDA (using enterprise value excluding lease liabilities), against roughly 20 times for
Cummins and 25 times for Generac. That looks like a fear-print. But the trailing figure is built on
two quarters that will not repeat: on the first quarter of 2026 run rate the same shares trade at
roughly 16 times. The discount is real but far smaller than it looks, and it is being applied to a
base that is currently shrinking. That is closer to a value trap than a mispricing until the second
half of 2026 proves otherwise.
