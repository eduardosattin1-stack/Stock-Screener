# Zimmer Biomet Holdings (ZBH) — a deep dive on management and the setup

Prepared 4 August 2026. Source material: five consecutive quarterly earnings calls (Q1 2025 through Q1 2026), the screener's financial trajectory, and figures verified against the company's own filings and dated press releases.

A note on the price used throughout: the shares last traded at **$96.28 on the New York Stock Exchange, in US dollars** (quote taken 4 August 2026; the previous close was $96.98). Second-quarter results are scheduled for the morning of 5 August 2026 — tomorrow — so every figure below is pre-print.

---

## 1. Narrative arc

The story management tells has changed shape three times in five quarters, and the direction of travel matters more than any single quarter.

**Act one (early 2025) — the product-cycle story.** The whole case rested on what the chief executive, Ivan Tornos, calls the "magnificent seven": a batch of new implants — the Z1 hip stem, the HAMMR surgical impactor, the OrthoGrid navigation software, the Persona OsseoTi cementless knee, the Oxford partial cementless knee, Persona Revision. In May 2025 the message was that these products close every gap in the core portfolio and put the company "on the offensive once again." Full-year organic revenue growth, meaning growth excluding acquisitions and currency swings, was guided to 3% to 5%.

**Act two (mid-to-late 2025) — the acceleration story, delivered.** This is the part that worked. Growth genuinely did accelerate: 2.3% in the first quarter of 2025, 2.8% in the second, 5.0% in the third, 5.4% in the fourth. The United States business — the largest and the one that had been the problem — went from 2.3% in the second quarter to 5.6% in the third and 5.7% in the fourth. Management had promised a second-half acceleration in unusually specific language ("we are extremely confident on the second half acceleration," August 2025) and it arrived.

**Act three (2026) — the self-inflicted reset.** In February 2026, having just posted the best two quarters in three years, management guided 2026 organic growth down to **1% to 3%**. The reason was not the market. Management's own read of the joint-replacement market, repeated on the April 2026 call, is that it is "4%, 4.5% durable." The reason was a decision: to rebuild the United States sales channel, converting roughly two-thirds of a 2,500-person American sales force from independent contractors into dedicated employees, and to specialise them by product line. It is a two-year project, targeted for completion by the end of 2027.

So the arc is: we have the products, then the products are working, then we are going to slow ourselves down for two years to fix how we sell them. Layered on top, the innovation story has migrated from implants to robotics — the Monogram acquisition (completed 7 October 2025) brings the mBôs semi- and fully-autonomous robot, with a semi-autonomous United States launch targeted for early 2027 and the fully autonomous version for late 2027 or early 2028.

**What was quietly dropped.** The 2025 talk of a "5% weighted-average market growth rate environment by the end of 2027" has faded from the script. Persona IQ, the connected "smart implant" that once got heavy airtime, was addressed in April 2026 only when an analyst raised it, and the chief executive conceded the point directly: "early on, probably, we talked too much about Persona IQ without having the data."

## 3. Tone and confidence trajectory

**Rating: Stable in volume, declining in precision.**

Mr Tornos's register is consistently promotional and does not vary with results. A partial sample: "transformational," "the boldest, most customer-centric company in med tech," "this type of technology will change the world of orthopedics," "like nothing that I've seen in my many years dealing with technology," "a lot of shots on gold," "totally different company." He opens every call with the same gratitude passage. This is a chief executive whose tone carries no information — it is identical in the quarter he beat and the quarter he cut.

What does move is specificity, and it moves in a helpful direction on operations and an unhelpful one on outcomes.

- **May 2025:** confident and quantified on inventory ("from around 370 days today to a much lower number").
- **August 2025:** peak offensive language — "extremely confident on the second half acceleration," "the strongest pipeline that we have had in the 6.5 years that I've been here."
- **November 2025:** the first defensive turn. Problems are disclosed but arrive late and unforecast: "late in the quarter, unexpected weakness in Eastern Europe, Latin America and noncore segments." On the restorative-therapies shortfall he was unusually blunt — "we didn't budget adequately. So that's a mistake we're not going to repeat."
- **February 2026:** the language shifts from growth to transformation and to a longer clock — "in 2028 and beyond, Zimmer Biomet will look and act like a totally different company." Asking investors to look three years out is a tell.
- **April 2026:** heavy operational specificity (contractor share down from 66% to below 60%, specialised reps from 25% to 30%, turnover in single digits against a 12% tolerance) paired with a refusal to raise revenue guidance after beating. "I give you one word: confidence" — repeated as a closing device.

The offensive-to-defensive shift is real but partial: the company is spending on growth, not cutting, so this is not a retrenchment. It is a company that has moved from promising a number to promising a process.

## 4. Guidance credibility

**Reliability: Medium.**

*Where they delivered.* The second-half 2025 acceleration was promised in specific terms in August 2025 and arrived. Earnings per share guidance was raised twice in 2025 ($7.90–8.10 to $8.10–8.30) and again in April 2026 ($8.30–8.45 to $8.40–8.55), and the screener records seven consecutive quarters of consensus beats. Free cash flow guidance was raised. The Paragon 28 dilution promise — under 3% in year one, neutral by the end of year two — has not been contradicted.

*Where the record is softer.* Revenue guidance was cut twice inside 2025 and the outcome was then described as the middle of the original range. The first-quarter 2026 beat was substantially a tariff refund. And there is a visible sandbagging habit that management does not deny: asked directly in April 2026 why they did not raise revenue after a first quarter at the top of the range, the answer was "it's probably prudent to wait, let's call it, 90 days." The same happened on pricing — first-quarter erosion of 40 basis points, full-year assumption left at up to 100.

The pattern is a management team that guides earnings conservatively and beats, while guiding revenue optimistically and trimming. For a business whose problem is revenue growth, that is the wrong way round.

## 5. Analyst pressure points

Four questions come back call after call, and the answers have been getting thinner on the first two.

1. **Will the sales-force rebuild cost more than a year of growth?** Jeff Johnson of Baird pressed hardest in April 2026: reps have been given two- and in some cases three-year income guarantees, so what happens in year three when the guarantee lapses? The reply was about career fulfilment — "money is not going to keep you there... having the future that they feel they have at Zimmer Biomet is keeping them here" — not about retention economics. The costs are being run through adjusted earnings rather than excluded, which is honest, but the year-three cliff was not addressed.
2. **Where is knee growth?** United States knees grew 2.2% in the first quarter of 2026 against a market management itself puts above 4%. The explanation given — two large accounts lost, a Kaiser strike, legacy-brand phase-out — is a list, and the chief executive conceded "we got to do better."
3. **How much of any given quarter is one-off?** Robert Marcus of JP Morgan asked the question directly in April 2026 (end-of-quarter buying, gross margin, discontinuations). The answer was candid on the tariff piece and vaguer on the rest.
4. **The Monogram timetable.** Analysts keep probing the path to a fully autonomous robot. Management has held the same dates for a year — semi-autonomous in early 2027, fully autonomous in late 2027 or early 2028 — which is a point in their favour. But the clinical study enrolment completed in the first quarter of 2026 was in India, and the United States regulatory submission is described as being "deeply focused on the preparation of the 510(k) submission," i.e. not yet filed.

The topic analysts are *not* pressing that they should: the return on the acquisition programme. Three deals in one year, a balance sheet two-thirds intangible, and a 5% return on invested capital have not been meaningfully challenged on any of these five calls.

## 6. Red flags and green flags

🔴 **The chief financial officer left in the middle of the transformation.** Suketu Upadhyay's departure after seven years was announced on the April 2026 call itself; the controller, Paul Stellato, is interim, and no permanent successor has been named as of today. Losing your finance chief in year one of a two-year commercial rebuild, with net debt at 3.19 times EBITDA, is the single most concerning item in this file.

🔴 **Guiding to a third of your own market growth rate.** Management says the market grows 4–4.5%; guidance is 1–3% organic. Even accepting the transition explanation entirely, that is two years of ceded share.

🔴 **Problems surface late and unbudgeted.** November 2025's "unexpected weakness... late in the quarter" cost about 120 basis points of growth; the restorative-therapies miss was admitted as a budgeting failure. This is a forecasting-control weakness, not a market event.

🔴 **No insider conviction.** Zero open-market purchases against three sales totalling 7,650 shares. In a stock management describes as materially undervalued relative to its future, nobody is buying.

🔴 **Promotional vocabulary that outruns the numbers.** "Magnificent seven," "transformational," "change the standard of care." The products are real; the adjectives are doing more work than the growth rate supports.

🟢 **The Q1 2026 tariff disclosure.** Management volunteered that $0.20 of a $0.23 beat was a tariff refund and that half of it was pulled forward from the second half. Very few management teams itemise the low quality of their own beat.

🟢 **The transition is being measured in public.** Contractor share of the American sales force down from 66% to below 60% in one quarter; specialised reps from 25% to above 30%; the top six independent distributors — about 40% of sales — signed to seven-year extensions; turnover in single digits against a 12% tolerance. These are falsifiable numbers, disclosed voluntarily.

🟢 **Free cash flow and the buyback.** Free cash flow has grown four consecutive years; on 12 May 2026 the company raised its 2026 repurchase expectation to **up to $1 billion**, a $250 million increase, which is roughly 5% of the shares outstanding.

🟢 **Genuinely differentiated products exist.** The Oxford partial cementless knee is the only device of its kind approved in the United States. The iodine-treated hip, launched in Japan, carries breakthrough designation and — a rare, checkable, priced fact — **a 40% price uplift versus the non-iodine implant** (February 2026 call).

## 7. Hidden signals

- **"Optionality" is doing a lot of work.** Asked repeatedly whether ROSA, TMini and mBôs compete with each other, management's answer is always "optionality" and "not all customers are created equal." Three robotic platforms plus a partnership with Think Surgical is a portfolio, but it can also be a hedge against not knowing which one wins. Note the tell in August 2025: asked about a competitor claiming it could already run fully autonomously, the reply was "if they can turn it on, I will recommend that at some point, they do" — a competitive jab, not a differentiation argument.
- **Capital allocation has flipped.** Through 2025: three acquisitions, $1.39 billion out the door, $1.0 billion of new debt. From February 2026: "we plan to prioritise meaningful return of capital to shareholders over M&A," followed in April by "we're going to pause." The pause is welcome, but it coincides with leverage at 3.19 times — it reads as a balance-sheet constraint being presented as a choice. The exception, the $140 million Pacira iovera purchase announced 1 July 2026, is small enough not to contradict it.
- **The dividend has not moved in years** — roughly $190–200 million annually since 2020, about a 1% yield. All the incremental capital return is going to buybacks. That is rational, and it also means the shareholder base is not a yield base.
- **New voices, consistent message.** Kevin Thornal (Group President, 2025), Greg Siller (ASCs), Chintan Desai (Asia Pacific, named 14 July 2026), Dr Jonathan — a surgeon from the Hospital for Special Surgery — as chief science and technology officer (April 2026). The hiring is real and squarely aimed at the two stated weaknesses, commercial execution and technology credibility.
- **A quiet capital-spending signal.** Capital expenditure has fallen to about 1.9% of revenue trailing, from 2.9% in 2025, while the company simultaneously claims to be expanding into ambulatory surgery centres and placing more robots. Both can be true — robots sold as capital equipment do not consume the company's own capital — but a maker of implants that under-invests in instrument sets while chasing new accounts is worth watching.

## 8. Capital-allocation verdict (for the director)

**Trajectory: PIVOTING.**

This is not a deteriorating business and it is not a strengthening one. It is a company deliberately trading two years of growth for a commercial model it believes will support durable mid-single-digit growth afterwards, while simultaneously pivoting its innovation story from implants to autonomous robotics. Both bets are legible, dated and being measured in public. Neither has paid yet.

**The single most important thing to weigh before sizing:** *the two-year window is entirely self-imposed and entirely unhedged.* Management has voluntarily guided to 1–3% growth against a 4–4.5% market until the end of 2027, with a departed chief financial officer, an interim replacement, net debt at 3.19 times EBITDA, and the payoff — mBôs — not yet submitted to the United States regulator. If the transition slips or the reps walk when their income guarantees lapse in 2028, there is no second engine. The buyback is real support, but a buyback is a bid, not a floor.

**Economic moat: NARROW. Moat trend: ERODING.**

The moat type is switching costs reinforced by scale: surgeons train on a specific implant system and its instrument trays, hospitals contract in multi-year bundles, and the installed base of ROSA robots (approaching or exceeding 2,000 units) pulls implant volume behind it. That is a real barrier — it is why the company can lose share slowly rather than quickly.

But it must be judged on the returns evidence, and the evidence says narrowing. Return on invested capital is 5.3% and has not recovered; return on equity is 6.0%; gross margin is guided down modestly to around 71%; pricing runs negative every year; and organic growth of 1–3% against a 4–4.5% market is arithmetic share loss. A widening moat shows rising returns and expanding margins. This shows neither. The one genuine widening signal is the iodine implant's 40% price premium in Japan — proof that differentiated products can still command price in this industry — but it is currently one product in one market.

**Secular threat: MATERIAL, not terminal.** The structural force is reimbursement compression — bundled-payment programmes, the migration of joint replacement into lower-priced ambulatory surgery centres, and steady annual price erosion of up to 1%. That grinds down value; it does not extinguish it, because the demographic driver is unambiguous and management's own top-account waiting lists remain long. The live competitive threat is the more immediate one: rivals' robotic platforms locking in implant choice at the point of purchase, which is precisely why the company is spending on three robot platforms at once.

**Fear-print or value trap?** Neither, cleanly — which is itself the finding. The base is not shrinking (revenue grows, cash flow grows, the end market grows), so this is not a value trap in the terminal sense. But the discount is not a fear print either: the shares have already rallied roughly 20% from their April 2026 level near $80 and now sit at $96.28, close to the middle of a defensible range. The cheapness is a *return* problem — a business earning 5% on capital deserves a low multiple — and it will only re-rate if the sales-force rebuild converts into growth at or above market. That evidence does not exist yet.
