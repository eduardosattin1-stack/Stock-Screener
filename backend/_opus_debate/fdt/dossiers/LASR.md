# Forensic Dossier — nLIGHT, Inc. (LASR)

**Prepared 2026-08-11 · price used $50.07 (Nasdaq, US dollars) · market value about $2.82 billion**

Five consecutive earnings calls reviewed (first quarter 2025 through second quarter 2026), cross-referenced
against the screener's financial block, the company's filed quarterly statements, and dated news.

---

## 1. NARRATIVE ARC

The story has changed shape twice in six quarters, and both changes were real rather than cosmetic.

**Act one (first quarter 2025): a struggling industrial laser company discovers it has a defense business.**
Chief executive Scott Keeney opened the May 2025 call by noting defense was "more than 63% of total sales in
the quarter, up from 49% in the same quarter a year ago." Everything else was apology: industrial and
microfabrication "remain challenging," Shanghai manufacturing had just been closed, and a long tariff
digression consumed a quarter of the prepared remarks. The single named program was HELSI-2 — a $171 million
Department of Defense contract to build a one-megawatt laser, then described as a "HEL-TD program" (the label
changed to HELSI-2 by August 2025, an early sign of loose naming discipline).

**Act two (August 2025 through February 2026): the defense guide is raised twice and the commercial business
is amputated.** Full-year aerospace and defense growth guidance went from "at least 25%" (May 2025) to "at
least 40%" (August 2025) and landed at +60% for the full year. In the fourth quarter of 2025 the company
exited cutting and welding entirely — the legacy industrial laser business it was founded on — and raised
$190 million of fresh equity to build a 50,000 square-foot plant in Longmont, Colorado. That is a genuine
identity change, not a pivot in emphasis: management chose to shrink the base business to fund the new one.

**Act three (2026): from component supplier to system-level prime.** The May 2026 call launched the HADES
product family and, for the first time, quantified the addressable budget — "nearly $400 million in each of
the 2027 and 2028 budgeted for Directed Energy prototypes," rising to "approximately $1 billion" annually
including science-and-technology funded high-power prototypes. In July 2026 the company was selected for the
Joint Laser Weapon System agreement. By the August 2026 call the framing was explicit: "we are increasingly
engaged not only as a laser supplier, but also as a system-level partner."

**What was quietly dropped.** Metal additive manufacturing was the designated commercial growth story in 2025
and was then folded into "advanced manufacturing" and de-emphasized — until the August 2026 call brought it
back as an upside surprise. Tariffs, which dominated the May 2025 call, vanished from the narrative entirely
and were replaced eighteen months later by a different and more dangerous version of the same risk: Chinese
export scrutiny on optics. Management spent two years de-risking China on the *revenue* side and,
by its own August 2026 admission, did not finish the job on the *supply* side.

**The single most important divergence.** Every call is anchored on "adjusted" profit. In the June 2026
quarter, adjusted net income was $9.6 million and reported net income was a $1.3 million loss — an $11 million
gap, essentially all of it stock compensation. Over the trailing year the company paid its people roughly
$54 million in shares against roughly $40 million of adjusted operating profit. That is not an accounting
quibble: it means the entire operating margin, and then some, currently accrues to employees rather than to
shareholders. Share count has gone from 47.9 million (fourth quarter 2024) to 57.0 million (second quarter
2026), a 19% increase in six quarters, of which the February 2026 equity raise explains roughly three-quarters
and stock compensation the rest.

**A fair offset.** The balance sheet is genuinely strong and unencumbered: $295.8 million of cash against
$14.8 million of total debt (all of it capitalised leases) at 30 June 2026, and the company repaid the
$20 million it had drawn on its credit line during the quarter. Management reported $330.8 million including
restricted cash and investments. There is no maturity wall and no financing pressure. On the serial-diluter
test — the standard failure mode in this sector — nLIGHT does **not** fit the pattern: it raised once, at
roughly $70 per share into strength, for a named plant with a signed lease, and it has never paid a dividend
or bought back stock at a peak.

## 3. TONE & CONFIDENCE TRAJECTORY

**Rising, steeply, then abruptly hedged — the inflection is 6 August 2026.**

- *First quarter 2025:* defensive and qualified. "Demand is expected to remain weak through the remainder of
  the year." A long unprompted tariff section. Chief financial officer Joe Corso: tariffs "caused us to think
  to widen our margin range a little bit."
- *Second quarter 2025:* the language turns offensive. "Strong execution across the board." The defense guide
  is raised from 25% to 40% mid-year, which is unusual and self-confident.
- *Third quarter 2025:* specificity increases — a named "$50 million contract for an existing long-running
  missile program." Naming contract values is a credibility-positive behaviour.
- *Fourth quarter 2025:* the most confident call in the set. "2025 was an exceptional year." Notably, when
  analyst Keith Housum asked what worried him, Keeney gave a real answer rather than a deflection: "arrogance
  is what kills companies… lasers are hard, right?" That is a good sign in a sector full of promoters.
- *First quarter 2026:* peak confidence — "exceptional quarter," record 43.6% products gross margin, budget
  figures quantified for the first time. But note the one honest hedge, on the timing of the prototype awards:
  "I'd like to predict how Congress will work this year, but I've got enough experience to know that there are
  error bars around that."
- *Second quarter 2026:* the tone splits. The prepared remarks are the most bullish yet ("extremely
  encouraged"), and then the guidance section delivers a $17 million revenue removal, a widened guidance range,
  and a gross margin guide of 24-30% against 33.1% delivered two quarters earlier.

The important tonal detail: management did **not** bury the bad news. Corso quantified the shortfall
explicitly and volunteered that fourth-quarter execution "is still a little bit of a question mark for us at
this point." Under questioning he conceded the defense business is not fully insulated — "we do use some of
our own commercial items, which do have exposure to some of these Chinese components that go into our defense
products." That is more candour than the sector norm, and it is why the credibility score below is not lower.

## 4. GUIDANCE CREDIBILITY — **High**

This is the strongest part of the file. Across the five calls the pattern is consistent under-promise and
over-deliver, corroborated by the screener's 86% earnings-beat rate (6 of 7 quarters):

- May 2025: aerospace and defense to grow "at least 25%." Delivered +60%.
- August 2025: guide raised to "at least 40%" with two quarters still to run. Delivered.
- November 2025: "we expect the second half to be better than the first." It was, decisively — the December
  quarter was $81.2 million against $51.7 million in March.
- February 2026: quarterly non-GAAP operating expense guided to a $17-19 million range. Actuals: $17.1 million
  and $19.5 million. In range.
- August 2026: the June quarter came in "at or above" every guided line.

There is mild sandbagging — the pattern of guiding to a number and then raising it mid-year is deliberate —
but sandbagging in this direction is a credibility asset, not a liability. Reliability: **High.**

The one caveat is that the company does not guide beyond one quarter and explicitly refuses annual commercial
guidance ("we don't guide for a full year basis because we don't have the amount of visibility"). So the
delivery record is a record on short-dated promises only.

## 5. ANALYST PRESSURE POINTS

**The backlog question — asked repeatedly, never answered, across all five calls.** This is the material one.

In May 2025 Ruben Roy of Stifel asked directly for funded and unfunded backlog progression. Corso: "we're not
in the position, you know, today where we're gonna provide that update quarter over quarter… we still have the
right level of, you know, funded backlog plus the unfunded portion." In August 2026, fifteen months later, the
entire disclosure is three words: "Backlog is strong."

nLIGHT has never published a backlog figure, a book-to-bill ratio, or a funded-versus-unfunded split in any of
these five calls. For a company whose whole thesis is multi-year defense order visibility, and which is
building a plant ahead of demand on the strength of that visibility, this is the single largest evidentiary
hole in the file. Undated, unquantified backlog language carries no weight.

**The headline-versus-funded gap.** The July 2026 announcement led with "$627 Million Joint Laser Weapon
System Contract." The initial funded award is **$44 million** — 7% of the headline. This is a legitimate
structure for an Other Transaction Authority agreement, and management answered the arithmetic honestly when
analyst Jan-Frans Engelbrecht of Baird probed the ceiling split ("Your math is right on that, Jan"). But the
choice to put the ceiling in the headline rather than the funded value is the classic capacity-inflation tell,
and readers should size on the $44 million.

**Prototype timing.** Greg Palm of Craig-Hallum pushed twice on when the prototype awards land. The answer in
May 2026 was that the quantified budgets are for fiscal 2027, not the current year, and depend on the
appropriations process. That is a soft, third-party-controlled timeline.

**What analysts pushed on that later turned into bad news.** In May 2025 Ruben Roy pressed on tariff
pass-through and margin exposure; management dismissed near-term impact. Eighteen months later the same
underlying exposure — a Chinese-dominated supply chain — removed $17 million of revenue and roughly 500 basis
points of guided gross margin in a single quarter. The analyst was directionally right and early.

**What nobody is asking.** No analyst on any of these five calls asked about stock-based compensation running
at 17% of revenue, or about the gap between adjusted and reported profit. The entire sell-side conversation is
conducted in adjusted terms.

## 6. RED FLAGS / GREEN FLAGS

🔴 **Backlog has never been quantified** in five consecutive calls despite direct, repeated questioning
(May 2025 through August 2026). A defense growth story without a disclosed order book is unverifiable.

🔴 **Stock compensation of roughly 17% of revenue** exceeds adjusted operating profit. Every "record adjusted
EBITDA" headline in these calls rests on adding that number back.

🔴 **Reported results are still loss-making.** GAAP net loss of $1.3 million in the June 2026 quarter, negative
return on equity and invested capital on a trailing basis. The three-year revenue growth rate is +2.6%.

🔴 **Insider selling is one-directional.** Zero open-market purchases against 75 sales totalling 717,360
shares. The chief executive sold 24,990 shares on 3-4 June 2026 at roughly $75.81 for about $1.89 million
(reported 6 June 2026) — nine weeks before the stock lost a third of its value.

🔴 **The China supply exposure was known and not closed.** Management spent 2024-2025 telling investors it had
de-risked China ("we no longer have any manufacturing operations in China," August 2025) while leaving the
optics *supply* chain in place. Corso, August 2026: "it doesn't take more than just a couple of components for
us to complete the build." A single choke point removed $17 million of shipments.

🔴 **The gross margin direction turned in the June 2026 quarter** — total margin 31.1% against 33.1% in March,
products margin 41.2% against 43.6% — and is guided down again to 24-30% for September.

🟢 **Guidance delivery is genuinely excellent** — two mid-year raises delivered, 86% beat rate, operating
expense guidance hit inside its range twice.

🟢 **Gross margin expansion over twelve months is large and real** — products margin from 28.8% to 41.2% year
over year, driven by mix and factory absorption rather than price gimmicks.

🟢 **Capital discipline through the last downturn was decisive.** Revenue fell 26% from 2021 to 2024 and
management responded by closing Shanghai, exiting cutting and welding, and taking a restructuring charge —
rather than by defending unprofitable volume.

🟢 **Financing was done from strength, in the right direction.** Equity issued at roughly $70 near the highs
for a named plant with a signed lease; no buybacks at the top; no dividend; credit line repaid.

🟢 **Management answers hard questions directly.** The candour on defense supply exposure and on the contract
ceiling arithmetic in the August 2026 call is above sector norm.

## 7. HIDDEN SIGNALS

**The self-description migrated up the value chain, deliberately.** 2025: "one of the most comprehensive
suppliers to the US government, other prime contractors and foreign allies." 2026: "increasingly engaged not
only as a laser supplier, but also as a system-level partner." A component supplier that becomes a system
integrator takes on integration risk and schedule risk it did not previously carry, in exchange for a larger
share of the contract. Nothing in the disclosure lets an outsider judge how that trade is going.

**"Department of Defense" became "Department of War" in the February 2026 call and stayed.** Cosmetic, but it
dates the transcripts and confirms which procurement environment they are operating in.

**The demonstration-versus-delivery distinction, volunteered.** On the megawatt laser, Keeney corrected an
analyst in May 2026: "it's not a delivery of a product. It's a demonstration of that technology." That is an
honest and important clarification — the flagship program produces a technology milestone, not a production
order.

**Capacity language reversed within two quarters.** February 2026: the Longmont plant is needed because "there
are no production lines to build multiple copies of beam combined lasers simultaneously," and equity was
raised for it. May 2026, asked what stops revenue breaking $80 million: "we are not capacity constrained
today… What we need… is demand signals from our customers." Building ahead of demand is a defensible strategy
in a programme-driven market, but the two statements sit awkwardly together and the fixed-cost base they
create is what makes the margin so volume-sensitive — which is precisely the mechanism now compressing the
September quarter.

**The chain classification is a poor fit and should not be leaned on.** The input bundle files this name under
the quantum value chain at medium confidence. Two-thirds of revenue is aerospace and defense — directed-energy
weapons and missile sensing. Quantum computing is not a revenue driver, is never mentioned in any of the five
calls, and any quantum-adjacent read-across here is a labelling artefact rather than a fact about the business.

## 8. CAPITAL-ALLOCATION VERDICT

**Classification: STRENGTHENING.** The operating trajectory is unambiguously improving — revenue up 34% year
over year, products gross margin up more than twelve percentage points year over year, adjusted profitability
achieved, a $627 million-ceiling flagship award won, a fortress balance sheet, and a management team with a
verifiable record of delivering what it guides.

**The single most important thing to weigh before sizing.** The business is improving, but nothing in this
file establishes that the *stock* is cheap, and the file contains no way to verify the order book that the
whole valuation rests on. At $50.07 the company is valued at roughly 8.2 times trailing revenue — against a
peer group of true photonics competitors trading at a median near 5.3 times — for a business that has never
earned a reported profit and whose entire adjusted operating margin is currently paid out in shares. The
director is not being asked to judge whether nLIGHT is a good company. It is. The director is being asked
whether a good company with an unquantified backlog, a live and unresolved single-source supply dependency on
a hostile jurisdiction, and a one-directional insider record is worth eight times sales after a 42% fall from
its high. Size to the possibility that the answer is no.

**Economic moat: NARROW, and honestly earned.** The moat type is **intangible technical capability plus
scale-cost from vertical integration** — twenty-five years of coherent beam combining, adaptive optics for
atmospheric correction, and ownership of the full stack from semiconductor laser diodes through fibre
amplifiers to the finished weapon module, all manufactured in the United States. The United States mandate to
build these systems domestically is a real regulatory shelter. The evidence supports width being genuine but
not wide: products gross margin rising from 28.8% to 41.2% year over year while revenue grew 34% is pricing
power, not volume bought with discounts. But three things cap it. Returns on invested capital are still
negative. The margin is explicitly volume-dependent — management says so plainly, and the September guide
proves it, falling to 24-30% purely on lower absorption. And a moat that a foreign export-licence decision can
puncture by withholding commodity optics is narrower than the technology alone suggests.

**Moat trend: STABLE.** Twelve-month margin expansion and the Joint Laser Weapon System award argue widening;
the sequential margin reversal and the demonstrated supply fragility argue the opposite. Net: stable.

**Secular threat: manageable.** No structural force impairs terminal value here. Directed energy is a
multi-decade growth category with rising, publicly documented budget lines, and low-cost-per-shot counter-drone
defence is a durable requirement rather than a fashion. The genuine long-term risks are competitive (larger
primes internalising the laser source) and geopolitical (supply denial), not secular decline. This is not a
melting-ice-cube; the risk is entirely about what one pays.
