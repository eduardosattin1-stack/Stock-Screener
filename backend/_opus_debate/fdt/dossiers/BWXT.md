# Forensic dossier — BWX Technologies (BWXT)

Prepared 2026-08-11. Sources: five consecutive quarterly earnings calls in the supplied
transcript file (May 2025 through May 2026), the second-quarter 2026 call held
2026-08-03 (retrieved separately, because it post-dates the supplied file and is the most
important document in this review), the screener's financial block, and company financial
statements through the quarter ended 2026-06-30.

Price used throughout: $169.68 per share, US dollars, New York Stock Exchange listing
"BWXT", as of 2026-08-11. Market value of the equity is about $15.5 billion.

---

## 1. Narrative arc

Over the six quarters on record the story has moved in one direction, and the direction is
outward. In May 2025 management described what it called an "all-weather portfolio" —
the pitch was defensive. The company was a sole-source supplier of nuclear reactors,
components and fuel to the United States naval propulsion programme, with a Canadian
commercial business attached, and the argument was that long-cycle government contracts
would carry it through whatever the economy did. The word used was "weather any storm."

By the August 2026 call the framing had changed completely. Management now describes
"the early stages of a multi-decade super cycle of growth" and calls itself a "super
merchant supplier" of critical nuclear components. The defensive language is gone. In its
place is a capacity-expansion story: a Canadian plant expansion at Cambridge, Ontario that
adds roughly half again as much capacity, a United States acquisition (Precision Components
Group, closed 2026-07-01) that management says roughly doubled its commercial
manufacturing footprint, and a decision pending on a new greenfield heavy-component plant
with deepwater port access.

Three growth drivers were introduced along the way and all three are now material:

- **Special materials.** Called "one of our most underappreciated businesses" in May 2025.
  By November 2025 it had produced two named awards: a $1.5 billion contract to establish a
  domestic uranium enrichment capability for defence purposes, and a $1.6 billion ten-year
  contract to supply high-purity depleted uranium. Both are now under construction.
- **Commercial nuclear power.** Backlog in this segment went from $1.3 billion in the first
  quarter of 2025 to $1.7 billion at the end of 2025, and organic revenue growth in the
  segment ran 31%, 39% and 33% in the last three reported quarters.
- **Technical services.** Management and operations contracts at fourteen United States
  Department of Energy and National Nuclear Security Administration sites, plus the Canadian
  Nuclear Laboratories contract (about 1.2 billion Canadian dollars a year).

Two things were quietly dropped or downgraded. The DRACO nuclear-thermal-propulsion
programme, discussed with enthusiasm in early 2025, "evolved into single agency support"
and management admitted in November 2025 that "the funding hasn't really shaped up for
that in a meaningful way yet" — microreactor volumes have been a drag on government
revenue in every quarter since. And the Tech-99 medical product, promised as a near-term
launch through 2025, slipped repeatedly ("we have not submitted to the FDA yet", February
2026) and was then removed from the company entirely: on 2026-08-03 BWXT agreed to sell its
medical business to Nordic Capital for $750 million in consideration, with shared economics
taking the total to as much as $800 million.

## 2. Claims versus financials

| Management claim | The numbers | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue growing at "high teens" | Revenue grew 18% in 2025 to $3.20 billion; the June 2026 quarter grew 18% to $902 million, of which 9 percentage points was organic | Matches. Roughly half of recent growth is acquired, and management says so |
| "Excellent execution", "operational excellence" | Adjusted earnings per share have grown every quarter; the June 2026 quarter beat the street's $1.01 with $1.07 | Matches |
| Margins improving | Government segment adjusted operating margin rose from 19.2% (September 2025 quarter) to 20.9% (June 2026 quarter), and full-year guidance was raised to about 20.5% from "greater than 19%" | Matches, at the segment level |
| Implied: the whole company is getting more profitable | Consolidated gross margin has gone the other way: 24.7% in 2022, 24.9% in 2023, 24.2% in 2024, 22.9% in 2025, and 22.4% in the June 2026 quarter against 25.1% a year earlier | **Diverges.** See below |
| Commercial segment margin | Guided to about 14% at the start of 2026, cut to about 13% on 2026-08-03. Actual June-quarter margin 11.9% | **Diverges.** Second consecutive downward revision |
| Free cash flow strength | 2025 free cash flow $295 million, up 16%; 2026 guidance raised by $30 million to $345–360 million | Matches, and the raise is credible |
| Backlog momentum | $8.4 billion at 2026-06-30, up 40% year on year — but **down** from $8.7 billion three months earlier | Mostly matches, with a first crack |

The gross-margin divergence is the single most important forensic finding in this file, and
it deserves care because the naive reading is wrong. Consolidated gross margin is
compressing on rising revenue, which in most industrial businesses means the company is
buying volume by cutting price. That is not what is happening here. Three things explain it:

1. **Acquisition mix.** Kinectrics is a services business and Precision Components Group
   earns, in the chief financial officer's own words, "low double-digit" operating margins
   today. Both dilute the consolidated gross line while adding profit dollars.
2. **Cost-plus revenue accounting.** Management cut its government revenue growth guidance
   from low teens to high single digits on 2026-08-03 and explained that "improved cost
   performance under our accounting rules results in lower reported revenue, but an overall
   favourable economic outcome." That is a real accounting effect, not spin — and it is
   why the same guidance revision *raised* the government margin guide.
3. **Deliberate investment.** "These investments will moderate near-term margin expansion,
   but they are essential to establishing the industrial scale required to lead this market."

So the honest read is: the segment that generates three-quarters of the profit is expanding
its margin, and the consolidated gross line is compressing for reasons of mix and
accounting. That is not commoditisation. But it does mean an investor cannot look at the
top-line gross margin and conclude the business is improving — and it means the promised
commercial margin recovery in 2027 is now the load-bearing assumption in the story, having
already been pushed once.

## 3. Tone and confidence trajectory

**Rising, and specifically so.** The evidence is not in adjectives but in what management
is willing to commit capital to.

- May 2025: "BWXT is positioned to weather any storm." Defensive.
- August 2025: "Demand across the global security, clean energy and medical end markets is
  accelerating." Backlog $6.0 billion, book-to-bill 2.2 times.
- November 2025: "unprecedented demand"; two contracts worth $3.1 billion combined booked
  in a single quarter; backlog $7.4 billion, up 119% year on year.
- February 2026: "a record 2025"; the balance sheet was refinanced with a $1.25 billion
  convertible bond at a zero-percent coupon.
- May 2026: capital expenditure warning that spending "may exceed" 6% of sales — the first
  time growth investment was flagged as open-ended.
- August 2026: "the early stages of a multi-decade super cycle"; "I believe this is just the
  beginning."

Language has shifted from defensive to offensive and has become *more* specific, not less,
which is the pattern one wants. Management names sites (Jonesborough and Oak Ridge in
Tennessee; Mount Vernon, Indiana; two Precision Components sites in New York/Pennsylvania
and Florence, New Jersey), names counterparties (GE Vernova, Westinghouse, Rolls-Royce,
TerraPower, Kairos, Applied Atomics, Core Power, Nordic Capital), and puts dollar figures
on contracts. Evasive management does the opposite.

The one place the tone gets vaguer is exactly where an investor would want it sharpest: the
timing of commercial new-build orders. "There's a credible opportunity to secure at least
one new build nuclear equipment order before the year-end" (August 2026) is a hope, not a
schedule, and the chief executive said as much when pressed — utilities "are sort of rightly
waiting to see how those deals come out before they step into it."

## 4. Guidance credibility

**High.** This is the strongest section of the file.

Track the promises forward. In May 2025 management guided to modest naval growth,
double-digit organic commercial power growth, and a 3–5% ten-year revenue growth rate in
naval propulsion. In February 2026 the company reported 2025 revenue up 18%, adjusted
operating profit up 15%, earnings per share up 20% and free cash flow up 16% — "all
exceeding the initial guidance we provided at the start of the year." In August 2026 they
raised full-year adjusted operating profit guidance by $10 million at the midpoint, raised
free cash flow guidance by $30 million, and raised earnings-per-share guidance to
$4.70–$4.80, with the increase "driven entirely by stronger operating earnings" rather than
tax, buybacks or one-time items. That last qualification is the mark of a company that
expects to be checked.

The screener records seven consecutive quarterly earnings beats; the June 2026 quarter makes
eight. There is a mild sandbagging pattern — the chief financial officer flagged in August
2025 that work was "hitting earlier in the year than we typically do" and warned the second
half would normalise, which it did — but it is the honest kind, where management tells you
the shape of the year rather than letting the market discover it.

Two guidance items were genuinely cut, and both should be recorded: government revenue
growth (low teens to high single digits, for the accounting reason above, with margin raised
in compensation) and commercial segment margin (about 14% to about 13%, for real investment
reasons, and for the second time). Neither is a credibility failure. The commercial margin
cut is the one to watch, because a third cut would change this assessment.

**Reliability: High.**

## 5. Analyst pressure points

Three questions come back every quarter, and management's answers have not improved.

**When does the commercial order actually land?** Asked in some form on every call. In
November 2025 an analyst asked directly whether 2026 was "a year of RFPs, wins" with no
revenue contribution; the chief executive answered "yes, that's correct." In August 2026,
asked why Westinghouse still had no firm United States AP1000 order despite a $17.5 billion
Department of Energy loan commitment, the answer was a structural explanation about
special-purpose vehicles and government ownership — clear-eyed, and also an admission that
the order the whole commercial thesis rests on is controlled by third parties and has not
been placed.

**Government segment margin.** Pressed in August 2025 on a second-half margin run rate that
"might be below 20% — you haven't done that in quite some time." The answer was timing, and
it turned out to be right. Pressed again in August 2026 on the path into 2027, the chief
financial officer said "I'm not setting '27 guidance at this point" and, notably, that "some
of the performance that we were expecting to see in '27 is starting to show up in '26."
That is a warning about the 2027 comparison that has not been widely absorbed.

**Tech-99 and the medical business.** Asked on four consecutive calls. The answers
degraded from "line of sight to address the final technical issues" (August 2025) to "I
have, frankly, imperfect clarity around that" (February 2026). The business was then sold.
Management's version is that an unsolicited approach produced a compelling offer; the
sequence also reads as an exit from a problem the company could not fix, and both readings
can be true.

The thing analysts are *not* pressing on, and should be: nobody asked why a company whose
shares have fallen roughly 30% from their April 2026 high, sitting on $750 million of
incoming divestiture proceeds, has explicitly guided to no share repurchases in 2026.

## 6. Red flags and green flags

🔴 **Consolidated gross margin down about 185 basis points over three years** while revenue
compounded at double digits. Explicable, but it means the reported blended profitability of
each dollar of sales is falling.

🔴 **Commercial segment margin guidance cut twice** (about 14% to about 13%, August 2026),
with the recovery pushed into 2027. This is now the assumption the valuation leans on.

🔴 **Backlog fell sequentially** for the first time in the record, from $8.7 billion at
2026-03-31 to $8.4 billion at 2026-06-30. Management's explanation — lumpy timing of large
multi-year awards — is fair and the trailing book-to-bill is still 1.7 times. But the streak
is broken.

🔴 **Capital spending is open-ended and rising.** 5.8% of sales in 2025, about 6% in 2026,
and management now says it "could approach 7% of sales in future years", on top of a
potential commercial fuel plant in Wyoming costing "a few hundred million dollars, up to
$500 million" and an undecided greenfield heavy-component plant. The decision gate on the
fuel plant is the Janus programme award, which the government has not made.

🔴 **No insider buying.** Zero open-market purchases against six sales totalling 13,761
shares, and no share repurchases planned for 2026, with the shares 30% off their high.

🔴 **A meaningful part of the pipeline is not contractual.** The Rolls-Royce arrangement is
a design contract plus a memorandum of understanding for manufacturing — a memorandum is
not an order. The Core Power arrangement is a feasibility study. The battleship programme
requires congressional authorisation and long-lead procurement would not begin until 2028.

🟢 **The naval franchise is contracted, named and dated.** A $2.1 billion pricing agreement
signed in late 2024, a further $2.6 billion agreement over eight years signed July 2025, and
a $1.4 billion second tranche booked in the March 2026 quarter. Government segment backlog
was roughly $7.0 billion against annual segment revenue near $2.5 billion.

🟢 **Capital structure is clean and the share count is flat.** Diluted shares went from 91.9
million (March 2025) to 92.0 million (June 2026) — no equity issuance, no serial dilution.
The $1.25 billion convertible bond issued in the December 2025 quarter carries a zero-percent
coupon, and a capped-call transaction pushed the effective conversion price above $396, so
there is no dilution anywhere near today's $169.68 share price. That is genuinely
opportunistic financing, and it is the opposite of the pattern that kills companies in this
sector.

🟢 **Acquisitions have worked.** Kinectrics "continues to exceed the acquisition business
case"; the depleted-uranium acquisition (A.O.T.) directly enabled the $1.6 billion National
Nuclear Security Administration contract. Management's stated discipline — "we certainly can
get assets within our multiple" — is corroborated by outcomes rather than asserted.

🟢 **The medical divestiture is a good price.** Roughly $130 million of 2026 revenue at a
margin modestly better than the commercial segment's 13% implies operating profit near $20
million. Selling that for $750 million, with up to $800 million available and a retained
minority stake, is an excellent multiple on a business the company could not scale.

🟢 **Input-cost exposure is genuinely managed.** Asked about a zirconium price spike (May
2025) and critical-mineral shortages (August 2025), management pointed to a contractual
pass-through mechanism with customers and said "our arrangements will have kind of pricing
locked in for roughly 70% over overall materials purchases." In June 2026 a five-year
materials agreement with ATI running through fiscal 2030 was announced. That is a real
answer, though it leaves roughly 30% of materials unhedged.

## 7. Hidden signals

**"Super merchant supplier" is doing a lot of work.** The phrase first appears in August
2025 and is now the company's self-description. It encodes a real strategic choice made in
2014, when BWXT stopped spending on its own mPower reactor design after about $400 million
with an estimated $600 million still to go, and decided to sell components to everyone
rather than compete with anyone. In June 2026 it licensed that shelved design exclusively
to Applied Atomics for land-based use, retaining the intellectual property, royalty rights
and exclusive manufacturing rights while the licensee funds the remaining design and
licensing work. That is a well-executed way to monetise a written-off asset, and it tells
you management thinks about capital rather than about being first.

**The Ford-class ordering cadence is the most underappreciated fact in the file.** In the
August 2026 call the chief executive walked through it at length: aircraft carriers have
been ordered on five-year centres, deliveries take eight years, and the arithmetic leaves
"a couple of gap years every decade" where one ship-set rather than two moves through the
plants. The Navy's May 2026 thirty-year plan moves to four-year centres, which "takes that
swale out." He then said flatly that this "has a bigger volume impact than even adding a
battleship." The benefit begins with long-lead procurement in 2028 — outside almost every
investor's horizon, which is precisely why it is not priced.

**Management is a net seller of its own stock and an aggressive buyer of assets.** Read
alongside "we've guided for '26 that we don't have any planned repurchases", the message is
that this team believes its capital earns more inside factories than in its own shares at
$169.68. That is a defensible industrial judgement and an unhelpful signal for a shareholder.

**Two chief financial officers in two years.** Robb LeMasters presented the May 2025 call;
Mike Fitzgerald, previously chief accounting officer, took over in August 2025. The new
voice is noticeably more precise about accounting mechanics — the cost-accounting
explanation of the revenue guidance cut is a good example — and noticeably less promotional.
That is an upgrade in disclosure quality.

## 8. Capital-allocation verdict

**Trajectory: STRENGTHENING.** Revenue compounding at high teens, backlog up 40% year on
year to $8.4 billion (about 2.2 times annual revenue), guidance raised on every line in
August 2026, free cash flow guidance raised twice, a clean share count, an opportunistically
financed balance sheet at 2.6 times net debt to operating profit, and a well-priced
divestiture of the one business that was not working. There is no forensic problem here.
This is not a promoter's company; it is an operator's company with a 75-year regulatory
franchise, and the sector's kill list — serial equity dilution, inflated capacity claims,
undated memoranda dressed as orders — does not describe it, though it partly describes some
of the pipeline it is quoting into.

**The single thing the director must weigh before sizing:** the price, not the business.
At $169.68 the shares carry roughly 36 times this year's guided earnings of $4.70–$4.80 and
about 25 times guided operating profit before depreciation, for a company whose largest
segment — three-quarters of profit — grows at the 3–5% rate management itself forecasts, and
whose fast-growing segment earns a 13% margin that has been guided down twice. Everything
above the naval base case depends on commercial new-build orders that, as of 2026-08-03,
have not been placed and are controlled by utilities and government special-purpose vehicles,
not by BWXT. The forensic record earns this company the benefit of the doubt on execution.
It does not earn it the benefit of the doubt on multiple.

**Economic moat: wide, of the regulatory-licence and intangible-qualification kind.** BWXT
is the sole-source supplier of nuclear reactors, components and fuel to the United States
naval propulsion programme, has delivered roughly 420 naval reactors over five decades,
operates the only surviving large nuclear component manufacturing plant in North America,
and holds the special-nuclear-material licences and qualifications that a new entrant would
need a decade and a government sponsor to replicate. The chief executive's explanation of
why is convincing: the naval programme was "a tremendous anchor tenant" that kept the
industrial capability alive while every competitor exited. That is a moat you cannot buy.

**Moat trend: stable, not widening.** The returns evidence does not support a widening
claim. Return on invested capital is roughly 7.6% on a trailing basis and about 11.5% on the
screener's measure — respectable, not exceptional, and falling as capital goes into
capacity ahead of the orders. Consolidated gross margin is down about 185 basis points over
three years. The offsetting evidence is real: government segment margin is expanding and the
commercial position is genuinely strengthening through the Cambridge expansion, the Precision
Components acquisition and the Kinectrics services layer. Net: stable.

**Secular threat: none.** There is no structural force eroding the terminal value of naval
nuclear propulsion or heavy nuclear component manufacturing. If anything the secular
direction is favourable — electricity demand, energy security and defence spending all pull
the same way. The risk here is cyclical and, more precisely, valuation-cyclical: the
enthusiasm that re-rated this sector is unwinding hard around BWXT (small modular reactor
and enrichment pure-plays are down 60% to 85% from their highs, against BWXT's roughly 30%),
and a share price can fall a long way without a single thing going wrong in the factories.
