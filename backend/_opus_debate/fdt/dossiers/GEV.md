# GE Vernova (GEV) — Forensic Dossier

**Prepared:** 2026-08-04 · **Price used:** $1,019.74 (NYSE, US dollars, live quote) · **Market value:** about $272 billion · **Diluted shares:** roughly 270 million

Transcripts reviewed: five consecutive calls — Q1 2025 (23 Apr 2025), Q2 2025 (23 Jul 2025), Q3 2025 plus the Prolec acquisition call (22 Oct 2025), Q4/full-year 2025 (28 Jan 2026), and Q1 2026 (22 Apr 2026). Financials and the Q2 2026 result (reported 22 Jul 2026) verified separately against filings and company releases.

## 1. Narrative arc

The story has not shifted — it has compounded. Across five calls the same three sentences keep getting bigger numbers attached to them, which is the rarest and most valuable pattern in this sector.

**April 2025 — the setup.** Scott Strazik opened with "the world is entering an era of accelerated electrification" and a $123 billion total backlog. Gas turbines under contract stood at 50 gigawatts (a gigawatt is roughly the output of one large power plant). The stated goal was to reach "over 60 gigawatts between backlog and reservation agreements" by year-end and to hit 20 gigawatts of annual turbine output in the second half of 2026. Wind was already the problem child, and management said so plainly: offshore contracts "running at losses," onshore orders "light."

**July 2025 — the second leg appears.** The narrative widened from gas to the grid. Strazik introduced synchronous condensers (equipment that stabilises voltage on a grid carrying a lot of intermittent wind and solar) as "a credible $5 billion market opportunity a year," flagged Saudi and Middle East demand, and disclosed almost $500 million of data-centre orders in the first half against $600 million in all of 2024. He also volunteered the negative — "weaker European HVDC orders in '25 ... some projects canceled or moving to the right as affordability challenges in EU becomes even more real." Volunteering a cancellation is a credibility marker.

**October 2025 — capital deployed.** The company bought the remaining half of Prolec GE, its 30-year North American transformer joint venture, for $5.275 billion. Prolec was disclosed at roughly $3 billion of revenue at 25% margins on earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation (EBITDA) — that is about seven times EBITDA for the whole business, a genuinely disciplined price for a supply-constrained transformer maker.

**January 2026 — the numbers go vertical.** Total backlog $150 billion, up $31 billion in a year. Gas under contract jumped from 46 gigawatts entering 2025 to 83 leaving it. Equipment backlog grew about 50% with "an incremental 6 points in equipment margin expansion." The dividend doubled and the buyback authorisation went from $6 billion to $10 billion.

**April 2026 — the horizon pulls forward.** Backlog $163 billion; the $200 billion milestone moved from 2028 to 2027. Gas under contract 83 to 100 gigawatts. And the single most commercially significant line in five calls: 2026 orders would price "10 to 20 points higher than our Q4 2025 orders on a dollar per kW basis." That is a price increase, not a volume story.

**July 2026 (verified separately).** Orders $24.2 billion, up 88% organically; backlog $176 billion; gas under contract 116 gigawatts heading to "at least 125" by year-end; capacity ambition extended to 30 gigawatts by 2030. Full-year revenue guidance raised to $45.5–46.5 billion and cash-flow guidance nearly doubled.

Nothing has been quietly dropped. Offshore wind, the one genuinely bad business, has been discussed openly and repeatedly rather than buried.

## 3. Tone and confidence trajectory

**Rising, and specific rather than promotional.**

- Q1 2025: "I am as confident and optimistic today as any day since our spin." Paired immediately with a hard cost admission — tariffs "$300 to $400 million in 2025."
- Q2 2025: sober. Year-to-date wind losses of about $300 million disclosed unprompted, European HVDC cancellations disclosed unprompted, and a specific warning that only "directionally 45% of next year's revenue" in onshore wind was in backlog.
- Q4 2025: still balanced — "on the negative, we have been impacted by the U.S. government halting of all offshore wind activity on December 22."
- Q1 2026: the most confident call, but the confidence attaches to countable things — 100 gigawatts under contract, 280 machines installed in factories, 10 to 20 points of price.

The language has moved from offensive-with-caveats to offensive-with-receipts. What is notably absent is the promotional vocabulary that defines this sector's failures: no total-addressable-market slide substituting for orders, no "transformational" partnerships without dollars attached, no reliance on non-binding memoranda of understanding as evidence.

The one recurring rhetorical soft spot is the phrase "a lot that we owe you" — used repeatedly in mid-2025 to defer wind detail. That deferral was eventually honoured with real disclosure, but it is the pattern to watch.

## 4. Guidance credibility

**High.** Track the promises forward:

- **20 gigawatts of annual gas output in the second half of 2026** — promised April 2025, reaffirmed every quarter, and as of July 2026 confirmed on track for the third quarter of 2026 with 280 machines installed and roughly 1,500 production workers added.
- **"Over 60 gigawatts between backlog and reservation agreements" by end-2025** — promised April 2025. Delivered 83 gigawatts. Substantial over-delivery.
- **"Approximately 100 gigawatts under contract in 2026"** — promised January 2026. Reached by 31 March 2026, nine months early, and 116 by June.
- **$200 billion backlog by 2028** — promised at the December 2025 investor update, pulled forward to 2027 four months later.
- **Electrification revenue "$13.5 to $14 billion" for 2026** — January 2026. Raised to "approach $14.5 billion" in April, then to $14.5–15.0 billion in July.
- **Wind** — the one place guidance has been repeatedly walked backwards. July 2025 promised the second half "closer to breakeven"; the Vineyard Wind stop-work in December 2025 forced a fresh accrual; Dogger Bank completion slipped from "a stub period" to "the four quarters of 2027."

There is a mild sandbagging pattern — floors set low and beaten — which management essentially admitted in October 2025: "like we've been managing with our core business, we're often trying to provide floors on day 1 and then to build off of them." That is a stated policy, not a discovered deception, and it has held for five consecutive quarters.

**Reliability score: High**, with wind as the standing exception.

## 5. Analyst pressure points

**The orders-versus-slot-reservations question** is the one analysts keep returning to, and management's answer is unusually candid. Michael Blum (Wells Fargo, October 2025) asked why backlog growth is mostly slot reservation agreements rather than firm orders. Strazik: "the orders classification to be candid is more our classification than the customers ... in many cases, they're providing us enough financial capital on day 1 that one could argue we could be booking these things as orders." Ken Parks added that down payments are "nonrefundable" in both categories.

This cuts both ways and the committee should hold both edges. It is a genuinely conservative accounting choice — most promoters would book the reservation as an order. But it also means that of the 116 gigawatts under contract, only about 44 sit in formal backlog. The remaining roughly 72 gigawatts is customer money against a slot, not a delivery obligation with fixed terms. A cancellation there costs the customer a deposit, not a lawsuit.

**Framework agreements are the soft spot.** David Arcaro pressed on them in April 2026. Strazik: "conversations continue on roughly 30 to 35 framework agreements, but we have not closed one to date." These have been discussed "for a period of time." Thirty-five conversations and zero closings is exactly the kind of undated language that is worth nothing until one signs. To management's credit, they said so themselves rather than counting the pipeline.

**Gross margin dilution** was pushed by Andrew Obin (July 2025) — why are service gross margins declining? Parks answered "a little bit of a mix issue, nothing there at all to be concerned about." That was a partial non-answer at the time. The subsequent year has vindicated it: gross margin has since expanded.

**What analysts are not pressing hard enough on:** the composition of free cash flow. The $5.1 billion quarter was celebrated; the fact that it was a $6.4 billion working-capital inflow was not interrogated on the call. When order intake plateaus — and 88% organic order growth cannot repeat indefinitely — the float stops growing and cash flow converges toward earnings, which are roughly half the current pace.

## 6. Red flags and green flags

**Red flags**

- **Trailing headline numbers are optically inflated** (Q1 2026). A $4.3 billion package of one-time gains sits inside trailing EBITDA and trailing earnings per share. Any valuation anchored on those figures is anchored on nothing.
- **Cash flow is prepayment float** (Q2 2026). Working capital supplied more than the whole of trailing free cash flow. The company's own multi-year target implies 2027–2028 runs at roughly half the 2026 pace.
- **Buying back stock at the top** (2025 through 2026). $3.6 billion returned in 2025 and $3.9 billion in the first half of 2026, executed while the shares roughly tripled. Retiring stock near $1,000 after a 92% move above the 200-day average is the classic industrial-cycle capital-allocation error.
- **Insiders sell, never buy.** Three open-market sales totalling 12,452 shares, zero open-market purchases.
- **No full-cycle record.** GE Vernova was spun out in April 2024. There is no observable behaviour from this management team, in this configuration, through a downturn. The capital-discipline test has not been administered.
- **Only 44 of 116 gigawatts is contractual backlog.** The rest is deposit-secured slots.
- **Wind remains structurally impaired** — US offshore activity halted by federal action in December 2025, Dogger Bank completion pushed through 2027, US onshore "remains soft" as of April 2026.

**Green flags**

- **Price, not just volume** (April 2026). Orders priced 10 to 20 points higher per kilowatt than three months earlier, with gross margin confirming it in the accounts.
- **Equipment backlog margin up 6 points in 2025**, including 11 points in Power — the profit is locked in before delivery, and delivery of the best-priced orders does not even begin until 2027.
- **Services is the annuity.** Services were over 60% of backlog in early 2025; the power services book alone reached $70 billion. With 231 gas units on order and more than 100 not yet commissioned (April 2026), the installed base that generates decades of service revenue is still expanding rapidly.
- **Bad news arrives on time** — European HVDC cancellations, wind losses, and the offshore stop-work were all disclosed by management before analysts found them.
- **Disciplined acquisition.** Prolec at roughly seven times EBITDA, immediately accretive, in the single most supply-constrained product in the grid.
- **Balance sheet is genuinely strong** — $13.1 billion of cash against about $4.0 billion of total debt, roughly $9.2 billion net cash as of 30 June 2026, even after funding Prolec.
- **Operational proof rather than slideware** — reactor foundation being poured at Ontario Power Generation's Darlington site, 280 machines installed in gas factories, a transformer-tank process improvement cutting rework hours by nearly 70%.

## 7. Hidden signals

**"Higher for longer gas" became "more gas longer."** In April 2025 Strazik framed a normalising market. By July 2025 the framing had inverted: the US tax bill was described as "another bull case for gas" because renewables incentives pull demand forward and leave a firm-power hole in the back half of the decade. The company now sells into both sides of that trade — turbines when gas wins, transformers and synchronous condensers when renewables win. That is the structural insight behind the whole position.

**The vocabulary shifted from capacity to scarcity.** Early calls talked about adding shifts and workers — "quote-unquote easier stuff." Recent calls talk about selling 2030 and 2031 slots and declining to cap intake where a competitor has. Joseph Ritchie (April 2026) noted a major competitor refuses orders beyond 2030; Strazik said GE Vernova will keep taking 2031 and beyond. The company is behaving like the scarce asset in the chain.

**"Financial floor of demand."** Strazik's phrase in April 2026 for the installed-base service book. It is the most economically precise thing he has said: the moat is not the turbine, it is the multi-decade service contract that follows it.

**Commodity exposure is contractually transferred.** Asked directly (January 2026) how contracts handle rising input costs, Strazik: "from when we take orders ... we lock in with the suppliers the price. So it's generally matched. The only exception ... is with our long-term service contracts, and those have material escalators." This matters because copper, grain-oriented electrical steel and turbine superalloys are input costs here, never revenue. The protection is real but it is only as good as supplier solvency across three-to-five-year lead times.

**Robotics and artificial intelligence entered as productivity, not narrative.** Introduced in July 2025 with an explicit sequencing discipline — "a business must get to standard work before investing in AI" — and quantified by April 2026 as 13 process transformations going to 26 and "tens of millions of dollars every year." Small numbers, honestly sized. Promoters do the opposite.

## 8. Capital-allocation verdict (for the director)

**Trajectory: Strengthening.**

**The single thing to weigh before sizing:** this is an exceptional business at a price that already discounts the 2028 plan and most of 2029. Nothing in five quarters of transcripts or in the verified accounts undermines the operating story. What undermines the investment is arithmetic. At $1,019.74 the enterprise is valued near $264 billion. Against the company's own 2028 target of $56 billion of revenue at a 20% EBITDA margin — roughly $11.2 billion — that is about 23.5 times 2028 EBITDA, which is what Siemens Energy trades at *today* on *trailing* numbers. Against clean trailing EBITDA of roughly $4.4 billion it is about 60 times. The forward return is therefore the speed at which estimates roll forward, not a re-rating, and it is exposed to any quarter where the order and deposit machine merely slows.

**Economic moat: wide, and widening.** The moat type is a blend of scale-cost and switching-cost. Heavy-duty gas turbines are a three-firm global oligopoly, and GE Vernova has by a distance the largest installed base — "when you have an exponentially larger installed base than the other OEMs" is a factual statement, not a boast. Every unit shipped attaches a service contract measured in decades, with material-cost escalators built in. The evidence supports "widening" rather than merely "wide": gross margin up about 100 basis points on 22% more revenue, adjusted EBITDA margin up 340 basis points organically in the latest quarter, six points of margin added to the equipment backlog in 2025, and realised prices up 10 to 20 points per kilowatt. Rising returns on expanding margins is the signature of a moat getting wider, not a company harvesting one.

**Secular threat: manageable.** The obvious candidate is the energy transition, and it points the wrong way for the bear. GE Vernova sells the hardware for whichever path the world takes — gas turbines if firm power wins, transformers, switchgear and grid-stabilisation equipment if renewables win, and small modular reactors if nuclear restarts. The genuinely impaired leg is wind, which is roughly a fifth of revenue, loss-making offshore, and subject to US policy reversal. That is a material drag on one segment, not a threat to terminal value. The cheapness question does not arise: this is not a fear-print and not a value trap. It is a high-quality asset whose price has run ahead of a very good plan.
