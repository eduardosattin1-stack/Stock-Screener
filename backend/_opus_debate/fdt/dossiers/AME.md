# AMETEK, Inc. (NYSE: AME) — Forensic Dossier

**Prepared 2026-08-04 · price used $246.69 (US dollars, NYSE listing) · six quarterly calls reviewed, first quarter 2025 through first quarter 2026, plus the second-quarter 2026 release published this morning.**

A note on timing before anything else. AMETEK reported its second-quarter 2026 results at 6:55 a.m. Eastern today, hours before this dossier was written. The transcript file supplied to the committee ends with the first-quarter 2026 call on 30 April. Everything in this dossier that refers to the June quarter comes from the company's own press release and filed income statement dated today, not from the transcript bundle, and is marked as such.

## 1. NARRATIVE ARC

The story management told across these six quarters did not change. What changed is that the numbers finally caught up to it.

In the first quarter of 2025 the company was defending. Sales were $1.73 billion, essentially flat against the prior year, and organic sales — the growth you get from the businesses you already owned, stripping out acquisitions and currency — were actually down 1%. Chief executive Dave Zapico spent most of that call on tariffs, on "a rapidly evolving trade conflict," and on why AMETEK's decentralised structure would let it absorb the hit. The forward-looking content was almost entirely about resilience: mitigation plans, localising production, a "flexible balance sheet," and a willingness to buy back stock "as we have shown in prior periods of market dislocation."

By the second quarter of 2025 the defensive frame was still there but a growth lever had been added: the $920 million purchase of FARO Technologies, a 3D measurement and "digital reality" business with roughly $340 million of annual sales and, importantly, recurring service and subscription revenue. Organic sales that quarter were flat. The growth in the headline came from deals.

The third quarter of 2025 is where the tone genuinely turned. Sales rose 11% to $1.89 billion, organic sales grew 4%, and orders jumped 13%. Zapico named the new drivers explicitly for the first time — "positive inflection in our Automation & Engineered Solutions markets," a "growing pipeline of opportunities within our Power businesses," and "strong secular trends driving that market."

The fourth quarter of 2025 and the first quarter of 2026 completed the shift. Fourth-quarter organic sales grew 5% and orders 18%. Then the first quarter of 2026 delivered something AMETEK had not produced in a decade: organic orders up 22%, against a nine-year average organic growth rate that Zapico himself puts at 4%. The vocabulary moved decisively to offence — defence modernisation, NATO spending, low-earth-orbit satellites, commercial nuclear, and computing hardware sold into semiconductor tools serving artificial-intelligence demand.

Two threads were quietly dropped along the way, and both deserve note. First, the tariff mitigation narrative that consumed the 2025 calls has faded to a single sentence; by the first quarter of 2026 Zapico said the tariff-driven pricing disconnects that hurt 2025 had "gone away." Second, the research and academia end market — which he sized at "about 10% of AMETEK" in the second quarter of 2025 and repeatedly flagged as a headwind — has stopped being discussed as a problem, replaced by the observation that nuclear and advanced-materials research is picking up. Neither drop looks like concealment; both look like conditions that resolved. But the research exposure is real and it is the one soft spot management has stopped talking about.

**Where the story is richer than the numbers.** Two places. First, the word "record" appears so often it stops carrying information — a business growing 10% a year sets a sales record almost every quarter by construction. Second, and more substantively, the reported operating margin has been consistently and materially below the "core" margin management leads with. Over 2025 the full-year reported operating margin rose 10 basis points while core margins rose 80. The difference is the cost of the acquisition strategy, and it is a real cost borne by shareholders even if it is temporary and even if the acquired businesses later improve.

**A data-quality correction for the committee.** The deterministic metrics supplied with this candidate carry a gross-margin series labelled "recent to old" reading 34.88%, 36.14%, 35.68%, 36.38%. Read as labelled, that says margins compressed by 1.5 points. Read against the filed annual statements, that sequence is exactly AMETEK's fiscal 2022 through fiscal 2025 gross margins in **old-to-recent** order. The array is correctly computed and correctly summarised as expanding; only the field name is reversed. The direction is expansion, and the trailing-twelve-month figure of 36.58% extends it.

## 3. TONE & CONFIDENCE TRAJECTORY

**Rising, and the inflection is datable to the third quarter of 2025.**

- *Q1 2025 — guarded.* "Despite a continued uncertain economic environment." "Although it is impossible to know how these trade conflicts will evolve." The confidence is about capability, not demand.
- *Q2 2025 — guarded, with a hedge on his own markets.* Asked to name the weak spots, Zapico was unusually direct: "the two negatives will be the semiconductor market and the research academia market. And it will be in the U.S. and globally."
- *Q3 2025 — the turn.* "Positive inflection." "Benefiting from the strong secular trends." Talking about a two-year stretch of manufacturing activity below the expansion line, he said he had been told at an investor conference that it "never happened" before.
- *Q4 2025 — confident and specific.* Vitality index — the share of sales from products launched in the last three years — hit 30%, up from a steady 26%, and he named data-centre products as a contributor.
- *Q1 2026 — offensive.* Named programmes replace generalities: three unmanned-aerial-vehicle programmes (one American, two with NATO allies), a low-earth-orbit satellite radio-frequency component order, nuclear submarine fluid transfer, a semiconductor tool maker buying Abaco computing for artificial-intelligence-driven demand.

Guidance has become **more** specific over time, not less — the opposite of the pattern that usually precedes trouble. In the first quarter of 2026 Zapico volunteered a segment-by-segment incremental-margin breakdown unprompted. Executives hiding a deterioration do not volunteer that.

One counter-signal worth recording: the confidence is asymmetric on the downside. Asked in the first quarter of 2026 about aerospace maintenance demand given a Gulf conflict and aviation fuel shortage, he said "if we think there's any downside, it's extremely modest" — while also conceding "that can all change in a week." The hedge is honest. The base case is not stress-tested aloud.

## 4. GUIDANCE CREDIBILITY

**Score: High — with an explicit sandbagging pattern the committee must price.**

Four consecutive quarters of guidance were beaten by seven to ten cents each, and the full-year number has been raised at every opportunity: from $7.02–7.18 mid-2025 to a delivered $7.43; and for 2026 from $7.94–8.14 up to $8.20–8.30 in this morning's release, a twenty-one-cent lift at the midpoint on top of a seven-cent raise one quarter earlier.

Zapico does not hide the mechanism. In the first quarter of 2026, twice in the same call: "it's a conservative guide" and "we're very confident in our guide. We think it's prudent to do what we did."

This cuts two ways and the committee should hold both. The guide is a floor, which lowers the risk of a negative surprise. But it also means a beat-and-raise is the *expected* outcome, not new information — which is precisely why the market's reaction today is so instructive. The stock opened at $254.70, printed a fresh 52-week high of $257.39, and by the time of writing had faded back to $246.69. A beat-and-raise that cannot hold its opening print is telling you the beat was already owned.

## 5. ANALYST PRESSURE POINTS

Analysts pushed hardest on three things, and management answered all three — though one answer is thinner than it sounds.

**The backlog conversion question.** Andrew Buscaglia (BNP Paribas) in the first quarter of 2025: "your backlog has been at record levels almost every quarter now going back as far as I remember... what gets this backlog to convert?" This is the right question and it has not gone away. It got a partial answer — tariff-driven delays were the 2025 blocker and have since cleared — but a backlog that only grows is not obviously better than one that converts. Book-to-bill above 1.1 for two straight quarters means AMETEK is currently *adding* to the pile faster than it ships.

**Order durability.** Jacob Moore (KeyBanc) and Nigel Coe (Wolfe) both probed whether order strength was genuine demand or an easy comparison against a destocking trough. Zapico's answer in the third quarter of 2025 — "I take your point that it's an easy comp, but it is what it is" — is candid rather than evasive, but it concedes the point. And in the first quarter of 2026 he disclosed that "several large orders in the quarter... help fill in our full year sales outlook." Large lumpy orders inflate book-to-bill and do not repeat. Neither he nor any analyst quantified what share of the 22–25% organic order growth those large orders represent. **That is the single most important unanswered question in this file.**

**Named customers.** There are none. Across six calls management names its own subsidiaries — Abaco, Kern, Gatan, Spectro, Virtek, Rauland, Paragon, FARO, NSI — and the *type* of programme, but never a counterparty and never a contract value. The one named platform, the ARTEMIS 2 crewed mission, is a delivery already made, not a forward order. This is a genuine verification gap. The mitigating fact is that AMETEK's diversification is real and disclosed — no single customer reaches a material share of sales — so the absence of names reflects a portfolio of thousands of small contracts rather than concealment of a concentrated one. But it does mean an outside analyst cannot independently verify a single order in that backlog.

A topic analysts have **not** pushed on and should: intangibles are three-quarters of the balance sheet after roughly $5.7 billion of acquisitions since 2021, and nobody has asked what happens to reported returns if one of them disappoints.

## 6. RED FLAGS / GREEN FLAGS

**Green flag — no dilution, at all (six years).** Diluted shares outstanding: 231.2 million (2020), 232.8m (2021), 231.5m (2022), 231.5m (2023), 232.2m (2024), 231.3m (2025), 229.8 million in the June 2026 quarter. Equity issuance across the entire period is $36 million of option exercises. For a sector where the standard failure mode is a company that funds itself by repeatedly selling stock, this is the cleanest possible reading.

**Green flag — buybacks were done on weakness, not at the top.** Repurchases by year: $5m (2020), $15m (2021), $333m (2022), $8m (2023), $212m (2024), $434m (2025). The largest two programmes ran in 2022 and 2025 — years in which the shares traded well below today's $246.69, with the 52-week low at $179.24. Management bought when it was cheap and has not been buying aggressively into this record high. That is discipline, and it is also a quiet signal about how they see the current price.

**Green flag — capital spending never inflated through the cycle.** Capital expenditure: $74m, $111m, $139m, $136m, $127m, $130m across 2020–2025 — roughly 1.7% to 2.3% of sales, and 1.69% on a trailing basis. AMETEK did not build capacity into the boom. There is no half-finished plant to write down, and the sustaining-versus-growth capital question is close to moot at this intensity.

**Green flag — the balance sheet is genuinely unlevered.** Net debt of $485 million at 30 June 2026 (borrowings of $981m less $495m of cash) against roughly $2.5 billion of trailing earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation — about 0.2 times. Solvency scores are at the top of the range (Altman-Z 8.1, Piotroski 8 of 9).

**Green flag — margin expansion through a severe input-cost spike.** This is the strongest single fact in the file and is developed in section 7.

**Red flag — intangible assets are 73.9% of total assets.** Return on *tangible* assets is 36.4% while return on total assets is 9.5%; the entire gap is purchase accounting. Return on invested capital is 11.4% against a 14.2% return on equity — respectable, but not the 20%-plus you would infer from the operating margins alone, because the capital base includes what AMETEK paid for these businesses. The "never written off goodwill" record is real, and it is also a record that can only ever get worse.

**Red flag — reported operating margin expansion is far slower than the headline.** Full-year 2025: reported operating margin up 10 basis points, core up 80. The compounding shareholders actually receive runs closer to the reported line.

**Red flag — zero insider buying at a record price.** Open-market activity shows no purchases and one small sale of 530 shares. On its own this is not evidence of anything. Combined with a stock at 98% of its 52-week range and a buyback that has not been leaned into at these levels, it forms a consistent picture.

**Red flag — superlative density.** "Bulletproof model," "unblemished record," "firing on all cylinders," "outstanding" used a dozen times per call. The saving grace, and it is a large one, is that every superlative in this file is attached to a number that reconciles to the filings. Promotional language over verifiable results is a style problem. Promotional language over unverifiable results is a fraud problem. This is the former.

## 7. HIDDEN SIGNALS

**The margin story survives a raw-material shock — and that is the whole moat argument.** Copper traded at $6.47 per pound on 3 August 2026, up about 46% year on year and 4.75% in the past month. Neodymium-praseodymium, the input to the rare-earth permanent magnets that go into electric motors, has approached one million Chinese yuan per tonne in 2026, with year-on-year increases in the 80–90% range. Electrical steel averaged roughly $763 per metric tonne in the second quarter of 2026 with energy-driven cost pressure still building. Every one of these is an **input cost** to AMETEK, never a revenue tailwind — the company makes motors and instruments, it does not sell metal. Against that backdrop, the Electromechanical Group — the motor and magnet-intensive half of the business — just reported core margin expansion of 290 basis points to a 26.3% operating margin, its highest ever. A company being commoditised cannot do that. Zapico's claim in the second quarter of 2025 that "the impacts of tariff and inflation will be offset by price" and that this "speaks to the highly differentiated nature of the AMETEK product portfolio" was, at the time, an assertion. Three quarters later it is a demonstrated fact.

**The Paragon disclosure is the most useful thing management has said about its own model.** In the fourth quarter of 2025 Zapico described the $1.9 billion Paragon Medical purchase: "we bought a business that, quite honestly, was undermanaged on the operations side." Bought during a customer destocking cycle when the market was nervous, restructured, and now the largest single contributor to record Electromechanical margins. That is a specific, checkable account of how the acquisition machine actually creates value, and the margin trajectory corroborates it.

**A vocabulary shift worth watching.** Through 2025 the company described itself as diversified and defensive. From the third quarter of 2025 onward the language attaches to named secular themes — defence modernisation, artificial-intelligence infrastructure, commercial nuclear, space. That is where the multiple expansion has come from. It is also where the vulnerability sits: a diversified instruments compounder that gets re-rated as a themed growth asset can be de-rated back to a diversified instruments compounder without a single number changing.

**On the chain assignment.** This candidate reaches the committee tagged to the electrification and grid chain, and the chain map itself concedes the fit is only medium confidence, noting that "no single chain dominates revenue." That assessment is correct and the committee should not over-weight the grid frame. AMETEK's actual second-quarter 2026 drivers were aerospace and defence (~18% of sales), medical technology, discrete automation, semiconductor test computing and space — with power and grid a real but secondary contributor. This is not a grid pure-play; judging it as one would misprice both the upside and the risk.

**On the payback profile.** This is a cash-now business, not a story. Free cash flow of $1.70 billion on $7.86 billion of trailing revenue is a 21.7% free-cash margin, converting above 100% of net income for three consecutive years. There is no funding gap, no runway question, and no dependence on capital markets. Whatever the risk here is, it is not solvency.

## 8. CAPITAL-ALLOCATION VERDICT

**Classification: STRENGTHENING.**

Every operating vector improved across these six quarters. Organic sales went from −1% to +11% (Electromechanical, first quarter 2026). Organic orders went from +3% to +25%. Backlog went from $3.47 billion to $4.11 billion. Gross margin expanded through the worst copper and rare-earth cost environment in a decade. Free cash conversion held above 100%. Leverage fell to two-tenths of one year's earnings. The share count went down. Guidance was raised twice in 2026.

**The single most important thing the director must weigh before sizing:** none of that is in dispute, and none of it is a secret. The entire question is price. The shares sit at 98% of their 52-week range, roughly 12% above the 200-day average, at about 7.3 times enterprise value to sales — the second-richest name in its own peer group and dearer on that measure than Eaton, which owns the pure data-centre power franchise the market is actually paying for. The company's own intrinsic-value models, and an independent discounted-cash-flow published on 17 June 2026, all land far below the market price. The freshest analyst target on file, Morgan Stanley's $245.00 dated 10 July 2026, is *below* where the stock trades today. And on the day of a genuine beat-and-raise, the shares opened at $254.70, made a new high at $257.39, and gave it all back. The business is compounding beautifully. The security is not obviously mispriced. Those are two different findings and only one of them argues for buying today.

**Economic moat: WIDE, and widening.** The type is a blend of switching costs and intangible engineering assets. AMETEK sells mission-critical, customised, low-cost-of-failure components — pressure transducers on a crewed spacecraft, ruggedised computing that controls a semiconductor tool, radiation detection in a nuclear facility. The dollar value is small relative to the system it protects, which is the classic structure of pricing power. The evidence supports the width rather than merely asserting it: gross margin rose 1.5 percentage points over three years and another 0.2 points in the last twelve months, on revenue growing 12.7%; Electromechanical core margins expanded 290 basis points to a record while its two principal raw materials inflated 46% and 80–90% respectively; the vitality index — new-product share of sales — stepped from 26% to 30%. Rising returns and expanding margins on accelerating volume is the definition of a widening moat. The one qualifier, and it is why the width is a judgement rather than a formality: return on invested capital of 11.4% is good, not exceptional, because three-quarters of the asset base is what AMETEK paid to acquire these franchises. The moat is around the operating businesses; the purchase price partly fills the moat back in.

**Secular threat: manageable.** Look for the structural force that impairs terminal value and it is hard to find one here. Artificial intelligence does not displace a thin-film pressure transducer or a rare-earth motor; the second-quarter 2026 order book suggests it *buys* them, through semiconductor tooling and data-centre power. There is no reimbursement squeeze on a diversified industrial, no channel shift, no disintermediation. The energy transition is a customer, not a predator. The two real structural exposures are narrower: about 10% of sales sit in research and academia, where public funding is politically contingent and was a live headwind through 2025; and the acquisition-funded model needs a continuing supply of reasonably priced private industrial assets, which becomes harder if private-market valuations stay elevated. Neither impairs the terminal value of the earnings base. The risk here is cyclical and valuation-driven, not terminal.

The cheapness question therefore does not arise: this is not a fear-print and it is not a value trap, because it is not cheap. It is a high-quality compounder at a full price, and the discipline required is patience, not diagnosis.
