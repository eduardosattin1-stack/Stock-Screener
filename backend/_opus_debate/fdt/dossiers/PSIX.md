# Power Solutions International (PSIX) — Forensic Dossier

**Prepared 11 August 2026 · price used: $41.44 (Nasdaq, US dollars) · market value about $955 million**

*Coverage note, stated up front so nothing here is over-read: the transcript bundle supplied to me contains one call — the second-quarter 2026 call held 6 August 2026 (prepared remarks plus the analyst question-and-answer, with the middle of the call omitted for length). I do not have seven prior quarters of management commentary. Everything below that describes the arc across earlier quarters is reconstructed from the filed quarterly financials, from company press releases, and from the dated disclosures documented in the securities class-action complaint — not from transcripts I was not given. Where I say "management said," I am quoting the one call I have.*

---

## 1. Narrative arc

The company builds emission-certified internal-combustion engines and the natural-gas, propane and diesel generator sets built around them. For most of its life the money came from oil and gas field equipment and from industrial and transportation engines. Beginning in 2025 the story changed completely: management repositioned the business as a supplier of power systems to artificial-intelligence data centers, and the market repriced it accordingly. The shares reached $119.70 within the last twelve months and trade at $41.44 today, a fall of about 65% from that high.

The arc has three distinct chapters, and the financials date each one precisely.

**Chapter one — the boom (through mid-2025).** Revenue went from $565 million in 2024 to $722 million in 2025, up about 28%. Gross margin actually rose over that stretch, from 24.7% to 25.4% for the full year. The second quarter of 2025 was the peak: $191.9 million of revenue at a 28.0% gross margin, and a reported net income of $51.2 million — of which $29.2 million, or $1.27 per share, was a one-time accounting benefit from releasing a valuation allowance on deferred tax assets, not operating profit. Management has been consistent and clear about flagging that one-time item, including on the current call, and that deserves credit.

**Chapter two — the ramp bites (November 2025 to March 2026).** On 6 November 2025 the company reported third-quarter gross margin of about 23.9%, roughly five points below the prior year, attributing it to "temporary inefficiencies" in an accelerated production ramp for data-center product lines. On 2 March 2026 the fourth-quarter report showed gross margin down about eight points year over year on the same cause. The stock fell nearly 29% on that second disclosure. Those two dates now bracket the class period of a securities-fraud lawsuit filed in the Northern District of Illinois covering purchasers from 8 May 2025 through 2 March 2026.

**Chapter three — the repair job (2026 to date).** The first quarter of 2026 was the trough: $128.6 million of revenue at a 22.7% gross margin and only $13.2 million of earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation. The second quarter, reported 6 August 2026, was a genuine sequential improvement — $152.5 million of revenue, up 18.6% from the first quarter, with gross margin recovering 4.2 points to 27.1%, $56.6 million of cash from operations, and total borrowings cut by about $30.8 million. The shares gapped from $33.02 to about $40 the next morning.

What was quietly dropped along the way is worth naming. The oil and gas business, which management concedes carries "relatively high gross margin for our products," is in an unrelieved downturn — "we're not seeing any sign for significant improvement." What replaced it is a data-center business the company will not size, will not date, and will not attach a customer name to.

## 3. Tone and confidence

**Declining, and unusually so for a quarter that beat.** This is the tell. Management delivered a large sequential improvement and then spent the call refusing to extrapolate it.

On revenue: "we are not predicting any specific level of data center revenue in any future period." On margin: "we are not providing a specific gross margin outlook for 2026 at this time," and the trajectory "will depend on product mix, flow paths and other operational factors." On next year: "we are not providing formal guidance for the sales outlook for '26 or '27," with 2027 visibility promised only "maybe in 4Q."

The single most revealing exchange came when an analyst asked what normalised gross margin looks like once Wisconsin stabilises. The answer named every variable that could move the number — "mix, pricing and variable efficiency, fixed cost absorption, all these kinds of things" — and then landed on 25%, which is where the business already was two years ago.

The language has also shifted from offensive to defensive. The forward-looking-statements preamble now runs to a full paragraph naming capacity ramp throughput, oil and gas demand, tariffs, acquisition integration, "changes in management or other personnel," and "pending or threatened litigation." That list is the risk register of a company under legal pressure, not the register of a supplier riding a shortage.

## 4. Guidance credibility

**Low.** The company gives essentially no quantitative guidance, which makes a formal delivery-versus-promise scorecard impossible — and that opacity is itself the finding. What can be tested is the one directional promise management does make, and it has a history.

The promise on the current call is that second-half 2026 revenue will exceed first-half 2026, and that internally the second half will be "consistent with the second half we had last year." The first half produced $281.1 million; the second half of 2025 produced $395.1 million. Meeting the internal expectation therefore requires about $395 million in the back half — a 40% step up from the first half, in a business that has just declined 21% year over year, with no disclosed backlog to underwrite it.

The one guidance data point I can score positively: management told investors the second quarter would be "generally consistent with the first quarter," and it came in 18.6% higher. That is a beat, and it is the reason the shares gapped up. It is also the pattern of a company that has learned to set the bar low after being sued for setting it high.

Reliability: **Low**, on absence of disclosure rather than on demonstrated dishonesty.

## 5. Analyst pressure points

Three questions were pushed on the current call, and all three were deflected.

**Competition.** An analyst framed it plainly: Generac uses Baudouin engines, which are Weichai engines; Power Solutions also uses a Weichai engine; what is the actual difference in size, price and performance? The answer was that "we serve different customers," followed by "frankly, I'm not so clear which type of engine gensets they sell to Generac," and "I don't think we're in the direct competition." The analyst gave up: "I'll just take the rest of this offline." A management team that cannot describe how its product differs from its largest listed competitor's — when both buy engines from the same source — has not answered the central question about its moat.

**2027 visibility.** Asked directly for colour on 2027, management gave demand adjectives and deferred the numbers to the fourth quarter.

**Normalised margin.** Asked what the business earns once the ramp is done, management gave the 25% figure described above.

What analysts are not yet pressing on, and should be: the company discloses no backlog, no book-to-bill, no customer names, and no concentration figures for a business whose entire investment case rests on contracted data-center volume.

## 6. Red flags and green flags

**🔴 The controlling shareholder and the then-chief executive sold into the top, weeks before the first bad disclosure.** This is the most serious finding in the file and it is fully documented in Forms 4. Weichai America Corporation, the controlling holder, executed a programme of open-market sales in late August and September 2025 at prices between roughly $80.66 and $95.20, leaving it with 10,672,860 shares as of 11 September 2025. On 9 September 2025 the chief executive, Constantine Xykis, sold 7,854 shares at $91.31 and reported **zero** shares owned afterwards. Director He Hong sold across 8–11 September 2025 at $89 to $95. Fifty-seven days later, on 6 November 2025, came the margin disclosure that started the collapse. Across the whole window our data shows one open-market purchase of 300 shares against 86 sales totalling 897,419 shares.

**🔴 An active securities-fraud class action, plus at least six separate derivative investigations.** *Dishion v. Power Solutions International* was filed in the Northern District of Illinois covering 8 May 2025 to 2 March 2026, alleging the company misrepresented its ability to capture data-center demand and understated the cost and inefficiency of the capacity ramp. The lead-plaintiff deadline passed 19 May 2026. Separate firms announced board-level fiduciary-duty investigations through 10 July 2026.

**🔴 No backlog disclosure of any kind.** The company states plainly that data-center revenue timing is "subject to customer scheduling, manufacturing flow paths, supply chain factors and other variables." There is no order book, no book-to-bill, no named counterparty, no dated milestone. For a capital-goods business selling into a boom, this is the single most important number a management team can give, and it is absent.

**🔴 The engines come from the parent.** Weichai supplies the core engine, and a January 2025 purchase agreement commits the company to a stated minimum of roughly $309 million over five years. The technology moat is rented from the controlling shareholder, who also sets the transfer price and can reallocate supply.

**🔴 Leadership churn at the worst possible moment.** The chief financial officer has been serving as interim chief executive. Richard Hu, most recently a vice-president and general manager at BorgWarner, becomes chief executive on 17 August 2026. New chief executives arriving after a stock collapse and a lawsuit have a well-known incentive to reset expectations downward in their first full quarter.

**🟢 The operational repair is real and measurable.** Gross margin up 4.2 points sequentially, revenue up 18.6% sequentially, debt down $32.4 million in a single quarter. Management named the mechanisms — labour efficiency, material availability, fixed-cost absorption on higher volume — and the numbers corroborate them.

**🟢 The balance sheet is genuinely sound.** Net debt of $64.7 million including capitalised leases sits at 0.67 times trailing earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation. Excluding leases it is under $10 million. Current assets cover current liabilities 2.6 times. There is no funding cliff and no dilution pressure.

**🟢 Returns on capital remain high.** Return on invested capital of about 21% and return on equity of about 37% on the trailing year, with capital spending at roughly 1% of revenue. Whatever else is wrong here, this is not a capital-destroying business.

**🟢 The one-time tax benefit was disclosed repeatedly and prominently.** Management raised the $29.2 million valuation-allowance release three separate times on the call unprompted, explicitly telling investors to "keep that context in mind when reading the year-over-year comparison." That is the opposite of hiding it.

## 7. Hidden signals

**The market is described as constrained, but the company's own margin guidance denies it.** Management says "the capacity for the power system for the data center is constrained. So there's a high demand for the system." A genuinely capacity-constrained supplier raises price and prints expanding margin. This one guides to the 25% gross margin it earned in 2024 and 2025, and its trailing gross margin of 23.7% is below both. The scarcity is real; the pricing power is not showing up in the accounts. That gap is the whole debate.

**The capacity story is a leasing story.** Square footage rose more than fivefold while capital expenditure ran at about 1% of revenue. The commitment sits in $54.9 million of lease obligations. This is cheaper and more reversible than owning — which is prudent if orders slip, and which also means the "capacity" is not a barrier to anyone else entering.

**"Larger custom orders" is doing a lot of work.** The phrase appears in the omitted-middle section and again in the outlook. Custom orders are, by construction, single-customer. Combined with a refusal to disclose concentration, the reasonable inference is that the second-half inflection depends on a small number of projects with one or two buyers.

**The free-float is only about 8.5 million shares, roughly 37% of the 23.05 million outstanding.** The controlling holder's stake alone is larger than the entire tradable float — which explains both the violence of the move to $119.70 and the violence of the move back to $33.

## 8. Capital-allocation verdict

**Trajectory: PIVOTING.** The business is mid-transition from a cyclical oil-and-gas engine supplier into a data-center power-systems supplier, and it is doing so while repairing a botched manufacturing ramp, under litigation, with an interim chief executive handing over to an outsider in six days.

**The one thing to weigh before sizing anything:** the second-half 2026 revenue step-up is the entire investment case, and there is no disclosed order book behind it. Meeting management's own internal expectation requires about $395 million of revenue in six months against $281 million delivered in the first six. If it lands, the trailing multiple collapses and the stock is cheap. If it slips again — as it slipped from the second half of 2025 into the first half of 2026 — the earnings base resets to roughly the first-quarter run rate and the current price has no support beneath it until the low $20s, where the shares traded four months ago. You are underwriting an unverifiable number in a company whose controlling shareholder sold at three times today's price.

**Moat: narrow, and rented.** The defensible asset is regulatory: emissions certification from the Environmental Protection Agency and the California Air Resources Board across a broad engine range is expensive, slow, and a genuine barrier. That is a real licence-type moat and it explains why returns on capital are high. But the engines themselves come from the controlling shareholder under a fixed-minimum purchase agreement, and management could not articulate on the record how its product differs from a competitor buying from the same source. A moat you licence from your majority owner is a moat your majority owner can reprice.

**Moat trend: eroding.** Gross margin fell from 28.0% in the second quarter of 2025 to 21.7% in the fourth, and at 27.1% in the most recent quarter is still below the year-ago level, on revenue that is 21% lower. Trailing gross margin of 23.7% sits under the 25.4% earned in 2025. Returns are high but falling, which is the definition of erosion rather than width.

**Secular threat: manageable, not terminal.** The structural risk is not that engines disappear — data centres will need on-site generation for a long time, and the mix shift management describes, toward gas engines for prime power with diesel as standby and batteries for instantaneous response, favours a gas-engine specialist. The risk is displacement within the niche: gas turbines from far larger vendors, faster grid interconnection, and fuel cells all compete for the same behind-the-meter dollar, and this company is the smallest credible participant in that fight. That threatens the growth rate and the multiple; it does not obviously melt the terminal value of a certified engine platform with 21% returns on capital.
