# Forensic dossier — General Dynamics Corporation (GD)

*A deep dive on management and the setup. Prepared 4 August 2026. Share price used throughout: $386.99 (New York Stock Exchange, US dollars, live quote 4 August 2026). Transcripts reviewed: 23 July 2025, 24 October 2025, 28 January 2026, 29 April 2026, 29 July 2026.*

---

## 1. Narrative arc

Across five consecutive calls the story has not shifted — it has **compounded**. That is unusual and worth stating plainly at the top: most companies rotate their emphasis when a segment stumbles. General Dynamics has kept the same four-part story and simply added weight to it each quarter.

**Where it started (July 2025).** The framing was operational recovery. Gulfstream, the company's business-jet arm, was digging out of a supply-chain hole: management spoke about "retrofit airplanes," aircraft "completed before engines were installed," and faults that were "becoming easier to fix." The submarine yards were a growth story with an apology attached — big revenue, thin margins. Combat Systems was the quiet steady one. Technologies was flat.

**Where it is now (July 2026).** Gulfstream has gone from recovery to leadership: the second-quarter 2026 aerospace operating margin of 14.5% is 130 basis points (1.3 percentage points) better than a year earlier, deliveries hit 41 in the quarter, and the order intake ratio — book-to-bill, meaning new orders divided by revenue recognized — was 1.5 times. The submarine yards have gone from an excuse to the headline: on 29 July 2026, the same morning as the results, Electric Boat announced it had been awarded **$42.1 billion for nine additional Virginia-class attack submarines and $29.5 billion for five additional Columbia-class ballistic-missile submarines**, part of a $76.6 billion U.S. Navy announcement, with work running to September 2035 ([General Dynamics press release, 29 July 2026](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/general-dynamics-electric-boat-awarded-construction-contracts-for-14-submarines-as-part-of-76-6-billion-navy-award-302838350.html); [USNI News, 29 July 2026](https://news.usni.org/2026/07/29/navy-awards-76-6b-for-9-virginia-5-columbia-subs-to-general-dynamics-hii)). That award landed *after* the quarter closed, so it is **not** in the record $136.5 billion backlog the company reported.

**What was quietly added.** European rearmament. It appears first as a demand comment in October 2025 ("demand for all classes of combat vehicles across the European theater has been increasing"), then as hard orders in the fourth quarter of 2025 — more than $4 billion of Eagle tactical vehicles in Germany, $600 million of bridges in Norway and the United Kingdom, $640 million of light armoured vehicles in Canada — producing a 4.3-to-1 order ratio in that quarter alone.

**What was quietly dropped.** Share repurchases. In July 2025 the company simply had no cash to spare. By April 2026 the language had hardened into policy: buybacks are "a highly sensitive subject in this current environment," and the company now buys back only enough stock to offset employee compensation dilution. This is a real change in capital allocation dressed as a passing remark.

**What keeps getting deferred.** Gulfstream's next two aeroplanes. In July 2025 the chief executive said of the G400, "we've slowed down the 400 a bit because we've got our hands full." Twelve months later, in July 2026: "we've whipped up our efforts on the 400, and we'll have more to say over the next couple of quarters." She has now formally exited the business of dating them: "I'm no longer in the business of estimating [entry into service] given that the regulators set the pace."

## 2. Claims versus financials (forensic)

The trailing-twelve-month figures below run from the third quarter of 2025 through the second quarter of 2026 and come from the filed statements, not from the calls.

| Management's claim | The evidence | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| "Strong operating leverage" (repeated every call) | Revenue grew 9.1% over the last twelve months to $54.86 billion; operating earnings grew 9.4% to $5.66 billion; company operating margin 10.4% in the second quarter of 2026 versus 10.0% a year earlier | **Holds, narrowly.** Leverage is real but modest — roughly 40 basis points a year, not a step-change |
| "Superb cash generation" | Free cash flow of $3.6 billion in the first half of 2026, a conversion rate above 150% of net earnings; trailing free cash flow $6.45 billion, an 11.8% margin on revenue | **Holds, emphatically.** This is the single most impressive number in the file |
| Aerospace margins move "into the high teens... maybe 2026, but for sure, in 2027" (July 2025) | Aerospace margin: 13.2% → 13.3% → 15.0% → 14.5% over the last four reported quarters | **On track.** Two of the last three quarters cleared her 15% definition |
| Marine margin improvement "represents our most meaningful opportunity" (October 2025) | Marine operating margin: 6.9% → 7.1% → 7.2% → 7.4% → 7.3%. Roughly **40 basis points of progress in a full year**, against a target repeatedly framed around 8–9% | **The story is richer than the numbers.** Two years of "supply chain" answers have bought less than half a point of margin |
| "Robust order momentum," record backlog | Backlog $103.7 billion (mid-2025) → $131 billion (first quarter 2026, +48% year on year) → $136.5 billion (second quarter 2026, +32%). Total contract value including options $186.9 billion | **Holds and understates.** The July award adds roughly $71.6 billion on top |
| "Demand is strong across Combat" (October 2025) | Combat Systems revenue in the second quarter of 2026: **$2.290 billion versus $2.283 billion — up 0.3%.** Operating earnings **fell 1.9%**, margin down 30 basis points to 13.9% | **Diverges.** The order book is genuinely excellent; the profit-and-loss statement is flat |
| Technologies "positioned well for better revenue growth" (October 2025) | Technologies revenue +4.1% in the second quarter of 2026; margin **9.4% versus 9.6%** a year earlier | **Partially holds.** Growth did improve; profitability went backwards |

**The gross-margin question, resolved.** Our automated screen flags the gross margin as "compressing," down 1.67 percentage points over three years. The filed statements say something more precise: trailing gross margin is **15.38%, versus 15.40% a year ago — flat**, and the two most recent quarters (15.90% and 15.49%) both beat their year-ago comparables (15.49% and 14.94%). The three-year decline is real, but it is a **mix effect, not price erosion**: Marine Systems, which earns about 7 cents of operating profit per dollar of sales, has grown from roughly 28% to 33% of the company. Selling more of your lowest-margin business is not the same as losing pricing power — and the metric management is actually judged on, operating margin, is expanding.

**The load-bearing weakness this table exposes.** Aerospace and Marine together are 59% of revenue and produced **all** of the second quarter's earnings growth. Combat Systems and Technologies — the other 41% — grew revenue 0.3% and 4.1% and both saw margins fall year on year. Any bull case for General Dynamics is, arithmetically, a bet on two segments out of four.

**Two transcript errors worth flagging** so nobody builds a model on them. The July 2025 call records backlog as "$103.7 million"; it is $103.7 billion. The January 2026 call records fourth-quarter operating earnings as "$1.152 billion"; the filed figure is **$1.452 billion**. Both are transcription artefacts, not company misstatements.

## 3. Tone and confidence trajectory

**Rising — with a caution attached to the rising itself.**

- *July 2025:* "In my view, this was a wonderful quarter that exceeded our expectations."
- *October 2025:* "In short, we had a superb quarter from my perspective."
- *January 2026:* "Full-year numbers are absolutely terrific."
- *April 2026:* "this was a very powerful quarter in all respects."
- *July 2026:* "This quarter is even better in almost all respects... it was a superb quarter and first half."

Five consecutive escalations in the adjectives, delivered while the stock climbed to a 52-week high. Management has earned the language — the numbers back every one of those quarters — but superlative inflation is itself a data point about where expectations now sit. There is no remaining room for a "good but not great" quarter to be received well.

Guidance has become **more** specific over time, not less, which is the healthy direction. Capital-expenditure guidance moved from "a little over 2% of sales" (July 2025) to a hard "between 3.5% and 4% of sales" (April and July 2026). Cash-conversion guidance moved from "around 90%" to "100%" to "a little north of 100%, let's say, around 105%." Each revision was upward and each was met.

The one place the language turned **defensive** is capital returns. Compare the offensive framing of investment ("we have been investing in our business, and we'll continue to do so," January 2026) with the defensive framing of buybacks ("it behooves us to continue to be cautious," April 2026). Management is comfortable spending shareholder money on steel and tooling and visibly uncomfortable spending it on its own shares.

## 4. Guidance credibility

**High.** This is the strongest part of the file.

| Promised | Delivered |
|---|---|
| Full-year 2025 cash conversion "around 90%," raised from a lower original plan (July 2025) | Beaten; the 2026 plan opened at 100% |
| 2026 conversion "100% of net income" (April 2026) | Raised to "around 105%" three months later |
| "About 13 G800s for the year" (July 2025) | Deliveries began in the third quarter of 2025 as promised; 158 aircraft delivered in 2025, 22 more than 2024 |
| Aerospace margin above 15% "for sure in 2027" (July 2025) | Reached 15.0% in the first quarter of 2026 — a year early |
| Full-year 2026 earnings per share $16.45–$16.55 | Raised to **$16.80–$16.90**, with revenue held at about $55.7 billion and operating margin at 10.5%, and the Marine Systems revenue outlook lifted by almost $1.3 billion ([Investing.com transcript coverage, 29 July 2026](https://www.investing.com/news/transcripts/earnings-call-transcript-general-dynamics-tops-q2-2026-estimates-and-lifts-outlook-93CH-4820684)) |

Against Wall Street's estimates the company has beaten in **seven of the last seven quarters** — by $0.19 (second quarter 2025), $0.18 (third quarter 2025), $0.43 (first quarter 2026) and $0.28 (second quarter 2026). The beat sizes are *growing*, which is the classic signature of conservative internal planning — sandbagging, in the mild and defensible sense. That is a positive for reliability and a negative for surprise: the market now prices the beat.

The one genuinely admirable act of candour cuts the other way. In July 2025 management volunteered, unprompted, that aerospace margins would improve through 2027 "with degradation again in 2028, with the delivery of a significant number of G400s." Very few chief executives pre-announce a margin decline three years out. It is credible, and it tells you the 2027 aerospace margin is a **peak**, not a plateau.

## 5. Analyst pressure points

**Marine margins — asked five times, answered the same way five times.** Every call contains a version of "when do submarine margins improve?" and every answer contains the word "supply chain." October 2025: "To the extent that the supply chain stabilizes, I think that's where we will see meaningful margin expansion." The answer has not evolved in two years and the margin has moved 40 basis points. When Scott Mikus of Melius Research asked directly whether reaching a two-Virginia-plus-one-Columbia-per-year cadence would restore 8–9% margins, he received a description of the drivers rather than a commitment to the number.

**Production rates — refused outright.** April 2026, Danny Deep on Virginia-class output: "I won't get into the specific rates that we're currently producing at... I don't think it's prudent to get into specific rates over this call." The one quantified output measure the company has offered in two years is "submarine tonnage produced, and Electric Boat is up 13% over last year" (January 2026). Tonnage is a real metric; it is also not deliveries.

**Gulfstream programme timing — deflected for a full year, and it has already cost them.** In July 2025 Myles Walton of Wolfe Research pressed on the G400: "I had in my notes that certification was in 2026. Does that [slip] to the right?" The answer — "I don't think so... we've slowed down the 400 a bit" — was a slip in everything but name. A year later there is still no date, plus a **new** disclosure: the last G280 delivers in the second quarter of 2027 and its replacement, the G300, enters service "late '27, early '28," creating what management calls "a planned production break." Asked whether that gap creates factory-absorption pressure, the answer was a flat "No" backed by a partner agreement rather than by numbers. This is the least-well-supported claim in the file.

**What analysts are not pressing on, and should be.** Nobody on any of the five calls asked how the newly definitized Block VI and Build II submarine contracts allocate cost risk between the Navy and the shipbuilder. Management teased it in October 2025 — "once we sign them, we can and will be a little bit as we have been in the past, transparent about what the incentives and obligations are" — and the contracts have now been signed. The terms are the single most important undisclosed fact about the next five years of Marine profitability.

## 6. Red flags and green flags

**Concerning**

- **Two of four segments have stalled.** Combat Systems grew revenue 0.3% and saw earnings fall 1.9% in the second quarter of 2026; Technologies grew 4.1% with margins down 20 basis points. Together that is 41% of revenue contributing nothing to growth. The domestic driver is real: the M10 Booker programme was terminated and U.S. vehicle rates are declining, which management itself framed in April 2026 — "those rates won't replace what we had seen historically."
- **Capital spending is about to double.** Guidance is 3.5–4% of sales for 2026 against roughly 2% in 2025 — call it $1.95–2.23 billion against $1.16 billion. The first half spent only $437 million (1.6%), which means the **second half must absorb $1.5–1.8 billion**. Add a $500 million discretionary pension contribution and more than $500 million of back-weighted cash taxes, and the company's own guidance implies second-half free cash flow of roughly $1.2 billion against $3.6 billion in the first half.
- **Insiders are one-way sellers.** Zero open-market purchases against ten sales totalling 126,539 shares. Meanwhile the company itself declines to buy stock beyond covering compensation dilution. Neither the people who run it nor the treasury are buyers at these levels.
- **A pre-announced 2028 margin decline** in the segment currently carrying the company.
- **The Gulfstream production gap** between the final G280 in mid-2027 and the G300 in late 2027 or early 2028, dismissed rather than quantified.

**Positive**

- **Cash conversion above 150% in the first half of 2026**, with operating working capital down more than $1 billion since December — the hardest number in the file to fake.
- **The balance sheet is being paid down through a capital-spending ramp.** Net debt fell from $7.2 billion (mid-2025) to $4.4 billion (first quarter 2026) to $3.2 billion on the company's definition (second quarter 2026), and the August 2026 maturity will be **repaid with cash rather than refinanced**. Net interest expense guidance fell from $330 million to $270 million.
- **The order book is genuinely extraordinary** and is corroborated by named counterparties and dated awards, not by framework language: the German Eagle awards, the Canadian armoured combat support vehicles, the Norwegian and British bridges, and above all the 29 July 2026 submarine contracts running to 2035.
- **Aerospace margin improvement is arriving early**, and management attributes it to a specific, checkable mechanism — manufacturing hours coming down on the G700 and G800 quarter over quarter.
- **The G800 exceeded the aircraft it replaced.** April 2026: "it was better than the G650s that it replaced... quite remarkable given how recently G800s have entered into service." The G650 was the company's highest-margin product; replacing it without a margin hole was the central execution risk of the last two years, and they cleared it.
- **Candour about future bad news** — the 2028 margin warning and the 2027 production break were both volunteered.

## 7. Hidden signals

**A chief-executive succession is visibly under way and nobody has named it.** Danny Deep appears in July 2025 as "Executive Vice President, Global Operations," described by Phebe Novakovic as "not that far into his new position." By January 2026 his title is simply "President." In April 2026 he ran the entire call alone — Novakovic absent for a family illness — and delivered guidance, took every question, and raised the outlook. By July 2026 he is doing the segment commentary while she does the framing. Investors should assume the executive who will operate this company through the submarine ramp is Deep, and should note that his answers are consistently more operational and less quotable than hers.

**The vocabulary shifted from "recovery" to "throughput."** In 2025 the company talked about faults, rework, retrofits and flood damage at the NASSCO yard. By 2026 the words are throughput, earned hours, tonnage and learning curves. April 2026, Deep on Marine growth: "you should think about it as a story of throughput... both labor output, and so more earned hours as well as material." That is the language of a factory that has stopped fire-fighting.

**Management has started defending the business model against its own valuation framing.** When Kristine Liwag of Morgan Stanley suggested in July 2026 that Gulfstream could earn luxury-goods margins above 20%, Novakovic rejected the premise outright: "I'm going to quarrel with you on 1 word, luxury... These are not luxury yachts that sail around the Mediterranean." She was declining a multiple, not just a description. A chief executive who pushes back on the flattering comparison is usually telling you the margin has a ceiling.

**"We'll stick to our knitting."** October 2025, asked about expanding in Europe beyond ground vehicles: "I don't see getting out of our core... one of the things we have differentiated ourselves is having the discipline to stick with what we know." Read alongside the buyback caution and the capital-expenditure ramp, this is a company that has decided the next five years are about building what it already sells, not about acquiring or diversifying. For a defence conglomerate in a rearmament cycle that is a genuinely disciplined answer — and it also removes the acquisition-driven upside some peers will chase.

**Single-source supplier risk is being discussed openly, which means it is real.** April 2026, on whether the Columbia steam turbine could be dual-sourced: "some of the challenges with single-source suppliers, you can conclude which some of those are, that's an area that is very critical to the overall success of the submarine enterprise." The chief executive named supply-chain fragility as "the single largest impact on the cadence" of Columbia deliveries in October 2025. The order book is now locked to 2035; the supply chain that has to feed it is not.

## 8. Capital-allocation verdict

**Trajectory: strengthening.**

Revenue has compounded from $38.5 billion in 2021 to $54.9 billion over the last twelve months. Earnings per share have gone from $11.55 to $16.41. Free cash flow is running at $6.45 billion, an 11.8% margin. Return on equity is 17.4% and return on invested capital 10.9%, both rising. Net debt has fallen for four consecutive quarters while capital spending doubled. The backlog is at a record and about to jump again. Guidance has been raised, not defended. There is no financing-pattern red flag here of the sort that plagues this sector — no dilution, no convertible-note treadmill, no promoter language, no capacity announced that is not funded by a signed contract.

**The single thing the director must weigh before sizing a position: none of this is in dispute, and all of it is in the price.** The stock is at a 52-week high, 81% up its 52-week range, 9% above its 200-day average, and — critically — trades at a **premium to every defence prime on enterprise value to EBITDA** (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation): General Dynamics at roughly 16.7 times against Lockheed Martin at 15.5, L3Harris at 14.4, Huntington Ingalls at 14.1 and Northrop Grumman at 13.1. Two sell-side downgrades landed on 30 July 2026 on valuation grounds, and UBS's 3 August target of $395 sits barely 2% above the market. The forensic file is clean. The entry price is not.

**Economic moat: wide, and stable.** Two distinct sources. First, a **regulatory licence**: Electric Boat is one of only two shipyards in the United States permitted to build nuclear-powered submarines, the Columbia-class is effectively sole-sourced to it, and the barrier is not capital but decades of accumulated naval-nuclear qualification that no new entrant can assemble. Second, an **intangible brand plus scale-cost** position at Gulfstream, where a fully refreshed clean-sheet fleet has no direct equivalent — as Novakovic put it in January 2026, "Nobody else has anything like it... There's a lot of long thoughtful, targeted R&D and capital investments." The evidence supports calling this wide rather than merely narrow: returns on invested capital are **rising**, gross margin is flat rather than eroding, operating margin is expanding, and the company is winning multi-decade contracts on non-competed terms. It is not widening — 40 basis points of Marine margin progress in a year is not moat expansion — but it is not being eroded either.

**Secular threat: manageable.** The one structural question worth taking seriously is unmanned systems. The U.S. Army is, in management's own words, "in a period of transition"; the M10 Booker was cancelled; Stryker production rates are falling; and analysts are already asking about unmanned ground vehicles. Over ten to twenty years, autonomous platforms plausibly compress the addressable market for crewed armoured vehicles — which is most of Combat Systems, 17% of revenue. Against that, submarines are the least automatable platform in the defence inventory and their order book now runs to 2035, and business jets are not displaced by software. Artificial intelligence is a modest deflationary risk to the Technologies segment's government-services revenue, which is visible in the market already: Leidos, the closest comparable, trades at roughly 8 times EBITDA against General Dynamics' 16.7. So the terminal value is not impaired — but the cheap-looking parts of this company are the parts the market is right to discount, and the expensive-looking parts are carrying the whole story.
