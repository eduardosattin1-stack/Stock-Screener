# Forensic dossier — Woodward, Inc. (WWD)
**Prepared 2026-08-04. Price used: $375.89, US dollars, NASDAQ listing.**

Woodward makes the fuel systems, combustion controls and actuation hardware that sit inside
aircraft engines and industrial gas turbines. It is a component and service supplier, not an
engine maker: its customers are GE Aerospace, Pratt & Whitney, Airbus, Boeing, GE Vernova,
Baker Hughes, Mitsubishi and Rolls-Royce's mtu power-systems arm. Roughly 61% of revenue is
Aerospace and 39% Industrial. This review covers eight consecutive quarterly earnings calls,
from the April 2025 call through the July 2026 call, checked line by line against the
reported financial statements.

---

## 1. NARRATIVE ARC

The story has changed character twice in eight quarters, and both changes were upward.

**Phase one — defensive (April 2025).** The April 2025 call was an exercise in damage control
dressed as confidence. Management led with what it had to exclude: sales grew 6%, but
"excluding China on-highway, our company posted revenue up 12%." Half the prepared remarks
were about tariffs, retaliatory trade actions, Chinese airlines cutting spare-parts orders,
and a $10 million to $15 million tariff cost bucket. Guidance was "reaffirming the top end"
and merely "pulling up the bottom end" — the language of a management team protecting a
number rather than chasing one.

**Phase two — the strategic build-out (July 2025 to November 2025).** The tone flipped
decisively at the July 2025 call, and the reason was content wins rather than cycle. Woodward
announced it had been selected for the Airbus A350 wing spoiler actuators — its first
actuation unit on a primary flight control surface of a commercial aircraft — and closed the
purchase of Safran's North American electromechanical actuation business, which brought
horizontal stabilizer trim actuators on the A350, Embraer E175/E190-E2, Gulfstream and Dassault
platforms. By November 2025 management quantified the prize: total A350 content per aircraft
rises to roughly $550,000 once spoiler shipments begin, currently scheduled for late calendar
2028. Two construction projects were named and dated — Spartanburg, South Carolina (broke
ground November 2025, operational 2027) and the Glatten, Germany fuel-injection expansion for
data-centre backup power.

**Phase three — the demand shock (February 2026 to July 2026).** From the February 2026 call
the constraint stopped being demand and became Woodward's own ability to build. First-quarter
fiscal 2026 revenue grew 29% and earnings per share 54%. By the April 2026 call, quarterly
sales passed $1 billion for the first time in company history. By the July 2026 call the
chief executive was describing a queue: "sequentially, numerous customers have come to us and
continue to increase their forecast for 2027, '28 and beyond into the early 2030s. And in
order to — if all those cases are true, we'll have to expand capacity."

**What was quietly dropped.** Three things vanished from the narrative, and each vanished for
a good reason. China on-highway heavy-truck products — a chronic drag cited in every 2025 call
— were formally put into wind-down in February 2026. The tariff discussion, which dominated
April 2025, disappeared entirely by 2026. And the "soft commercial services" warning that
management issued repeatedly through 2025 simply never happened: commercial services grew 24%
in the June 2026 quarter.

**What was added.** Two new threads. Automation became a headline pillar by July 2026, with a
specific target: "a reduction of approximately 1,000 incremental hires by 2029... about 50% of
our projected hiring needs in that time frame." And data-centre power moved from a passing
mention in April 2025 to the single most-discussed industrial driver by July 2026.

**Where the narrative is richer than the numbers: the buyback.** This is the single most
important forensic finding in the file, and management did not address it. Over the last four
reported quarters Woodward spent **$602 million repurchasing its own shares** — roughly 2.7% of
its market value. Over that same span the weighted-average basic share count went from 59.442
million (June-2025 quarter) to 59.632 million (June-2026 quarter), an **increase** of 0.3%, and
diluted shares fell only from 61.374 million to 61.317 million, a decrease of 0.09%. In other
words, more than $600 million of cash bought shareholders essentially no reduction in their
share of the company. Economically this is not a return of capital; it is the funding of
employee stock compensation. Option exercises brought in $55 million of proceeds over the nine
months, and the company is buying the offsetting shares back at 40 times earnings.

**Where the narrative is richer than the numbers: cash conversion.** Management raised the
earnings guidance three times in fiscal 2026 and left free-cash-flow guidance unchanged. The
chief financial officer was candid about why when pressed on 2026-07-29: inventory efficiency
targets were abandoned because "with the demand growth we were seeing, we just weren't willing
to take" the supply risk. The result is that trailing free cash flow of $377 million converts
only 68% of $552 million of trailing net income, and receivables sat at $1,056 million against
quarterly revenue of $1,110 million — 92 days of sales outstanding.

**Where the narrative is poorer than the numbers (in Woodward's favour).** Management has
consistently guided commercial aftermarket down and been wrong. In April 2025 they told
investors to expect "a slower commercial services growth rate in the second half." In July 2025
the chief executive admitted "the softening in services we had anticipated in our plans and
guidance hasn't materialized." By July 2026 commercial services was still growing 24%. That is
sandbagging, and it is the good kind — but it means the current 8% company-wide price
realization is being extrapolated by the market into a future management itself does not
forecast.

## 3. TONE & CONFIDENCE TRAJECTORY

**Rising, and steeply.** The trajectory is unusually clean to trace.

- *April 2025 — defensive.* "We are putting actions in place to mitigate tariff impacts as well
  as manage impacts from a slight economic downturn." Guidance framed by what could go wrong:
  "the low end of the aerospace sales range assumes current level headwinds in supplier
  performance, Boeing rate break delays or moderate Woodward inventory destocking."
- *July 2025 — turning offensive.* Two strategic announcements in one call, and a first
  admission of over-caution: "the softening in services we had anticipated... hasn't
  materialized."
- *November 2025 — confident but hedged.* "2025 was another remarkable year." Still careful on
  margin: "we intend to expand margins, and that's what you see a guide there that allows for
  some headwinds to get in the way of intent."
- *February 2026 — assertive.* "2026 is off to an exceptional start." When an analyst probed
  whether guidance was conservative, the chief executive did not deflect: "we have plenty of
  orders. To achieve the high end of the guide. It's really a question of can we and our supply
  chain deliver that much output."
- *April 2026 — confident with a new caveat.* First mention of Middle East geopolitics and
  employee safety, but explicitly deferred: "If those impacts do occur, we expect them to be
  felt in fiscal 2027."
- *July 2026 — assured, almost relaxed.* "In the many years I've attended the show, I can't
  remember another time when the industry remained so calm and collected during a period of
  such significant growth."

Guidance has become *more* specific over time, not less — a reliable marker of genuine
confidence. Compare April 2025's ranges-with-caveats to July 2026's segment-by-segment
precision: Aerospace growth 21-23% at approximately 23.5% margins, Industrial 19-21% at
approximately 19%, tax 22.5%, capital spending $290 million, shareholder returns approximately
$700 million.

The one place tone runs ahead of disclosure is capacity. Management has now twice deferred the
central question — how much capital the data-centre power opportunity will absorb — to a future
Investor Day, most recently to 4 March 2027.

## 4. GUIDANCE CREDIBILITY

**High.** This is the strongest part of the file.

Track the fiscal-2026 earnings guide across four calls: the year opened around $8.35-8.65,
was raised in February 2026, raised again in April 2026 to roughly $9.15-9.45, and raised again
on 2026-07-29 to $9.30-9.50. Three raises, no cuts, no restatements, no missed quarters. The
screener records seven beats in seven quarters. The June-2026 quarter delivered $2.52 adjusted
against a $2.39 consensus.

Specific promises made and kept:
- The Glatten, Germany expansion was promised in July 2025 as "more than halfway through...
  tracking ahead of schedule," reaffirmed in November 2025 as "on track to become operational by
  mid-2026," and inaugurated on 21 July 2026 — described by the chief executive as "on schedule,
  on budget and without disrupting customer deliveries."
- The China on-highway wind-down was announced February 2026 with a $20-25 million restructuring
  cost estimate and completion by fiscal year end; the July 2026 call confirms it finishing on
  schedule.
- The Spartanburg facility broke ground November 2025 and was described in April 2026 as "walls
  are erected and floors are being poured," on target for 2027.

**The sandbagging pattern is real but benign.** It runs in one direction — commercial aftermarket
is repeatedly guided down and repeatedly beats. Investors should not, however, read that as a
promise. The chief financial officer explicitly told the market on 2026-07-29 that the price
lever is closing: "as we look at going forward that I would expect price between 3% and 5% would
be a more normal rate," against roughly 8% for fiscal 2026 and 10% in the June quarter. That is
management removing its own biggest earnings driver from the forward model.

**Reliability: High.**

## 5. ANALYST PRESSURE POINTS

Four questions come up call after call, and the quality of the answers varies sharply.

**1. Commercial aftermarket durability — answered well, but the risk is real.** Scott Mikus of
Melius asked directly on 2026-07-29 whether airlines are "piling up excess inventory on the
legacy platforms" and whether that creates destocking in fiscal 2027. Management's answer was
specific and checkable: monthly shop inputs by unit type (V2500 fuel control, CFM56 hydro-
mechanical unit, GEnx, GE90) show "normal variation in noise, not anything that would say
there's a trend." That is a good answer. But the chief executive himself flagged the mechanism
in July 2025: "once airplanes start to get parked out from the legacy fleet, more used
serviceable material will be available, and while it could really, I think, eat into that
business pretty quickly."

**2. Free cash flow versus earnings — deflected twice.** David Strauss of Wells Fargo pressed on
2026-07-29 on why three earnings raises produced no cash-flow raise. The answer — inventory
built deliberately to protect delivery — is honest, but it means the gap persists indefinitely.

**3. Industrial margin sustainability — walked back within the same call.** Louis Raffetto of
Wolfe asked whether the 21.2% core Industrial margin was clean. The chief financial officer
conceded favourable business-mix and guided back to first-half levels next quarter. Investors
extrapolating 21% Industrial margins are extrapolating a mix quarter.

**4. Data-centre capacity — deferred, twice.** The most consequential capital-allocation
question in the company has now been pushed from the April 2026 call to the July 2026 call to
the March 2027 Investor Day.

**What analysts are not asking.** Nobody on any of these eight calls asked why $602 million of
repurchases produced no reduction in share count, or what average price the company paid while
the stock ran to a 52-week high of $450.92.

## 6. RED FLAGS / GREEN FLAGS

**Concerning**

- **The buyback that does not shrink the company (four quarters to June 2026).** $602 million
  spent; diluted share count down 0.09%. Funded, in effect, with debt: net debt rose from roughly
  $391 million at the September-2025 year end to $885 million at 30 June 2026 while the shares
  traded between roughly $300 and $451.
- **Price is doing the heavy lifting, and management says it is ending (2026-07-29).** Company-wide
  price realization is about 8% for fiscal 2026 and was 10% in the June quarter; the chief
  financial officer guided to 3-5% "more normal" thereafter, because "we've really made it
  through... all of our LTA agreements for the first round that we closed out sort of post 2022."
  Roughly 8 of the 21 percentage points of current growth is a lever with a stated expiry.
- **Two one-time items flattered the June-2026 quarter.** A retroactive contract price true-up
  added about 100 basis points to Aerospace margin, and the dying China on-highway line added
  about 90 basis points to Industrial margin. Both disappear.
- **Free cash flow guidance unchanged through three earnings raises.** Capital spending is
  guided to $290 million for fiscal 2026 against $156 million spent in nine months — the fourth
  quarter alone must nearly match the first three combined.
- **Insider selling is one-sided.** Zero open-market purchases against 62 disposals covering
  47,254 shares.
- **A named, forecast supply constraint (2026-07-29).** "Rare earth metals as we look forward.
  We haven't had a big problem yet with those, but that's one that we all forecast coming to be
  a big challenge within the next few quarters."
- **Revenue quality on the defense line.** Defense original-equipment sales fell 6% in the June
  quarter on "a one-time revenue recognition adjustment" that was not quantified.

**Positive**

- **Gross margin expanding while revenue grows 21% (June-2026 quarter).** 31.5% against 27.2%
  a year earlier. In a supply-constrained niche this is the clean signature of pricing power,
  not of volume bought with discounting.
- **Portfolio pruning with follow-through (February 2025 through July 2026).** Greenville sold,
  Niles pilot controls sold to Ontic, small-engine lines exited, China on-highway wound down,
  the acquired Canadian electronics plant consolidated into Poland and Bulgaria. Each was
  announced with a cost and a date, and each has landed.
- **Content wins with durable service tails (July 2025, November 2025, March 2026).** The A350
  spoiler award, the Safran electromechanical actuation purchase, and the Valve Research &
  Manufacturing solenoid acquisition together take A350 content to roughly $550,000 per aircraft
  and position the company for the next single-aisle competition.
- **A service-network strategy that avoids capital (2026-07-21).** The HAECO Elite Licensed
  Repair Service Facility agreement is the third such licence; Woodward supplies materials, work
  scopes and technical approach and shares the economics without funding the shops.
- **The installed-base multiplier is arithmetic, not hope.** Service content on LEAP and geared-
  turbofan units runs roughly five times the legacy generation, and management confirmed on
  2026-07-29 that the crossover from legacy to new-generation repair revenue is on track.

## 7. HIDDEN SIGNALS

**A vocabulary shift from "capacity" to "allocation."** Through 2025 the language was about
building capacity Woodward already knew it needed. By July 2026 it is about rationing capital
against demand it cannot yet underwrite: "trying to make sure that we understand it, and we'll
make a prudent allocation of capital to capture this fairly unique opportunity." That is a more
disciplined posture than an equipment maker in a boom usually adopts, and it is the single most
encouraging sentence in the file for anyone worried about a capital-cycle blow-off.

**The word "turnaround" appeared, then was owned.** In April 2026 the chief executive corrected
himself mid-sentence about the Industrial segment: "I use the word turnaround. I've not really
used that, but I think it's an apt description." Six quarters earlier Industrial was described
only as "mixed." Management has stopped defending that segment and started rebuilding it.

**Innings language moved.** The same executive tracked Industrial from "early innings" (2025) to
"middle innings" (April 2026) — a self-imposed clock.

**How competition is discussed has changed.** In 2025 Woodward positioned itself as a follower of
engine-maker growth: asked whether investors should benchmark it against GE Vernova and Rolls-
Royce, the answer was "broadly speaking, we see the same kind of growth they do." By July 2026
the framing is that customers are queuing at Woodward's door with raised forecasts. Whether that
is a genuine bargaining-power shift or the normal top-of-cycle sensation is the central open
question in this file.

**A margin guide that keeps being beaten from below.** Management set a "mid-teens" Industrial
margin target at the December 2023 Investor Day and printed 21.2% core in the June-2026 quarter.
The chief executive's own framing — "we've been demonstrating better than that fairly
consistently" — is a tell that the next Investor Day resets targets upward, which is the
mechanism most likely to move the shares.

**No management turnover.** The same chief executive, chief financial officer and investor-
relations director run all eight calls. There is no new voice saying anything different, which
removes a common source of narrative discontinuity — and also removes a fresh set of eyes.

## 8. CAPITAL-ALLOCATION VERDICT (for the director)

**Trajectory: STRENGTHENING.** Revenue grew 21% in the June-2026 quarter with gross margin up
430 basis points, return on invested capital has climbed to roughly 11.9% on a trailing basis,
return on equity to 21.7%, and every dated operational promise in these eight calls — Glatten,
Spartanburg, the China wind-down, the Safran integration — has landed on schedule. Guidance was
raised three times in one fiscal year with no offsetting cut. This is a genuinely well-run
industrial franchise operating into two simultaneous demand waves: the aircraft-engine
maintenance cycle and grid and data-centre power.

**Two proxy figures in our own screener disagree with the company's reported numbers, and the
company is right.** First, our scan carries gross margin at 26.8% and net debt at $395 million
— both are fiscal-2025 annual figures. The current reality from the 30 June 2026 balance sheet
and income statement is gross margin 31.5% in the quarter (29.5% trailing) and net debt of
$885 million including $18 million of capitalised leases. Second, our leverage proxy of 0.86
times earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation is a net-debt measure; the
company reports 1.6 times on a gross-debt basis. Both are defensible; the gap is definitional,
not a discrepancy. The material point is the direction: net debt more than doubled in nine
months.

**The single most important thing to weigh before sizing.** Woodward's earnings are currently
being produced by a pricing lever that management has publicly told the market is closing —
about 8% company-wide price in fiscal 2026, 10% in the June quarter, guided to 3-5% thereafter.
At the same time three other supports come out of the model simultaneously in fiscal 2027: the
China on-highway line disappears (roughly $130 million of revenue that was margin-accretive by
about 90 basis points in the June quarter), the one-time retroactive contract price true-up does
not repeat (about 100 basis points of Aerospace margin), and capital spending steps up to $290
million and beyond as the data-centre capacity decision is made. The business is excellent. The
question is whether the market is paying for a 21%-growth, 430-basis-point-expansion company
when fiscal 2027 is likely to be a high-single-digit-growth, modest-expansion company. At
roughly 27.6 times trailing enterprise value to earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and
amortisation, 40 times trailing earnings and a free-cash-flow yield of 1.7%, it is.

**Economic moat: narrow, and widening.** The moat type is a combination of switching costs and
regulatory licence. Woodward's units are certified, sole-source flight-critical hardware; once a
fuel metering unit or actuator is qualified on an engine programme it stays there for the life
of the platform, and the aftermarket that follows is protected by the same certification. The
evidence supports "widening" rather than "wide": gross margin is expanding, return on invested
capital is rising from a low base (roughly 7.8% on the fiscal-2025 annual measure to 11.9%
trailing), and content per aircraft is going up, not down — A350 shipset value rising to about
$550,000. But 11.9% return on invested capital is not yet wide-moat territory, and the moat is
one rung below the engine makers who own the platform specification. Woodward is a price-taker
on which engine wins; the chief executive said so plainly in July 2025.

**Secular threat: manageable.** The obvious long-horizon risks — sustainable aviation fuel,
hybrid-electric propulsion, grid decarbonisation — all still require fuel metering, combustion
control and actuation, and several increase content rather than removing it (more than half of
new ship orders in the June-2025 quarter specified alternative fuels, which pulls Woodward
hardware). The real near-term risks in this file are cyclical, not structural: a legacy-fleet
retirement wave releasing used serviceable material into the aftermarket, an airframer build-rate
stall, and a data-centre power order book that proves to be forecast rather than contract.
