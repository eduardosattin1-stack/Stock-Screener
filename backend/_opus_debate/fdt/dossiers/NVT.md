# Forensic dossier — nVent Electric plc (NVT)

**Prepared 2026-08-04.** Price used throughout: **$163.21 per share (US dollars, NYSE)**, the live quote at the time of writing. Market value about $26.4 billion; enterprise value (market value plus net debt) about $27.8 billion.

Sources: six consecutive earnings-call transcripts (2025-05-02 through 2026-05-01) supplied in the input bundle, plus the second-quarter 2026 call of 2026-07-31 and the accompanying filing, which I pulled separately because the bundle stops one quarter short of today. Quarterly income statements as filed. All quotes below carry their quarter.

## 1. Narrative arc

The story in this company changed shape twice in six quarters, and both changes were announced in advance rather than discovered afterwards.

**Phase one — "portfolio transformation" (first half of 2025).** The opening framing was a housekeeping story: sell the thermal-management business, buy Trachte and the Avail Electrical Products Group, and become "a more focused, higher-growth electrical company" (first quarter 2025). Growth at the time was ordinary — organic sales, meaning sales excluding acquisitions and currency, grew just 2% in the first quarter of 2025. Management was selling a *mix shift*, not a demand story. Infrastructure — the vertical that contains data centres and power utilities — was 12% of sales when the company was spun out and 45% by the end of 2025.

**Phase two — the AI data-centre wave takes over (from the third quarter of 2025).** The pivot is visible in one sentence. Third quarter 2025: "Organic orders were up approximately 65% primarily driven by large orders for the AI data center buildout." From that call onward the acquisitions stop being the headline and become supporting cast. By the first quarter of 2026 infrastructure was "over 55%" of sales; by the second quarter of 2026 it was "nearly 60% in the first half," and data-centre sales are now guided to **$2 billion in 2026** against roughly $1 billion in 2025 and $600 million in 2024 — a figure management volunteered on the fourth-quarter 2025 call ("In 2024, it was $600 million") and re-stated on 2026-07-31.

**Phase three — capacity, and only capacity (2026).** Every call in 2026 is organised around physical bottleneck relief: the Blaine, Minnesota liquid-cooling plant opened in the first quarter of 2026, "effectively doubling our capacity"; on 2026-07-31 a third plant, "Blaine 2," of similar size, was announced for the first half of 2027. Capital spending is guided to roughly $130 million this year, up about 40%.

**What was quietly dropped.** Two things. First, the *acquisition flywheel* has gone from the centre of the story to a routine answer at the back of the call — the last deal closed in 2025, and every question since has been answered with "we have a good pipeline" and nothing more. Second, and more interesting, **backlog growth stopped being the headline in the second quarter of 2026** — because it stopped growing. More on that below.

## 3. Tone and confidence trajectory

**Rising, steeply, and it is earned rather than asserted.**

- *First quarter 2025* — hedged and operational: "While there remains uncertainty given the dynamic environment…" and a candid admission that first-quarter margin fell, with the chief financial officer conceding "that net productivity bar was down $70 million."
- *Second quarter 2025* — the first superlative: "outstanding second quarter results," and backlog "increasing more than fourfold what it was a year ago."
- *Third quarter 2025* — the inflection: "For the first time, quarterly sales were more than $1 billion."
- *Fourth quarter 2025 / first quarter 2026* — confident but still specific: hard figures attached to every claim (data-centre sales of about $1 billion; backlog $2.3 billion then $2.6 billion; infrastructure at 45% then 55% of sales).
- *Second quarter 2026* — the most confident call of the set, and the only one with a mild rhetorical flourish: "Our future is bright."

Crucially, guidance has become **more** specific over time, not less — the opposite of the pattern that usually precedes a disappointment. The company now gives a quarterly earnings range, a full-year range, an organic growth range, a tariff-cost figure, a capital-spending figure and a cash-conversion figure on every call.

There is **no shift from offensive to defensive language.** The vocabulary is investment, capacity, new products and hiring throughout. The only defensive note is on cost — copper, fuel and tariffs — and it is handled numerically rather than rhetorically.

## 4. Guidance credibility

**High.** This is the cleanest section of the file.

| Guidance given | Later delivered |
|---|---|
| Q1 2025: full-year 2025 organic growth 5-7%, adjusted earnings per share $3.03-3.13 | Raised to 8-10% (Q2 2025), then 10-11% (Q3 2025). Actual full-year 2025: 13% organic, adjusted earnings per share up 35% |
| Q1 2025: Avail acquisition worth "a nickel" of earnings per share | Q3 2025: "it will be approximately a $0.10 impact to EPS, higher than the $0.05 that we originally quoted." Two analysts (Wolfe, Goldman) told them on the Q1 call the nickel looked too low — **the analysts were right, and management conceded it within two quarters** |
| Q4 2025: full-year 2026 reported sales growth 15-18%, adjusted earnings per share growth 20-24% | Q2 2026: raised to 37-39% reported sales growth and $5.00-5.10 adjusted earnings per share (against original guidance of $4.45-4.55), roughly 50% growth |
| Tariff cost: $120m (Q1 2025) → $90m (Q2 2025) → held at $90m (Q3, Q4) | Full-year 2025 actual: "approximately $90 million." **Guided high, landed on it** |

Six consecutive quarters of beating the earnings guidance range and raising the year. There is a mild **sandbagging pattern** — the initial nickel on Avail, the initial $120m tariff figure, and the current second-half guide (see below) — but it is conservatism, not evasion, and management admits it in plain language: "it is important that we are prudent in our guidance. And we will continue to be that way" (chief financial officer, second quarter 2026).

**One thing the reader should size independently.** The raised full-year 2026 guide of $5.35-5.42 billion implies second-half revenue of roughly $2.64-2.71 billion against $2.71 billion actually delivered in the first half. The third-quarter guide (32-35% organic) implies roughly $1.40 billion, which leaves roughly **$1.24-1.31 billion for the fourth quarter — a 10%-plus sequential decline.** Jeffrey Sprague of Vertical Research put exactly this to them on 2026-07-31: "the guide is for a little bit down revenue sequentially in Q3 and more so in Q4." The answer was "this is just our planning, and we are being very prudent." On this management's six-quarter record, prudence is the likelier explanation than a real cliff — but it is unproven, and it is the number to watch on the October call.

**Reliability score: High.**

## 5. Analyst pressure points

Three questions recur, and only one is genuinely deflected.

**1. Orders and backlog lumpiness — asked every single quarter, and increasingly pointed.** Nigel Coe of Wolfe Research opened the second-quarter 2026 call with "I hate to be the annoying analyst asking the question about orders." Nicole DeBlase of Deutsche Bank and Joe Ritchie of Goldman Sachs both asked whether the backlog grows from here. The answers are honest but circular: orders are "large and lumpy," backlog is "around the right level," and "we are off to a really strong start here in Q3 on the order front."

This is the file's most important tension. Organic order growth ran **+65% (Q3 2025) → +30% (Q4 2025) → +40% (Q1 2026) → +low double digits (Q2 2026)**. Backlog rose from about $0.77 billion to $2.3 billion to $2.6 billion — and then **fell** to $2.5 billion. Management's reading is that they executed the backlog down on purpose to protect customer lead times, which the 53% revenue growth genuinely supports. The bear's reading is that the order rate has already rolled over and one quarter of shipment strength masked it. **Both readings fit the same data. Nobody can settle it until the October print.**

**2. Electrical Connections margin — pushed on twice and, to management's credit, answered with a number that then came true.** That segment's return on sales fell 390 basis points year over year in the first quarter of 2026 on "higher-than-expected raw material inflation." Management said margins would recover to the high twenties. In the second quarter they came in at 27.3% — recovered sequentially, still 140 basis points below the prior year. Half-answered, honestly framed.

**3. The genuinely deflected question: what happens after 2027.** Brian Drab of William Blair asked it directly on 2026-07-31 — "is 2026 and 27 gonna be great? And what happens in the out years?" The answer moved immediately from the data-centre build-out to the replacement cycle: liquid cooling is "maybe 10% to 15% of cooling in data centers," and "maybe the build out of data centers at some point down the road slows but that white space and that refresh cycle… are going to continue to expand." That is a plausible argument, not evidence. It is the one place where the story runs ahead of anything verifiable.

**What analysts are not asking that they should:** customer concentration. Not one analyst across six calls asked what share of data-centre revenue comes from the largest customer, and management has never volunteered it. Customers are named only by category — "hyperscalers to neo clouds and multi-tenants" — with NVIDIA mentioned once, as a roadmap collaborator through 2030, not as a contracted counterparty.

## 6. Red flags and green flags

🟢 **Operating margin expanding while revenue grows 53%.** Second quarter 2026: return on sales up 110 basis points on an adjusted basis, 417 on the reported basis, with the Systems Protection segment up 150 basis points to 23.2%. In a supply-constrained niche this is the honest signature of pricing power; the alternative — buying volume with price — would show margins falling as revenue rose. It is not happening here.

🟢 **Capital spending is genuinely light for the growth being funded.** Roughly $130 million of capital expenditure on about $5.4 billion of guided revenue — 2.4% of sales. The new Minnesota capacity is **leased and stood up in about 100 working days from lease signature.** That is the most important fact in this file for anyone worried about an AI-capex digestion: if demand pauses, there is very little stranded concrete to write down.

🟢 **Buyback timing was right, not reflexive.** More than $250 million repurchased in the first half of 2025, when the shares traded in the $60-80 area; only $50 million in the first half of 2026 with the shares between roughly $130 and $180. Net leverage fell from post-acquisition levels to 1.2 times, well below the company's own 2.0-2.5 times target range. Management chose deleveraging over buying its own stock at the high. That is unusual and it counts.

🟢 **Guidance conceded to analysts when analysts were right** (the Avail nickel became a dime).

🟢 **Backlog disclosed against itself.** Management volunteered that backlog fell, that it is "mostly 12 months or less," and — asked directly by Vlad Bystricky of Citigroup whether customers were locking in longer — answered "that has not extended out." A promoter would have quoted a multi-year figure. This one did the opposite.

🔴 **The order rate decelerated hard and the book-to-bill went below 1.0.** Implied orders of about $1.37 billion against $1.47 billion of revenue. One quarter is not a trend, and management says the third quarter started strong — but it is the first such reading of the cycle and it is the number that breaks the thesis if it repeats.

🔴 **Backlog covers less than six months of revenue.** $2.5 billion against roughly $5.4 billion of 2026 revenue, and explicitly "mostly 12 months or less." The word "visibility" is doing more work in the prepared remarks than the underlying coverage supports. For contrast, Vertiv — the nearest listed comparison in data-centre thermal and power — carries a reported order backlog several times larger relative to its revenue.

🔴 **Gross margin has not recovered.** 37.9% in the second quarter of 2026 versus 38.6% a year earlier and roughly 40% in 2023-24. The screener proxy in our own input bundle labels this trajectory "expanding, +1.89 percentage points over three years," and **the sign is inverted** — its own underlying series runs 37.74% (oldest) to 35.85% (most recent), a *contraction* of 1.89 points. Do not rely on that field for this name.

🔴 **Insider selling with no offsetting buying.** Zero open-market purchases against eleven sales totalling 95,282 shares. On a stock that roughly doubled in a year this is unsurprising, but zero buys is still zero buys, and it is the only signal in the file that runs against management's stated confidence.

🔴 **No named contracted counterparty anywhere.** Every customer reference is a category. There is no dated, named, firm order in the public record — only a backlog aggregate and a roadmap collaboration with NVIDIA.

🔴 **Liquid cooling is attracting entrants and management says so.** First quarter 2026: "the space is growing so significantly that it is not a surprise that there would be a lot more entrants into the space." The defence offered — application expertise, a decade of experience, the largest installed base — is a real but narrow moat, not a structural one.

**Kill-list tests, explicitly:** *Serial diluter?* **No** — share count is falling (164.1 million diluted, down from 167.3 million in early 2025), and every acquisition was funded with cash and debt, with management stating deals "certainly can be managed without any additional equity." *Capacity or backlog inflation?* **No** — backlog is disclosed with its decline and its duration limit, and capacity announcements come with square footage, a location and a dated opening. *Promoter language?* **Minimal** — "our future is bright" is the strongest phrase in six calls; everything else is arithmetic.

## 7. Hidden signals

**A vocabulary shift worth noticing.** Through 2025 the growth engine is described as *acquisitions* and *portfolio transformation*. From the first quarter of 2026 the vocabulary is *gray space and white space* — industry terms for the building shell and electrical infrastructure versus the server hall itself. That shift matters commercially: it means the company is now selling into two separate budget lines within the same data-centre project, which is a wider share of wallet than enclosures alone.

**New products stopped being a rounding error.** New-product contribution to sales growth: "over two points" (Q1 2025) → over 3 points (Q2 2025) → over 5 points (Q3 2025) → about 10 points for full-year 2025 → **over 20 points (Q1 2026) → over 30 points (Q2 2026)**. Either the internal definition of "new product" is loose, or the research pipeline is now the dominant growth driver. Management has never defined the term on a call, and no analyst has asked. That is a disclosure gap, though the aggregate revenue growth means it cannot be hiding much.

**The new voices say the same things as the old ones.** Gary Corona joined as chief financial officer in the first quarter of 2025 from Medtronic and General Mills — a consumer-and-devices background, not an electricals one. He has not changed the accounting presentation, the segment definitions or the guidance philosophy, and he has been consistently the more conservative of the two speakers ("it is important that we are prudent"). In June 2026 the company added a chief strategy officer and a chief revenue officer, both reporting to the chair and chief executive — a build-out of the commercial organisation consistent with the stated Europe expansion, not a shake-up.

**Two admissions against interest.** First, on 800-volt direct-current architectures — the next data-centre power standard — management placed itself outside the core opportunity: "there are others who are more, you know, power players, so to speak." That is honest scoping of a limitation. Second, asked whether Blaine 2 could be opened in a hundred days like Blaine 1: "I think that is too aggressive. I mean, we are, like, running flat out." Managements under pressure to sustain a narrative do not usually decline a free superlative.

**The distributor channel check.** Asked whether short-cycle strength was inventory restocking rather than real demand, management answered that sell-in and sell-through are "well balanced" — the correct answer, and one that can be falsified later if channel inventory shows up.

## 8. Capital-allocation verdict (for the director)

**Trajectory: strengthening.** Six straight quarters of beating and raising, an operating margin that expands while revenue grows more than 50%, net leverage cut to 1.2 times, capital spending held to 2.4% of sales while doubling physical capacity, and a buyback that was heavy when the stock was cheap and light when it was expensive. On execution this is close to the best file I have read in this chain.

**The single thing to weigh before sizing.** *Not* whether the business is good — it demonstrably is. It is that **the order rate has already decelerated from roughly 40% to low double digits and the book-to-bill has gone below 1.0, while the shares have roughly doubled in a year and now trade at about 32 times this year's guided earnings.** Every dollar of the recent re-rating assumes the 2026 growth rate persists. The backlog covers less than six months and does not extend. That combination — a spent multiple against a leading indicator that has already turned — is the risk, and it is a *valuation* risk, not a quality risk. Size it as an entry-price problem.

**Economic moat: narrow.** The type is **switching costs plus scale-cost**, not brand or network. The switching-cost element is real and specific: liquid-cooling distribution units are qualified into hyperscaler and chip-vendor roadmaps years ahead ("working with NVIDIA and others on their road maps out through 2030"), and requalifying a thermal loop mid-programme is expensive. The scale-cost element is the ability to stand up 160,000 square feet of qualified capacity in a hundred days next to an existing campus, sharing labour and supply base — a genuine advantage over a new entrant, but a lead measured in quarters, not decades. Around this sits a very ordinary short-cycle electrical business (cable management, grounding, fastening) whose moat is distributor relationships.

**Width judged on the evidence, not the story.** Return on invested capital is roughly 10.7% and return on equity roughly 15.8% on a trailing basis — respectable, not fortress-like, and depressed by about $6 billion of acquisition goodwill and intangibles (62.5% of total assets). Return on *tangible* assets is 22.3%. The direction is the point: operating margin is rising (up 110 basis points adjusted, 417 reported in the latest quarter) and returns are rising with volume, but **gross margin is not** — it sits roughly 2 to 4 points below the 2023-24 level. So the moat is **stable, not widening**: the company is earning more because it is bigger, not because it can charge more per unit. Management's own acknowledgement of "a lot more entrants" in liquid cooling is the reason to keep the label narrow.

**Secular threat: manageable, and directionally favourable.** No structural force is eroding the terminal value here — this company sits on the winning side of electrification rather than the losing side of it, and the risk of artificial intelligence displacing its product is nil (it sells the physical hardware that artificial intelligence requires). The real threat is **cyclical, not secular**: a digestion phase in hyperscaler capital spending. That would be painful for the multiple and largely harmless to the asset base, because the capacity is leased and the capital intensity is low. There is one genuine secular-adjacent risk worth naming — the shift to 800-volt direct-current data-centre architecture, where management explicitly positions itself as a follower rather than a power player. That could cap content per megawatt on the power side over five years, but it does not touch the cooling franchise, which is where the growth is.

**Cheapness assessment: neither. This is not a fear-print and it is not a value trap — it is a good business at a full price.** The multiple has expanded with the earnings, so there is no mispricing to harvest. The question for the director is entry level, and only entry level.
