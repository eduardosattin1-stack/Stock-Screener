# Forensic Dossier — Teradyne, Inc. (TER)
**Prepared 2026-08-04 · price used $404.86 (NASDAQ, US dollars) · six consecutive earnings calls reviewed, 2025-04-29 through 2026-07-29**

Teradyne builds the machines that test semiconductors before they ship, plus the collaborative robot arms (Universal Robots) and autonomous mobile robots (MiR) that move parts around factories. Over the six quarters reviewed the company went from a business apologising for lost visibility to one printing back-to-back record quarters, with revenue in the June 2026 quarter of $1.33 billion, up 104% from a year earlier. This dossier tests whether the story management tells is supported by the numbers, and where it is not.

---

## 1. NARRATIVE ARC

**April 2025 — the story is defence.** The first call in the window is a company with the lights dimmed. Chief executive Greg Smith opens by conceding that "the combination of trade policy and our customers' heightened uncertainty around end market demand has caused orders to push out," and then does something management teams rarely do voluntarily: he withdraws the ability to forecast. "Beyond the second quarter, our visibility is very limited. As such, we are not commenting on or reaffirming our expectations beyond the second quarter." Three long-term themes are named — artificial intelligence, verticalization (business concentrating into a handful of very large, vertically integrated customers), and electrification — but they are framed as things that will help *eventually*. The near-term content is cost control: robotics breakeven cut from $440 million of revenue in 2024 to $365 million in 2025.

**July 2025 — the tone turns.** "We are significantly more confident than we were 90 days ago." The pivot point is named precisely: artificial-intelligence compute demand, and the observation that customers have finally exhausted the pool of idle testers they had been upgrading rather than replacing. That last point matters more than it sounds — for two years Teradyne had been selling cheap upgrades into an over-supplied installed base. Smith: "we have turned the corner towards more new system sales rather than selling upgrades."

**October 2025 — the story becomes quantified.** Second-half compute revenue is "more than 50% higher than our expectations just 3 months ago." Memory test revenue doubles sequentially to $128 million. Management begins putting a number on the artificial-intelligence share of revenue: roughly 50% in the September quarter, 60% guided for December.

**February 2026 — the pivot is declared complete.** The retrospective is the most useful passage in the whole set. In 2023, about 10% of the chip-test product revenue was compute and 40% was mobile phones. By 2025, compute was nearly half, and compute revenue grew 90% year over year. The company that was a mobile-phone cycle proxy became an artificial-intelligence data-centre proxy in roughly eight quarters.

**April and July 2026 — records, and a new frame.** Revenue of $1.28 billion then $1.33 billion, both records, both above the top of guidance. The narrative widens from "we test chips" to "wafer to data center" — testing silicon, then boards, then server trays, then the robots that assemble them. The July call introduces a genuinely new argument: test equipment is taking a rising share of total chip-industry capital spending (roughly 4% in 2023, 7% in 2025, 8% in the first five months of 2026), because advanced packaging stacks more chips into one package and a single bad chip ruins the whole assembly.

**What was quietly dropped.** Mobile phones. In April 2025 the mobile recovery was a named 2026 growth driver tied to the two-nanometre manufacturing transition. By July 2026 mobile is "a muted part of the overall portfolio" and is explicitly forecast to *decline* in the second half. Also softened: the 2028 revenue-mix target of one-third mobile, one-third compute, one-third automotive and industrial, abandoned when compute alone reached 70% of chip-test product revenue.

**Where the narrative is *poorer* than the numbers.** This is unusual and worth stating plainly: management has consistently under-described its own business. The screener records seven earnings beats in seven quarters. The June-2026 quarter beat the consensus estimate of $2.04 per share with $2.47 — a 21% miss by the analyst community, on revenue that came in 9.3% above expectations. A company that had been talking about tariff risk and lost visibility fifteen months earlier has quadrupled its quarterly earnings.

**Where it is *richer*.** Two places. Robotics, discussed above — the segment the company keeps positioning as a strategic pillar has been a rounding error and a margin drag for years. And the customer-concentration framing: the words "verticalization" and "wafer to data center" describe something that is, financially, three customers at roughly 10% of revenue each (disclosed February 2026) and a business Smith himself called "increasingly concentrated to these customers into a smaller number of very large… device programs."

**A number the market should look at harder.** Receivables. Trailing days-of-sales-outstanding is **90.7 days**, and the March-2026 quarter absorbed a **$322 million** receivables build on $1,282 million of revenue. In the September-2025 quarter free cash flow was **$2.4 million** on $769 million of revenue, entirely because of working capital. The earnings are real; the cash conversion is lumpy and lags by one to two quarters. Trailing income quality (cash from operations divided by net income) is 0.93 — earnings are running slightly ahead of cash.

## 3. TONE & CONFIDENCE TRAJECTORY

**Rising, steeply and steadily.** The progression is one of the cleanest in the set:

- **April 2025:** "Beyond the second quarter, our visibility is very limited." Defensive vocabulary throughout — "prudently managing expenses," "green shoots," "we have historically emerged stronger coming out of challenging macroeconomic periods."
- **July 2025:** "We are significantly more confident than we were 90 days ago." First offensive language returns.
- **October 2025:** "2026 looks stronger today than it did 6 months ago, and all indications suggest solid growth from 2025." Still hedged on timing.
- **February 2026:** "In our mind, the answer to that is unequivocally true" — on whether the end markets are poised for growth. The strongest single sentence in the window.
- **April 2026:** "We are calling 2026 the year of execution. And we are… hitting on all cylinders."
- **July 2026:** "Our optimism around 2026, 2027 and through the midterm has grown. We are leaning further into investments."

Critically, the confidence is being **spent, not just spoken**. Operating expenses are deliberately being raised into the second half — chief financial officer Michelle Turner: "we expect OpEx in Q4 to be comparable to Q3 guidance positioning us for further growth in 2027." Hiring and research spending ahead of revenue is the costly signal; talk is free.

The counterweight, and it is a real one: the confidence has become *specific about direction and vague about magnitude*. Management will say 2027 is "another year of healthy growth" and will not put a number on it, deferring the updated target earnings model to the Q4 call in early 2027.

## 4. GUIDANCE CREDIBILITY — **High**

Teradyne has under-promised and over-delivered for six consecutive quarters, and the pattern is documented inside the calls themselves.

- April 2025 guided the June quarter and refused to go further. The June quarter came in "above the midpoint of our guidance ranges."
- The October 2025 call contains an explicit self-correction: second-half compute revenue "more than 50% higher than our expectations just 3 months ago." Management was materially too pessimistic about its own business, in writing, one quarter earlier.
- February 2026 flagged "robust double-digit year-over-year growth." Revenue is tracking toward roughly $5.1 billion for 2026 against $3.19 billion in 2025 — about **60%** growth. Double-digit was a large understatement.
- Both 2026 quarters came in "above the high end" of the guidance range.

Is this sandbagging? Partly, and management has an unusually honest explanation for why the caution is structural rather than tactical. Smith, April 2026, on why he cannot see further than chip-makers can: "the lead time for a tester is on the order of the same as the lead time for the actual wafer… until they actually are seeing the wafers go through, they are holding back on the orders for the test equipment." Tester lead times are roughly sixteen weeks. The company genuinely does not have a long order book — which is both why guidance is conservative and why the order book cannot be leaned on as a source of safety.

The one place to withhold credit is robotics, where a breakeven promise has now been made three years running and not delivered.

## 5. ANALYST PRESSURE POINTS

**Visibility — asked every single call, never fully answered.** Vivek Arya of Bank of America put it most sharply in April 2026: "everyone else has great visibility and confidence, but we are not hitting that right from the testing side as much?" The answer is the sixteen-week lead time explanation above. It is a good answer, and it is also an admission that Teradyne is the last link in the chain to see an order and the first to see a cancellation.

**Market share — the recurring soft spot.** Timothy Arcuri of UBS pushed directly in July 2026 on when the design wins convert into share. Smith's answer contains the most important sentence management said all year: "we are gaining share in most of the segments of the market but the part of the market that is growing most strongly is the part of the market where we are starting with the lowest share." Translated: even winning, Teradyne's blended share can go sideways.

**Gross margin durability.** C.J. Muse of Cantor asked what is behind the guided margin step-down to 58–59%. Turner's answer was candid rather than reassuring: some of the first-half margin came from "non-recurring, non-operational" items that will not repeat, and "memory is going to continue to be a strain from an overall margin perspective… we expect that will continue in 2027."

**Robotics — asked, deflected into narrative.** Mehdi Hosseini of SIG noted in July 2026 that robotics "is not getting as much of a headline." The response was thematic ("physical AI applications") rather than numeric. No robotics profitability figure has been volunteered in six calls.

**The question analysts are *not* pressing hard enough.** Nobody challenged Smith's own admission that the test share of chip-industry capital spending — the arithmetic underpinning the whole bull case — has run from 4% to 8% in three years and, in his words, "is not going to continue to go up from there," possibly reverting to "6, 7% or so" in 2027. Management volunteered the single most important caution in the story and it drew no follow-up.

## 6. RED FLAGS / GREEN FLAGS

🔴 **Customer concentration is intensifying by design.** Three customers at roughly 10% each (February 2026), and an explicit acknowledgement that the business is concentrating further. Smith, April 2026: "This concentration also increases the risk that bottlenecks in other areas could shift demand for our products, which can lead to short-term demand peaks and valleys."

🔴 **The second half of 2026 steps down.** September-quarter guidance is $1.2–1.3 billion of revenue and $1.85–2.15 of earnings per share, against $1.33 billion and $2.47 just delivered. Operating margin guided to 28–30% from 33.7%. Management is explicit about the cause — "order timing in compute" — and about the fix being far away: "the next surge for these customers is expected to be in the first half of 2027."

🔴 **Operating expenses are being held up into the down quarters.** Deliberate, and defensible as an investment in 2027 — but it means falling revenue meets a fixed cost base. That is negative operating leverage by choice.

🔴 **Cash conversion lags badly.** 90.7 days of receivables and the working-capital pattern described in section 2.

🔴 **No insider is buying.** Zero open-market purchases against 32 sales totalling 36,970 shares. On a stock up roughly 366% over the past year, that is not damning, but it is not conviction either.

🔴 **Robotics: three years of unmet breakeven promises.** $440 million breakeven in 2024, $365 million in 2025, "aiming at breakeven" in 2026, on a segment still running at roughly $400 million a year.

🔴 **Management's own ceiling warning.** The 4%→7%→8% test-share-of-capital-spending trajectory, and the caution that it settles at 7–9% and may revert. Extrapolating it is the market's error, not management's.

🟢 **Share count is falling, not rising.** Diluted shares went from 163.5 million in the June-2024 quarter to 157.7 million in June 2026 — down 3.5%. There is no serial-dilution pattern here of any kind. That single fact clears the largest failure mode in this sector.

🟢 **Counter-cyclical buyback — the strongest capital-discipline evidence in the file.** Repurchases were **$243.8 million** in the September-2025 quarter and **$183.4 million** in December 2025, when the shares traded in roughly the $90–200 band. They fell to **$5.5 million** in March 2026 and **$68.7 million** in June 2026, with the stock between roughly $250 and $400. Management bought heavily near the low and stepped back near the high. Very few management teams do this; most do the reverse.

🟢 **Balance sheet is clean and got cleaner.** A $200 million short-term borrowing taken in the September-2025 quarter was fully repaid by March 2026. Net cash of roughly $255 million; net debt to earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation of −0.14 times. Cash and investments of $517 million, up over 30% in the quarter.

🟢 **Gross margin expanded while revenue doubled.** 57.2% to 59.8% year over year on +104% revenue. In a supply-constrained niche that is pricing power. Commoditisation looks like the opposite — volume bought with margin.

🟢 **A named, quantified order metric with direction.** Turner, July 2026: memory demand is "driving our book-to-bill ratio in the quarter over two." Two dollars of orders for every dollar shipped, in the segment management says will grow more than 40% at the market level this year. That is the one hard order-book datapoint in the entire file, and it is strong.

🟢 **Returns are compounding, not just recovering.** Return on invested capital of 30.8% and return on equity of 38.1% on a trailing basis, against the screener's fiscal-year figures of 22.1% and 25.1%.

🟢 **Low capital intensity.** Capital spending is 5.9% of revenue. There is no half-built plant to finish and no sustaining-capital hole — the growth spending is roughly $155 million across a half-year against $2.6 billion of revenue.

## 7. HIDDEN SIGNALS

**A new chief financial officer, and a different register.** Sanjay Mehta presents through October 2025; Michelle Turner from February 2026. Turner is noticeably more forthcoming with mechanism and less with reassurance. She volunteered the memory book-to-bill above two, and she volunteered that first-half margins contained non-recurring benefits that will not repeat. Both cut against her own quarter. That is a good sign about the numbers.

**The moat language got weaker, from the chief executive.** In October 2025 Smith described "an incumbency advantage" in system-level test, "similar to the incumbency advantage that you have in [automated test equipment]… pretty strong." By February 2026 he described the steady state differently: "Teradyne and Advantest are going to be competing on a generational basis for new design wins. And those decisions are going to be made on the basis of the features of the tester, the reliability of the tester **more than sort of incumbency**." That is a chief executive telling you the switching costs are thinner than they used to be — and it is the direct consequence of the same customer dual-sourcing push that is handing Teradyne its wins. The mechanism that lets Teradyne into a rival's account is the mechanism that lets a rival into Teradyne's.

**"Invited to the dance."** Smith, July 2025, on the merchant graphics-processor opportunity: "The reason that the opportunity is there is because of customers' desire to have more resilience in their supply chain. That's why we were invited to the dance." The share gains are being *granted* by customers who want two suppliers, not *taken* on product superiority alone. Real, durable while the customers want it, and reversible.

**Capital allocation language shifted from returns to reinvestment.** Turner, July 2026: "Our first priority for capital continues to be reinvesting into the business… keep dry powder available for accretive M&A." Buybacks and the small dividend have moved down the list. Three acquisitions closed in fifteen months — Quantifi Photonics ($127 million, June-2025 quarter), the MultiLane Test Products joint venture and TestInsight (together $166 million, June-2026 quarter). All small, all cash-funded, all adjacent. Nothing here looks like empire-building yet, but the stated priority has changed at exactly the point in the cycle when the shares are most expensive.

**An arithmetic wobble worth noting.** In the July 2026 prepared remarks Smith says chip-industry capital spending "is now forecasted to approach $250 billion," then a few sentences later "as WFE CapEx approaches $200 billion by the end of the decade." Two different anchors for the same forecast in the same paragraph. Not deceptive — but the total-market arithmetic that supports the multiple is looser than the confident delivery suggests.

## 8. CAPITAL-ALLOCATION VERDICT (for the director)

**Trajectory: STRENGTHENING.** This is not a promotional company. Across six calls management was repeatedly too cautious about its own business, corrected itself in public, beat its own guidance every quarter, retired 3.5% of the shares, bought back stock heavily at the low and stepped back at the high, carries net cash, and expanded gross margin through a doubling of revenue. Almost every failure mode this lane is built to catch — serial dilution, inflated capacity claims, promoter language, capacity built on borrowed money — is simply absent.

**The single most important thing to weigh before sizing.** Not whether the business is good. It is. The question is whether the *price* already contains the 2027 that management will not yet quantify. Three facts sit uncomfortably together: the second half of 2026 is guided to step down in revenue, margin and earnings; the next compute surge is not expected until the first half of 2027; and the chief executive has personally warned that test's rising share of chip-industry capital spending — the arithmetic the market is extrapolating — settles at 7–9% and could revert toward 6–7% next year. The stock has risen roughly 366% in twelve months and 121% this year. There is no operational problem to solve here. There is a multiple to survive.

**Economic moat: NARROW, and widening cyclically rather than structurally.** The moat type is **switching costs** — test programs are written against a specific tester platform and porting them is expensive, which is why incumbency in a socket persists across device generations. The width evidence cuts both ways. Returns are rising hard (invested-capital returns from 22% to 31%; gross margin +256 basis points year over year), which by the usual test reads as widening. But the improvement is overwhelmingly cyclical operating leverage on a doubling of volume, and the chief executive has himself downgraded the durability of the switching cost, saying design wins now turn on tester features and reliability "more than sort of incumbency." Blended chip-test share is flat at roughly 37% by management's own account. A duopoly with Advantest in a structurally growing market is a real and defensible position — it is not a wide one.

**Secular threat: manageable.** Teradyne sits on the beneficiary side of the artificial-intelligence build-out, not the displaced side: more transistors and more bits mean more test seconds, and advanced packaging raises test intensity per die because one latent defect ruins an expensive multi-chip assembly. There is no structural force eroding the terminal earnings base. The genuine long-term risks are competitive and cyclical rather than terminal — Advantest taking the fastest-growing sockets, Chinese domestic testers advancing in the automotive and industrial tier (management concedes "significant competition" there), and the ordinary violence of a capital-equipment cycle. The cheapness question does not arise: this is an expensive stock, so the value trap risk is not a melting base but a peak-earnings multiple applied to peak-cycle earnings.
