# Flowserve Corporation (FLS) — Forensic Dossier

**Prepared 2026-08-11 · price used: $80.46 (US dollars, New York Stock Exchange, live quote 2026-08-11) · market value about $10.3 billion**

Flowserve makes industrial pumps, valves, seals and actuators — the plumbing of heavy industry. About half of what it sells is aftermarket work: spare parts, repairs and service on equipment already installed in refineries, chemical plants and power stations. The other half is original equipment: new pumps and valves sold into new plants. The reason this name sits in a future-technology lane is narrow and specific: Flowserve holds nuclear quality-assurance certifications and has equipment inside more than 75% of the roughly 400 reactors operating worldwide, which positions it to sell into the nuclear build-out that artificial-intelligence power demand is reviving.

Eight quarterly calls were reviewed, from the first quarter of 2025 through the first quarter of 2026, supplemented by the second-quarter 2026 results released 2026-07-29 and the surrounding disclosures.

---

## 1. NARRATIVE ARC

**The story has genuinely changed shape over these eight quarters, and mostly in a good direction — but the emphasis has migrated from one engine to another in a way worth naming.**

*Early 2025 — an execution story.* The opening frame was internal: the "Flowserve Business System," an 80/20 complexity-reduction programme (cutting the long tail of low-volume products to concentrate on the profitable core), and operational excellence. On the first-quarter 2025 call the chief executive described visiting a US pump plant where "we reduced SKUs by nearly 80%." The promise was 200-plus basis points of gross-margin improvement by 2027. The external environment was framed as a threat to be managed — tariffs, with an annualised gross exposure sized at $90-100 million before mitigation.

*Mid-2025 — a fork survived.* In July 2025 the proposed merger with Chart Industries was terminated after Baker Hughes made an all-cash offer for Chart. Flowserve walked away and collected a $266 million termination payment. Management's framing — "further pursuing the merger would have been value diminishing to Flowserve shareholders given the additional cash, leverage and diluted ownership required" — was disciplined, and the money was largely returned through buybacks. This was the first real test of the capital-allocation claim, and they passed it.

*Late 2025 — nuclear arrives as the headline.* The third-quarter 2025 call is the pivot point. Two full slides were devoted to nuclear for the first time. Management quantified it: content in over 75% of roughly 400 operating reactors; power at roughly 7% of revenue, half traditional and half nuclear; a year-to-date power book-to-bill of 2.0 times; a record $140 million of nuclear bookings in the quarter; and a forward map of "40 new large nuclear reactors to be under construction in the next 10 years" plus "as many as 30 SMRs" (small modular reactors) in five years. By the fourth-quarter 2025 call the per-reactor content figure had been raised from roughly $100 million to $115-120 million on the back of the Trillium valve acquisition.

*2026 — the margin engine slows while the order engine accelerates.* This is the most important development and the one the market is now arguing about. In the first quarter of 2026 the Middle East conflict cost an estimated $50 million of bookings and dented sales. In late May 2026 the activist investor Starboard Value delivered a letter to the board arguing that "margins and overall operational performance fall short of what shareholders should expect." Then the second quarter of 2026 delivered a genuinely split result: bookings surged 26% to $1.35 billion with a book-to-bill (orders divided by sales) of 1.15 times and backlog up 16.9% to $3.34 billion — but sales fell 1.6%, adjusted operating margin expanded only 70 basis points versus 420 basis points a year earlier, and the full-year organic sales guide was cut from up 3-6% to down about 1%.

**Quietly dropped:** the RedRaven digital-services scale-up. It was introduced on the second-quarter 2025 call as an MoU (a memorandum of understanding — a statement of intent, not a contract) with Honeywell to embed RedRaven into Honeywell's Forge platform, described as "an opportunity to significantly scale our RedRaven offering" and "a recurring stream of revenue." Management said "we look forward to sharing more details." Four quarters later there is no disclosed revenue, no named customer and no further update in the material reviewed. This is the classic pattern the sector demands scepticism about, and it should carry zero value in any valuation.

### The two divergences that matter

**First: the margin story is real but the rate of improvement has collapsed.** Track the adjusted operating margin expansion quarter by quarter: +420 basis points (Q4 2025), +230 basis points (Q1 2026), **+70 basis points (Q2 2026)**. The direction is still positive and the streak is intact, but the slope has flattened by more than 80% in two quarters. Anyone underwriting a straight line to the 2030 target of a 20% adjusted operating margin is extrapolating the 2025 slope, not the 2026 one.

**Second: the gap between reported and adjusted results widened sharply in the most recent quarter.** In the second quarter of 2026, reported gross margin was 32.9% while adjusted gross margin was 35.9% — a 300-basis-point gap, or roughly $35 million of charges sitting inside cost of sales. On a reported basis, gross margin actually *fell* about 175 basis points against the year-ago quarter. Reported diluted earnings per share was $0.77 against the adjusted $0.95. For full-year 2025 the gap was a full dollar per share: $2.64 reported against $3.64 adjusted. Much of that is genuinely non-recurring (the Chart break fee, the asbestos-liability divestiture, footprint closures, Trillium purchase accounting), but an add-back load of that size is a permanent question mark that deserves monitoring, not dismissal.

**Third, and least discussed: the divisional result split violently in the most recent quarter.** The pumps division reported a 22.3% operating margin on $814 million of sales. The flow-control division reported a **3.1%** operating margin on $357 million of sales — against 19.7% in the fourth quarter of 2025. Acquisition accounting for Trillium explains much of it, but a swing of that magnitude in a single quarter is not something to wave through.

## 3. TONE & CONFIDENCE TRAJECTORY

**Rating: Rising through 2025, then shifting from offensive to defensive in 2026.**

- **Q1 2025 (defensive-cautious):** "the current tariff environment introduces a new dynamic to our outlook." Guidance was reaffirmed, not raised, despite a strong quarter — deliberate conservatism.
- **Q2 2025 (rising):** "exceptional second quarter earnings," guidance raised to $3.25-3.40. Confidence in the walk-away from Chart was unqualified: "we have no regrets."
- **Q3 2025 (peak confidence):** "exceptional results across bookings, margin expansion, earnings and cash flow." Second guidance raise of the year. The nuclear opportunity is presented with hard numbers rather than adjectives — always a better sign than the reverse.
- **Q4 2025 (peak, with a self-check):** "achievement of our long-term margin targets 2 years ahead of plan." Note the buyback disclosure: $255 million repurchased at an average of $53, described as "a discounted share price relative to intrinsic value."
- **Q1 2026 (defensive):** the language shifts hard. "Highly dynamic environment," "resilience," employee safety in the Middle East, an "$50 million headwind." The full-year outlook was *maintained* rather than raised — the first time in five quarters that momentum did not translate into a raise.
- **Q2 2026 (split):** confident on orders ("14th consecutive quarter of year-over-year adjusted gross margin expansion," record aftermarket bookings), defensive on revenue (organic guide cut, Middle East described as "temporary or transitory," large projects pushed "potentially into 2027").

The vocabulary migration is the tell. In 2025 the operative words were growth, funnel, opportunity, capture. In 2026 they are resilience, mitigation, disruption, transitory. That is a management team that has moved from offence to defence on the top line while staying on offence on cost — which is exactly what the numbers show.

## 4. GUIDANCE CREDIBILITY

**Score: High.**

The 2025 track record is unusually clean. Guidance was set at $3.00-3.20 in adjusted earnings per share, reaffirmed under tariff pressure in the first quarter, raised to $3.25-3.40 in the second, raised again to $3.40-3.50 in the third, and delivered at $3.64. That is two raises and a final beat above the top of the last raised range. The screener's beat rate of six out of seven quarters (86%) corroborates it.

There is a mild sandbagging pattern, and management essentially admitted it. Asked in the first quarter of 2025 whether they would ordinarily be raising, the finance chief laid out the low end as including "some mismatch in tariff impact and mitigating actions" plus "some level of demand destruction in the back half" — a range built around a downside they did not expect to occur. That is prudent, not deceptive, but it means the beats are partly engineered.

**The 2026 guidance is a different and harder test.** The company raised the low end of the adjusted earnings range to $4.05-4.20 while simultaneously cutting the organic sales guide to down about 1%. Delivering the earnings number now depends on a back half that management concedes must produce roughly 5% organic sales growth after a first half that was down about 4%. First-half adjusted earnings per share of roughly $1.80 requires $2.25-2.40 in the second half. It is achievable — the backlog supports it and the year-ago comparison is favourable — but it is the most back-half-dependent guide in the eight quarters reviewed, and it rests on a Middle East recovery that management itself calls dynamic.

## 5. ANALYST PRESSURE POINTS

**The recurring question analysts keep asking, and management keeps answering obliquely, is: where is the revenue growth?**

- **Bank of America, Q3 2025:** "last year, bookings grew 9%. This year, bookings are sort of flattish… how did this year's bookings translate into hitting the 5% revenue target next year?" The answer redirected to what growth looks like *excluding* Middle East original-equipment energy projects ("has grown at 9%"). That is an ex-the-bad-part number. Six months later the 2026 organic sales guide was cut to negative — the analyst was right to push.
- **TD Cowen, Q2 2025, on Mogas:** "I think the expectation initially was something around $200 million in revenue. I think it's way lower than that." Management conceded the shortfall and reframed the figure as a destination. Credit for candour; debit for the original framing.
- **Bank of America, Q3 2025, on power excluding nuclear:** "it seems that they've been quite weak this year. I think we calculate down high teens." The answer — "a little bit of a timing impact," "a more competitive type environment" — is a soft acknowledgement that outside the nuclear niche, Flowserve is a price-taker. This matters directly to the power-for-artificial-intelligence part of the thesis: gas-turbine and combined-cycle balance-of-plant work is competitive, and management said so.
- **The under-asked question:** nobody in the material reviewed pressed hard on the widening gap between reported and adjusted results, or on the 3.1% flow-control division margin. Analysts are underwriting the adjusted line.
- **The unaddressed structural question:** 80/20 deliberately removes revenue in exchange for margin. Organic sales are now negative while margins expand. No analyst has forced management to quantify how much of the negative organic growth is self-inflicted product pruning versus lost demand — and management has not volunteered it.

## 6. RED FLAGS / GREEN FLAGS

**🔴 Red flags**

1. **Organic revenue is shrinking while the story is about growth.** Trailing-twelve-month revenue growth of negative 0.26%; the 2026 organic guide cut from up 3-6% to down about 1%. Every headline growth figure in the reported accounts is carried by acquisitions and currency (Q2 2026: sales down 1.6% despite Trillium and a weak dollar).
2. **Margin expansion has decelerated by more than 80% in two quarters** — from 420 basis points to 70 basis points of adjusted operating margin gain. The streak is being defended; the slope is not.
3. **The adjustment load is large and growing.** A 300-basis-point gap between reported and adjusted gross margin in Q2 2026, up from a much narrower gap earlier; a full dollar per share of add-backs in 2025.
4. **The flow-control division's reported operating margin collapsed to 3.1%** in Q2 2026 from 19.7% two quarters earlier.
5. **The RedRaven / Honeywell arrangement is an MoU that has produced nothing disclosed in four quarters** and was described in scale-up language at introduction. Value it at zero.
6. **Working capital is heavy and getting heavier.** Days sales outstanding of 110 and a cash conversion cycle of 145 days; trailing free cash flow of $411 million on adjusted earnings of roughly $500 million is about 82% conversion, below the 97% claimed for full-year 2025.
7. **Leverage rose materially to fund Trillium.** Net debt went from $1.10 billion at 31 March 2026 to $1.59 billion at 30 June 2026 (borrowings of $2.14 billion plus $183 million of capitalised leases, less $731 million of cash). Management's "1 times net levered" is a year-end 2025, pre-acquisition, ex-lease figure — the current figure on adjusted profits is closer to 1.9 times including leases.
8. **Nuclear timing is genuinely long.** Management's own finance chief conceded on the Q3 2025 call that nuclear content creates "slight pressure on backlog conversion" — these orders sit in backlog longer than the rest of the book. The order growth is real; the cash is years out.

**🟢 Green flags**

1. **Fourteen consecutive quarters of year-over-year adjusted gross-margin expansion.** Whatever the deceleration, that is a long, verifiable, self-generated improvement streak, and 80/20 gives it a mechanism rather than a hope.
2. **Long-term margin targets hit two years early.** Adjusted operating margin of 16.8% in Q4 2025 against a 14-16% target for 2027.
3. **The aftermarket franchise is the real asset and it keeps compounding.** Eight consecutive quarters of bookings above $600 million, a record roughly $696 million in Q2 2026, up 12%. This is repeat service revenue on an installed base — the closest thing to an annuity in heavy industry.
4. **Walking away from Chart, and the reason given.** Terminating a transformative merger on dilution and leverage grounds, collecting $266 million, and then buying back stock at $53 is textbook capital discipline.
5. **Buyback timing has been price-sensitive rather than mechanical.** $255 million at an average $53 during 2025; $25 million at roughly $67 in Q2 2026 and another $25 million in July. The pace fell roughly tenfold as the price rose. That is management behaving like an owner — and it is also a revealed opinion about value at current prices.
6. **The nuclear position is specific and defensible.** Content in over 75% of about 400 operating reactors; main-steam isolation valves and actuators in the nuclear island; the quality-assurance certifications that are the actual barrier to entry. Trillium adds four nuclear-certified product lines and raises content per new large reactor from about $100 million to $115-120 million.
7. **Order momentum verified in the most recent quarter:** bookings $1.35 billion (up 25.5%), backlog $3.34 billion (up 16.9%), book-to-bill 1.15 times, nuclear bookings above $110 million including an award for new large reactors in Asia and North American life-extension work.
8. **Insider behaviour is clean, if thin.** Two open-market purchases totalling 3,500 shares against zero sales. Small, but the sign is right.

## 7. HIDDEN SIGNALS

**The single most informative disclosure in eight quarters is the buyback price history.** In 2025 management deployed $255 million at an average of $53 and explicitly called it "a discounted share price relative to intrinsic value." In the second quarter of 2026, with the stock near $67, they deployed $25 million — one-tenth the pace — and simultaneously spent roughly $500 million on the Trillium acquisition and sold the PMV Automation product line (closed 2026-08-10). At $80 they are buying businesses and selling product lines, not buying their own stock. Management's revealed view is that the shares are no longer the best use of a dollar.

**The vocabulary around large projects has been steadily downgraded.** In the Q3 2025 call the chief executive volunteered that large engineered projects, once "20-plus percent of our bookings," are now "typically around a mid-single-digit percentage." That is presented as de-risking, and it is. But it also means the company has deliberately shrunk its exposure to exactly the kind of large capital project that a nuclear build-out consists of — which is a quiet tension inside the bull story.

**The competitive framing outside nuclear is more honest than the slides.** On traditional power: "It is a more competitive type environment. And so we've got to be really selective." That is a company describing itself as a price-taker in gas and combined-cycle balance-of-plant work — the very segment most exposed to data-centre power build-out. The pricing power is in nuclear and in the aftermarket, not in power generally.

**The Middle East disclosure is being staged.** First-quarter 2026: a $50 million bookings headwind. Second-quarter 2026: roughly $60 million of first-half headwind, the region sized at 12-13% of sales, large projects potentially pushed into 2027, and about $50 million of restoration work identified. Management is converting a disruption into a future opportunity in the narrative faster than the orders are converting in reality.

**Governance is being reshaped under external pressure.** Starboard Value delivered a letter to the board on 2026-05-28 calling execution and margins short of what shareholders should expect. The board was reduced from eleven members to nine effective 2026-05-14, and a new director was appointed effective 2026-08-05. Management's public response reaffirmed 2026 guidance and 2030 targets (mid-single-digit organic sales growth, a 20% adjusted operating margin by 2030, double-digit adjusted earnings growth) and cited 860 basis points of adjusted operating margin improvement since 2022. The pressure is real, the response has so far been to defend rather than accelerate, and the disagreement is about pace, not direction.

## 8. CAPITAL-ALLOCATION VERDICT (for the director)

**Classification: STRENGTHENING.**

This is a genuinely improving industrial franchise. Fourteen straight quarters of adjusted gross-margin expansion, long-term margin targets hit two years early, a disciplined walk-away from a transformative merger, price-sensitive buybacks, a record and still-growing aftermarket order book, and a defensible nuclear qualification position. The forensic file is clean by the standards of this sector: no serial dilution, no capacity or backlog inflation (the backlog is disclosed, dated and independently corroborated by reported bookings), and only one piece of promoter-style language — the RedRaven memorandum of understanding — which has simply gone quiet rather than been oversold.

**The single most important thing to weigh before sizing:** *the margin engine and the revenue engine are moving in opposite directions, and the price now embeds the margin engine continuing at its 2025 pace.* Organic sales are guided to be down about 1% in 2026 while the stock has run from a 52-week low of $48.71 to $80.46 — a 65% move — leaving it 70% up its 52-week range and about 8% above its 200-day average. The company earns its improvement through complexity reduction, which by construction subtracts revenue. That works beautifully until the multiple demands growth as well as margin. At roughly 19.5 times 2026 adjusted earnings and about 14 times enterprise value to adjusted profits before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, the shares are no longer priced as a self-help story; they are priced as a nuclear growth story whose organic revenue is currently negative.

**Economic moat: NARROW, and WIDENING.**

The moat type is a combination of **regulatory-license** and **switching-costs**. The regulatory piece is the nuclear quality-assurance certification and customer approval regime — management is right that these "barriers to entry" are the real asset, and Trillium was bought partly to acquire more of them. The switching-cost piece is the installed base: once a Flowserve pump or main-steam isolation valve is inside a reactor or a refinery, the spare parts and service revenue follows it for decades, which is why aftermarket bookings have exceeded $600 million for eight consecutive quarters.

The evidence says **widening, not eroding**: gross margin has risen from 27.5% to about 35% over three years, return on invested capital has improved to roughly 8.6%, and return on equity has risen from about 8% in 2021 to a mid-teens level. A widening moat is one where returns and margins rise together, and here they do. The width is only *narrow* rather than *wide* because return on invested capital, at roughly 8.6%, is still around or below a reasonable cost of capital — the franchise is improving from a mediocre base, not defending an excellent one.

**Secular threat: MANAGEABLE.** The obvious structural force — the energy transition — cuts both ways here and on balance favours Flowserve. The legacy exposure to oil, gas and refining is a real long-duration risk, but it is being actively diversified away from, and the same transition is what creates the nuclear, grid and electrification demand that now drives the order book. There is no artificial-intelligence displacement risk to a physical pump, no disintermediation risk, and no reimbursement or channel-shift dynamic. The genuine risk here is **cyclical, not terminal**: an order-cycle turn in utility and process-industry capital spending, and rising input costs for castings, forgings and metals landing on fixed-price backlog. That is a valuation and timing problem, not a melting-terminal-value problem.

**Therefore the cheapness question is moot in the other direction:** this is not a fear-print and it is not a value trap — it is a good business that has already been re-rated. The discipline required here is price discipline, not credibility scepticism.
