# Crane Company (CR) — Forensic Dossier
*Prepared 2026-08-04. Price used throughout: $224.20 (New York Stock Exchange, US dollars), the live quote on the morning of 4 August 2026. Market value about $12.9 billion; roughly 58.7 million diluted shares.*

Transcripts reviewed: second quarter 2025 (29 July 2025), third quarter 2025 (28 October 2025), fourth quarter 2025 (27 January 2026), first quarter 2026 (28 April 2026), second quarter 2026 (29 July 2026).

---

## 1. Narrative arc

The story Crane tells has changed shape twice in five quarters, and both changes were upward.

**Mid-2025 — a two-engine industrial with a deal in hand.** In July 2025 the pitch was operational: an in-house management method they call the Crane Business System, 6.5% core sales growth, orders up nearly 20%, and a newly signed agreement to buy the Precision Sensors and Instrumentation unit from Baker Hughes. Management described the balance sheet as having "both the financial and operational capacity for significant additional" deal-making, and sized the pipeline at "sub-$100 million to $1 billion."

**Late 2025 — the aerospace engine takes over the microphone.** By October 2025 the emphasis had shifted decisively toward defence and aerospace: record order book, the Golden Dome missile-defence programme, the LTAMDS radar and Patriot missile positions, collaborative combat aircraft, the XM30 and Common Tactical Truck vehicle programmes. Notably, the deal pipeline was quietly re-sized *downward* — from "sub-$100 million to $1 billion" in July to "most range in deal-size purchase price from $100 million to $500 million" in October. That is a small, honest downgrade of ambition that nobody asked about.

**2026 — the acquisitions become the story.** The Baker Hughes deal closed on 1 January 2026 (three brands: Druck, Panametrics and Reuter-Stokes), alongside a smaller German purchase, optek-Danulat. From the first quarter of 2026 onward, roughly half of every prepared remark is about how well those four businesses are performing. The segment was renamed from Aerospace & Electronics to Aerospace & Advanced Technologies — an explicit signal that management intends to widen the acquisition aperture beyond aviation. Alex Alcala replaced Max Mitchell as chief executive on 27 April 2026, with Mitchell staying on as executive chairman for up to two years.

**What was quietly dropped.** Nothing material has been dropped, which is itself notable. But two things never grew into what an investor might have hoped. The chemical end-market weakness first flagged in mid-2025 persisted through four calls before management would say in July 2026 that it is "starting to see something." And the nuclear story — the reason this name reaches our desk at all — has never been sized. Reuter-Stokes carries roughly $90 million of annual revenue on a company that will do close to $2.9 billion this year. That is about three percent of the business.

## 2. Claims versus the financials

| Management claim | The evidence | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| "Record" total company adjusted operating margin of 21.3% in the second quarter of 2026, up 180 basis points | The audited, unadjusted figures agree: operating margin was 19.9% ($144.3m on $724.7m) against 17.8% a year earlier ($102.9m on $577.2m), a 210 basis-point gain. The margin expansion is real, not an artefact of what gets excluded. | Holds up |
| Gross margin is expanding | Trailing-twelve-month gross margin is 41.7% versus 41.3% a year ago — a gain of about 40 basis points on revenue up 13.9%. Over three years the gain is larger: 37.2% in fiscal 2022 to 42.2% in fiscal 2025. | Holds up, but the recent pace is far slower than the three-year headline |
| Aerospace and defence is the growth engine | Segment sales of $339.1m in the second quarter, up 31.3% in total and 13.3% excluding the Druck acquisition, with a record order book near $1.27 billion. | Holds up |
| Execution is improving everywhere | The same aerospace segment's adjusted operating margin *fell* 80 basis points year on year, to 25.8% from 26.6%. The segment doing the growing is the one losing margin. | Diverges from the story |
| Process Flow demand is "stable" | Sales rose 20.9% — but core sales, stripping out the acquisitions, *declined* 1.4%. Margin rose to 22.2%. | Technically true; the growth is purchased, not earned |
| Free cash flow conversion runs near 100% of earnings | Trailing free cash flow is $280.4 million against trailing net income of roughly $336 million — about 83%. Management itself guided 2026 down to a 90-100% range because of integration spending. | Softer than the long-run claim |
| The balance sheet is "very strong" | It is investment-grade but no longer pristine. Net debt was $765.3 million at 30 June 2026 (borrowings of $1.10 billion plus $17.3 million of capitalised leases, less $350.4 million of cash) against net *cash* of $514 million a year earlier. That is about 1.4 times earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation — comfortable, and below management's own 2-3 times target. | Holds up |

**The one number that should give a buyer pause.** Goodwill and intangible assets now stand at $1,993 million, or 49.4% of total assets, against total shareholders' equity of $2,178 million. Tangible asset value is $187 million. Almost the entire book value of this company is the price it paid for other companies. Returns reflect that: return on invested capital is 10.0% and return on equity 16.2% — respectable, but not the 20%-plus that a 32-times earnings multiple implies.

**And the multiple.** At $224.20 the shares change hands at about 32 times the midpoint of 2026 adjusted earnings guidance, 39 times trailing reported earnings, 24.6 times trailing earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, and roughly 46 times trailing free cash flow — a free cash flow yield of 2.2%. Our own screening models are unanimous and blunt: every one of the five valuation methods (discounted cash flow, earnings power, Graham, owner earnings, and a 15% required-return model) shows the shares trading above their estimated worth, with the screener's blended fair value at $112 and its discounted cash flow estimate at $78. Those models are calibrated for cheapness and will always look harsh on a quality compounder, but the direction is unambiguous.

## 3. Tone and confidence trajectory

**Rising, and steadily so.** This is one of the more confident management teams in the industrial sector, and the confidence has been earned rather than asserted.

- *July 2025*: "delivering yet another excellent quarter, outperforming expectations despite an uncertain macro backdrop." Raised the full-year outlook.
- *October 2025*: "results coming in ahead of our expectations… another outstanding year." Raised again, and narrowed the range — a tell that visibility had improved.
- *January 2026*: "we exceeded even our high expectations." Full-year adjusted earnings up 24%.
- *April 2026*: notably more guarded on the outside world — "the external environment became more challenging… geopolitical dynamics are evolving." Management deliberately *pre-cut* its own commercial aftermarket assumption from mid-single-digit growth to a mid-single-digit decline, and still raised the earnings guide. That is the single most credible move in these five calls: they built a cushion in public and told everyone they were doing it.
- *July 2026*: "record second quarter results." Raised again, and by July the pre-cut aftermarket assumption had been reversed back to mid-single-digit growth.

There is no shift from offensive to defensive language. If anything, the language grew more offensive as the environment worsened. The only defensive note is on input costs, discussed below.

One stylistic observation for the record: both chief executives end calls with a quotation from a deceased celebrity — a chef, a fashion designer, an actress. It is harmless, but it is promotional packaging on a company that does not need it.

## 4. Guidance credibility

**High — and this is the strongest single fact in the file.**

| Date | Full-year adjusted earnings-per-share guidance | Move |
|---|---|---|
| Jan 2025 (initial 2025) | $5.30 – $5.60 | — |
| Jul 2025 | $5.50 – $5.80 | raised |
| Oct 2025 | $5.75 – $5.95 | raised and narrowed |
| Full-year 2025 delivered | up 24% year on year | delivered at the top |
| Jan 2026 (initial 2026) | $6.55 – $6.75 | — |
| Apr 2026 | $6.65 – $6.85 | raised |
| Jul 2026 | $6.85 – $7.05 | raised |

Five consecutive raises across two fiscal years, corroborated by the screener's record of seven earnings beats in seven quarters. The pattern is mild, disciplined sandbagging: they set a range they are confident of, then raise it as evidence accumulates. That is the behaviour of a team that would rather under-promise than defend a number.

Deal-specific guidance shows the same shape. The four January acquisitions were guided to add about $0.15 per share for 2026; in April that became "at least double what we communicated in January, or about 15 cents"; by July it was "approximately $0.20." Two upgrades in six months.

Reliability score: **High.**

**The one accounting caveat.** In January 2026 — the same quarter the $1.06 billion Baker Hughes deal closed — management changed the definition of adjusted earnings per share to exclude non-cash, tax-effected acquisition-related intangible amortisation. Acquired intangible assets jumped from $149.5 million to $667.3 million on that deal, which implies roughly $50 million a year of amortisation, or something in the neighbourhood of $0.65 to $0.70 per share after tax. In other words, management changed the yardstick in the exact quarter the yardstick was about to get harder, and the change is worth close to a tenth of the headline number. They restated the prior year on the same basis and flagged it clearly, which is the honest way to do it. But an investor comparing the $6.95 guidance midpoint to Crane's own history is not comparing like with like. Reported earnings per share will land far below the adjusted figure this year — the first half already showed gaps of $0.51 in the first quarter and $0.15 in the second.

## 5. Analyst pressure points

Analysts have pushed hardest on four fronts, and management has answered three of them squarely.

1. **Chemical end-market weakness (every call from July 2025).** Jeffrey Sprague of Vertical Research asked directly in July 2025 whether projects were being cancelled or merely delayed. Alcala: "we don't see this big trend of projects being cancelled. It's really things shifting to the right." Four quarters later, in July 2026, he finally conceded improvement — "I have been quite cautious to talk about improvement, but now we are starting to see something." That is a slow, honestly-reported cycle, not a deflection. But it took a year, and Process Flow core sales were still negative in the most recent quarter.

2. **Commercial aftermarket durability.** Repeatedly probed. Management's answer is genuinely differentiated: "we make good margins on commercial OE… that arbitrage between aftermarket and it is not as significant for us as for others." That claim is supported by the fact that the segment held a 25-26% operating margin while its sales mix shifted.

3. **Integration costs and free cash flow.** In January 2026 Nathan Jones of Stifel asked what the cash cost of achieving synergies would be in 2026. Richard Maue: "I don't have that off the top of my head here, Nathan. So we'll look to provide more color on that at the right time." That question has not been answered in a subsequent call. It is the one genuine non-answer in the file, and it sits on the metric — cash — where this company is most expensive.

4. **What nuclear is actually worth.** Justin Ages of CJS Securities asked in July 2025 for "a sense of the opportunity in nuclear," and Justin Bergner asked again in July 2026. Both times the answer was qualitative: strong share in boiling-water reactors, replacement demand, restarts, and small modular reactors, where Reuter-Stokes has "a formal partnership with one of the leading firms." The partner has never been named, no date has been given, and no dollar value has been attached in any of five calls. What analysts are not asking, and management is not volunteering, is how much revenue any of this represents.

**The topic nobody is pressing:** valuation and capital returns. In July 2026 Maue said the priority is "M&A, number one, first and foremost," and that buybacks happen "when we think it is the right time." With the shares within 3% of an all-time high, that is a defensible answer — but it means shareholders are underwriting the next deal at the current multiple, not getting cash back.

## 6. Red flags and green flags

**Concerning**

- *The growth segment's margin is going the wrong way.* Aerospace & Advanced Technologies adjusted operating margin fell to 25.8% from 26.6% in the second quarter of 2026 while its core sales grew 13.3%. Expanding margin on growing revenue is pricing power; compressing margin on growing revenue is volume bought with price. One quarter is not a trend, and the Druck acquisition dilutes the mix, but this is the metric to watch.
- *Core demand at Process Flow is still shrinking.* Core sales down 1.4% in the second quarter of 2026; full-year core growth guided only "flat to up to low-single-digits."
- *Input-cost inflation is now acknowledged, not theoretical.* First quarter 2026: "we are starting to see inflation on commodities as well as freight," and the guidance "assumes continued elevated energy prices and inflation through the balance of the year." For a valve, pump and sensor manufacturer, rising copper, steel and energy prices are a cost headwind that must be recovered through price with a lag — management said explicitly that the next three quarters' margins would be "slightly down versus the first quarter" for exactly this reason.
- *The non-GAAP definition changed at the moment of maximum convenience* (section 4).
- *Half the balance sheet is purchase price.* $1,993 million of goodwill and intangibles against $2,178 million of equity. If the acquired businesses disappoint, the write-down is large and the equity base is thin.
- *Insiders are not buying.* Over the last year, open-market activity was a single purchase of 150 shares against a single sale of 2,693 shares. The screener's higher "insider buy ratio" is inflated by stock grants and should be ignored.
- *Cash conversion is the weak link.* Free cash flow of $280 million supports a 2.2% yield at this price, and capital expenditure of $48.5 million is only 61% of depreciation — the company is not reinvesting heavily in its own plant; growth comes from the cheque book.

**Positive**

- *Five consecutive guidance raises* across two fiscal years, and seven earnings beats in seven quarters.
- *They pre-cut their own assumption in public* (April 2026, commercial aftermarket) and still raised earnings — then reversed the cut three months later. That is the opposite of narrative management.
- *The margin expansion survives the unadjusted test.* Reported operating margin rose 210 basis points year on year in the second quarter of 2026, more than the adjusted figure.
- *The acquisitions are working faster than promised.* Accretion guidance went from about $0.15 to about $0.20 per share within six months, with margins at the acquired businesses "substantially improved from last year and ahead of our plan." The purchase multiple — roughly 17.7 times the target's 2025 earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation on approximately $390 million of sales — was full, so the improvement is doing real work.
- *Named customers, not pilots.* Space-launch cryogenic projects with SpaceX and Blue Origin secured in the second quarter of 2026; content selected on the GE RISE engine programme; a brake control system for the Otto Aerospace Phantom 3500; orders received for the PAC-3 missile and LTAMDS radar programmes; the F-16 brake retrofit at roughly $30 million a year with orders already in backlog "in excess of that annual rate."
- *Leverage discipline through a large deal.* Net debt peaked around $860 million after the January purchases and was paid down to $765 million by June, keeping leverage below the company's own 2-3 times target range.
- *A clean, well-signalled leadership handover.* Alcala had been chief operating officer for years and the succession was announced a full quarter ahead of the April 2026 effective date.

## 7. Hidden signals

- **The segment rename is a strategy statement.** Dropping "Electronics" for "Advanced Technologies" was explained as intent "to selectively and carefully widen our aperture in this segment." Translated: the acquisition mandate has been loosened beyond aerospace, into land-based defence, industrial gas turbines and aeroderivative markets. That is more optionality and more integration risk at the same time.
- **The deal pipeline shrank, then the appetite for leverage grew.** In July 2025 the pipeline stretched to $1 billion; by October 2025 it was "$100 million to $500 million." Yet in January 2026 Maue said Crane would go "to 3x even strategically… or even above that for the right deal" against a 2-3 times target. Smaller deals, larger stated risk tolerance.
- **A new voice, the same script.** Alcala's first calls as chief executive are stylistically indistinguishable from Mitchell's — same operating-system vocabulary, same closing celebrity quotation. Continuity, not change. That reduces execution risk and also reduces the odds of a portfolio shake-up or a capital-return pivot.
- **Automation was gently deflated.** Asked in October 2025 about factory automation and its margin potential, Mitchell declined the easy answer: "There is no one Crane site that I can think of that would have that type of vision… it tends to be very spot-based." A promoter would have taken that question and run. He did not. That is a credibility marker.
- **The pricing lever is disclosed with unusual specificity.** Long-term contract exposure at the acquired units — about 30% at Druck, about 40% at Reuter-Stokes, "very low" at Panametrics — tells you roughly two-thirds of the acquired revenue can be repriced quickly. That is the mechanical source of the faster-than-planned margin improvement, and it is a finite, one-time lever.

## 8. Capital-allocation verdict

**Trajectory: strengthening.** Every operating and financial series is pointing the right way — revenue, margin, order book, guidance delivery, and the integration of a large acquisition. The management team is credible, specific, and has repeatedly set expectations below what it delivered.

**The single most important thing to weigh before sizing a position: the price already contains all of it.** Nothing in this file is a warning about the business. The warning is about the entry. At $224.20 the shares sit within 3% of their all-time high, 84% of the way up the 52-week range, 14% above the 200-day average, on about 32 times adjusted 2026 earnings and a 2.2% free cash flow yield, with reported earnings materially below the adjusted headline. Wall Street's consensus target of $246.67 (three estimates in the last quarter, the newest five days old) implies only about 10% upside — the street is not looking the wrong way here; it is looking at the same thing and finding little room. Meanwhile the market's own reaction to a beat-and-raise on 29 July 2026 was to sell the stock roughly 7% — the crowd is already fully fed on this story, even though the dip was bought back within a week.

A second, narrower point for anyone underwriting this as a nuclear or small-modular-reactor investment: it is not one. Reuter-Stokes contributes roughly $90 million of an approximately $2.9 billion company, and the small-modular-reactor angle inside that is a partnership with an unnamed firm on an unstated date. Crane is an aerospace, defence and process-flow compounder that happens to own a good nuclear instrumentation business. Underwrite it as such.

**Economic moat: narrow, and stable.** The moat type is switching costs reinforced by regulatory qualification. Crane's parts are sole-sourced onto aircraft platforms, safety-qualified into nuclear plant fluid systems, and specified into cryogenic and municipal water installations — once designed in, they are expensive and slow to replace, which is why the company can hold a 42% gross margin and 26% segment operating margins. The evidence supports narrow rather than wide: gross margin is expanding (37.2% to 42.2% over three years) and reported operating margin is at a record, which argues against erosion; but return on invested capital is only 10.0% because so much capital is acquisition goodwill, and the fastest-growing segment's own margin declined 80 basis points in the latest quarter. That combination — real pricing power, mediocre returns on the price paid for it — is a narrow moat holding its width, not a wide one.

**Secular threat: manageable.** There is no structural force visibly eroding the terminal value here. Aerospace fleets, defence electronics, municipal water, cryogenic gas handling and nuclear plant instrumentation are all growing or stable end markets, and the artificial-intelligence displacement risk that stalks software and services businesses does not reach a company that sells physically-qualified hardware into safety-critical systems. The genuine risks are cyclical (aerospace aftermarket, chemical capital spending) and self-inflicted (overpaying for the next acquisition), not terminal. The cheapness question does not arise — the shares are not cheap, so this is neither a fear-print nor a value trap. It is a good business at a full price.
