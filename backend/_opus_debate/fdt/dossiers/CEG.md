# Constellation Energy (CEG) — a deep dive on management and the setup

Forensic review of five consecutive management calls (May 2025, August 2025, November 2025, the March 2026 business and earnings outlook, and May 2026) against the reported financials and the tape as of 4 August 2026. Price used throughout: **$268.73 (US dollars, Nasdaq listing)**, a live quote taken today; the stock is down about 35% from its 52-week high of $412.70 and sits roughly 18% above the 52-week low of $228.63 set on 1 July 2026.

---

## 1. Narrative arc

The story management told changed shape three times in fifteen months, and the change is the most useful thing in the transcripts.

**Phase one (May 2025) — proving the pricing model.** The chief executive, Joe Dominguez, spent his prepared remarks arguing that Constellation does not need a data centre physically bolted onto a power plant to get paid well for its electricity. His words: the company "weren't sure about that at the beginning of this strategic effort," and had worried that fair pricing "might only be available in behind-the-meter transactions." By that call he declared himself "now convinced" that pricing works either way. This is a genuine thesis expansion — it widens the addressable customer set from the handful of sites with adjacent land to the whole regional grid.

**Phase two (August–November 2025) — the promise of a large deal.** Here the narrative narrowed onto a single un-named transaction. In August 2025 Dominguez reached for a baseball metaphor: "we're in the late innings on one transaction. We're past the seventh inning stretch." In November 2025 he repeated it almost verbatim — "That remains true, and we continue to progress" — while adding, with unusual candour, that "some of the later innings can seemingly drag out." Alongside this he added a genuinely new and, in my view, under-appreciated claim: the buyer had matured. "Today, we're seeing a far more sophisticated and aggressive customer walk through our door. They have done deals."

**Phase three (March–May 2026) — retreat to the numbers.** At the March 2026 outlook the deal did not arrive. Dominguez said it plainly: "I recognize that the last time we spoke, I indicated that we expected to be done with an important transaction by this call, but we're not ready to announce anything today." The narrative was rebuilt on arithmetic instead of anticipation — a stated 20% compound annual growth rate in base earnings through 2029, a commitment to at least 10% growth on a rolling three-year basis thereafter, a free-cash-flow outlook of $8.4 billion across 2026–2027 rising to $11.5–13 billion across 2028–2029, and a buyback authority lifted to $5 billion.

That is the arc: from *can we price it*, to *the big one is coming*, to *stop waiting for the big one, here is the baseline*. Growth drivers quietly dropped along the way include behind-the-meter co-location, which by August 2025 had become "a longer-term option" with the admission that "what we're working on right now is all front of the meter." Drivers quietly added include natural gas and geothermal (through the Calpine acquisition), nuclear power uprates — squeezing more megawatts out of existing reactors — and, most recently, a venture stake in small modular reactor developer Blue Energy announced on 16 July 2026.

## 3. Tone and confidence trajectory

**Rating: Stable, with a marked shift from anticipation to evidence.**

- May 2025 — offensive and expansive: "we're seeing a very, very favorable environment." Specificity about deals: low. Confidence: high.
- August 2025 — peak anticipation. "We're past the seventh inning stretch. We've song take me out to the ballpark on that one." Also the most quotable overreach of the whole sequence: "clean and reliable megawatts are the most important energy commodity in the world today."
- November 2025 — the metaphor is recycled and hedged: "some of the later innings can seemingly drag out." Simultaneously the market claim escalates: "the market is hotter now than ever." That combination — softer on the specific, louder on the general — is the classic tell that a promised event is slipping.
- March 2026 — the tone breaks. Management stops selling anticipation and starts selling arithmetic, and does so with an explicit acknowledgement of the missed promise. Guidance specificity rises sharply: a three-year growth rate, a rolling growth commitment, a two-period free-cash-flow forecast, a named buyback authority.
- May 2026 — steady and operational. "Right now, our focus is on execution." Two plants placed in service in the quarter with named capacities and locations.

There is no shift into defensive cost-and-macro language. There is a shift from *offensive about the future* to *offensive about the installed base*, which is a healthier place to be arguing from.

## 4. Guidance credibility

**Reliability score: Medium-High.**

What they delivered:
- The Crane Clean Energy Center restart (the former Three Mile Island Unit 1) was *pulled forward*, not pushed back — from 2028 to the second half of 2027, announced August 2025. Dominguez: "people were skeptical that we would restart in 2028. They were right to be skeptical. We're going to start it in '27."
- The Calpine acquisition closed, having been guided to close by end-2025 and completed on 7 January 2026. Approximately on time.
- The 2026 earnings guidance range of $11 to $12 per share was set in March 2026 and affirmed in May 2026.
- Two named projects entered service on schedule in the first quarter of 2026: the 105 megawatt Pastoria solar project and the 460 megawatt Pin Oak Creek gas peaking facility.
- Earnings have beaten expectations in five of the last seven quarters, roughly 71%.

What they missed:
- The large data-centre contract, promised as near-complete in August 2025 and still unannounced in May 2026. Four quarters of slippage on the single item the market cared most about.

The pattern is not sandbagging. Management sets deliverable operational dates and hits them; it sets no date at all on commercial negotiations and then narrates progress. The failure mode here is narrating a negotiation as though it were a schedule.

One structural caution for anyone reading the headline earnings line: reported accounting earnings and the adjusted figure management guides to diverge violently, because the hedge book is marked to market each quarter. First quarter 2026: $4.49 reported against $2.74 adjusted. First quarter 2025: $0.38 reported against $2.14 adjusted. The reported number is noise. Judge this company on adjusted operating earnings and on cash flow.

## 5. Analyst pressure points

**The deflection, repeated.** Every call, an analyst asks where the large deal stands; every call, management declines. Paul Zimbardo of Jefferies in May 2025: "is it a fair expectation that kind of you're pretty close to a deal?" Answer: "I don't want to touch that." In August 2025 the same question produced the baseball metaphor. Analysts who kept pushing were vindicated — the deal did not arrive, and the stock lost about 35% of its value in the first half of 2026.

**The question that later became bad news.** In May 2025 Angie Storozynski of Seaport asked about "quite a dramatic pullback" in forward power prices in Northern Illinois and whether data centres were shifting away from the state. Dominguez dismissed the state-specific reading. She was pointing at the right thing from the wrong angle: the risk was not Illinois politics, it was that forward power prices and the location of demand are both less settled than the narrative implied.

**The question analysts are asking that management is not fully answering.** In November 2025 Storozynski asked whether rival announcements were "more equivalent to LOIs than firm take-or-pay power contracts." Dominguez answered about customer interest, not about contract structure: "I feel that what we have and what we're offering outcompetes just about any other opportunity in the space." He never addressed the distinction. That distinction — a letter of intent versus a binding take-or-pay contract — is the whole game in this sector, and management chose not to draw it even when handed the chance.

**Where management gave a genuinely useful answer.** In May 2026 Nicholas Amicucci of Evercore asked how much of the uprate programme is inside the 2029 plan. Chief financial officer Shane Smith: "None of the uprates. The capital is out the door, but nothing is showing up as EPS accretion in '29. 2030 is the earliest that they'll add to EPS." That is the opposite of capacity inflation — spending disclosed, benefit explicitly excluded from the plan. Credit where due.

## 6. Red flags and green flags

**Red flags**

- *Serial slippage on the flagship commercial catalyst* (August 2025 through May 2026). Four quarters of "late innings" language with nothing signed at that scale. This is the sector's characteristic failure, and it happened here.
- *Refusal to distinguish binding contracts from framework agreements* (November 2025), when directly invited to.
- *Self-marking the Calpine acquisition at twice cost* (May 2025). Promoter language in an otherwise measured executive.
- *Grid readiness, not plant readiness, is the binding constraint on the Crane restart.* PJM identified transmission upgrades running to roughly December 2030 or later. This was materially mitigated on 1 June 2026 when the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission granted a waiver permitting the transfer of 760 megawatts of capacity interconnection rights from the retired Eddystone plant to Crane — but it took a regulatory exception, not an engineering fix, and that is a precedent that can be litigated.
- *Plaintiff-firm securities investigations announced 7–8 April 2026*, following the share-price decline. These are routine after a large drawdown and no complaint of substance is evidenced in the materials; note it, do not weight it heavily.
- *Free cash flow of $1.14 billion over the trailing twelve months* against a promise of roughly $4.2 billion a year. Explainable by growth spending, but unproven.
- *Data caveat, not a management flag:* the screening feed reports a gross margin of 75.8% and expanding. That figure is corrupted — the fourth-quarter 2025 filing feed carries a negative cost of revenue of $10.25 billion and a gross profit of $15.70 billion on $5.46 billion of revenue, an accounting-tagging artefact around the acquisition. Do not use the reported gross margin for this name. Use operating cash profit margin, which is clean and expanding.

**Green flags**

- *This company is not a serial diluter* — the opposite. The share count went from 313 million to 359 million entirely through the 50 million shares issued as acquisition currency for Calpine. The 11 million share offering priced at $281 on 1 June 2026 was a *secondary* sale by Calpine's former owners; Constellation received no proceeds and simultaneously bought stock back. Add a $5 billion repurchase authority and $735 million of actual repurchases across 2025–2026. For a sector where the standard playbook is issuing equity into a narrative, this is a meaningful differentiator.
- *Dated, named commercial wins did keep landing* while the flagship deal slipped: a 20-year agreement with Meta covering more than 1,100 megawatts at the Clinton plant (announced 2025); a carbon-free energy agreement with Comcast (August 2025); a federal government agreement (January 2025); and on 23 June 2026 a first-of-its-kind agreement with Walmart for approximately 176 megawatts from the Dresden plant, including 30 megawatts of new uprate capacity, across two fifteen-year terms beginning in 2029 and 2030.
- *Licence extension work is real and filed*: applications lodged 26 June 2026 to run the Ginna and Nine Mile Point Unit 1 reactors to 2049.
- *Uprate figures have been consistent and engineered*, not marketed: roughly 900 to 1,000 megawatts identified with completed engineering, concentrated at LaSalle, Limerick and Calvert Cliffs, with Byron and Braidwood in the plan.
- *Capacity revenue is now contracted, not hoped for*: the PJM auction for the 2028/2029 delivery year cleared at the $325 per megawatt-day ceiling on 14 July 2026.

## 7. Hidden signals

**The most important terminology shift** is from "behind the meter" to "front of the meter." In May 2025 co-location was the presumed structure. By August 2025: "what we're working on right now is all front of the meter." This is usually read as a negative — the premium structure did not work. I read it as a positive for durability and a negative for price: selling into the grid is a lower-friction, lower-ceiling business than selling a dedicated plant to a single hyperscaler at scarcity pricing.

**The second shift is the arrival of a new chief financial officer.** Dan Eggers led the finance commentary through November 2025; Shane Smith led it from March 2026. The change in what finance chooses to disclose is stark. Smith gave a three-year growth rate, a rolling growth commitment, a two-period free-cash-flow forecast, and — the most telling item — an explicit statement that the buyback is *excluded* from the growth target: "there is no benefit in the 20% base EPS CAGR from capital allocation for the share repurchase. So that is all upside." A finance chief who strips buyback accretion out of a headline growth number is not inflating the number.

**On capital allocation, the discipline language sharpened.** Smith, May 2026: "we won't make an ill-informed investment decision because we feel the money has got to go somewhere." Paired with a stated hurdle — growth projects at "double-digit unlevered returns" — and a stated willingness to buy stock instead when that hurdle is not met. That is the correct framework for a mid-build capital-intensive business, stated out loud.

**A subtle admission worth noticing.** In November 2025, asked whether the company would hold back capacity to capture scarcity pricing later, Dominguez said "we're not at a point where we're even entertaining the discussion of, hey, are we going to stop selling long term." Many investors value Constellation's uncontracted nuclear output as a free option on scarcity. Management has told you it intends to sell that option rather than hold it. That materially undercuts the most aggressive versions of the bull case.

## 8. Capital-allocation verdict

**Trajectory: Strengthening.**

The earnings base is larger, more diversified and better contracted than it was fifteen months ago. Revenue for the trailing twelve months reached $29.9 billion, up 23% year on year; operating cash profit margin runs at 27.6%, near the top of the peer cohort; net debt of $21.30 billion sits at 2.58 times operating cash profit, which is conservative against merchant-power peers running at five to seven-and-a-half times. Return on equity for the trailing twelve months is 20.1%.

**The single most important thing to weigh before sizing a position:** return on invested capital is 3.8% and return on capital employed 5.9%. Those are poor absolute returns. The kind explanation is that the Calpine capital base landed in full while only one quarter of Calpine earnings sits in the trailing numerator. The unkind explanation is that management paid a full price for a gas fleet and is telling you it was worth twice that. Which explanation is right will be visible in the returns line by mid-2027, and it is the difference between a compounding franchise and an expensive one. Do not size this position on the assumption that the kind explanation wins — size it so that either can be true.

**Economic moat: wide.** The moat type is a regulatory licence combined with scale-cost advantage. You cannot replicate the largest nuclear fleet in the United States at any price; new nuclear takes a decade and the replacement cost of even a new gas plant has risen from roughly $2,500 to roughly $3,000 per kilowatt on management's own account. Operating licences now run toward 2049 on filed applications. Federal nuclear production tax credit support, restated and strengthened in 2025 legislation, provides an effective revenue floor in the region of $40 to $44 per megawatt-hour on nuclear output — a genuine truncation of the downside, though far out of the money at today's power prices.

**Moat trend: stable, not widening.** Expanding operating margin argues for widening; a return on invested capital of 3.8% on a freshly stepped-up capital base does not permit that call yet. Revisit after two more quarters of combined-company reporting.

**Secular threat: none.** The structural force in this industry — electrification and artificial-intelligence-driven load growth — points toward this company, not away from it. The genuine risk is cyclical and political, not terminal: a digestion phase in data-centre capital spending, and a regulatory or political response to consumer electricity bills that caps the pricing the scarcity thesis depends on. The July 2026 auction clearing at an administratively set ceiling is exactly that risk made visible. A ceiling is protection for consumers and a lid on shareholders.
