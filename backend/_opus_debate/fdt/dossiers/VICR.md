# Vicor Corporation (VICR) — a deep dive on management and the setup

*Prepared 4 August 2026. Share price used throughout: $229.56 (Nasdaq, US dollars, live quote 4 August 2026). Six consecutive quarterly earnings calls reviewed, from the first quarter of 2025 (29 April 2025) through the second quarter of 2026 (21 July 2026), cross-checked against the reported financial statements through the quarter ended 30 June 2026.*

---

## 1. Narrative arc

Vicor makes high-density power-conversion modules — the components that step high-voltage electricity down to the roughly one volt that an artificial-intelligence processor actually runs on. Over these six calls the story changed shape twice, and both changes are important.

**Where it started (spring 2025): a company apologising for its own factory.** In April 2025 the tone was defensive. Revenue was $94.0 million for the quarter, gross margin — the share of each sales dollar left after manufacturing cost — had just fallen 520 basis points to 47.2%, and the chief financial officer spent most of his prepared remarks explaining why: a switch to new enterprise software, a mandatory paid week off in December, outside consultants, and new depreciation from a semiconductor plant in Andover, Massachusetts that was running well below capacity. Management refused to give quarterly guidance, calling 2025 "a year of uncertainty and opportunity." The forward-looking material was almost entirely about products that did not yet exist: a second-generation vertical power delivery system, an 800-volt module that would "begin sampling" later in the year, and a claimed addressable market "expected to exceed $5 billion by 2028."

**The first shift (mid-2025): patents became a revenue line.** The June 2025 quarter carried a $45 million patent-litigation settlement. That single item lifted reported gross margin to 65.3% and reported earnings to $0.91 a share, and it changed how management talked about the business. By October 2025 the chief executive was describing licensing as a business in its own right — "in Q3 we reached a licensing revenue run rate of nearly $90 million per year" — and framing patent enforcement as strategy rather than housekeeping. Royalty revenue for the 2025 full year was $57.4 million, up 23.2% from $46.6 million in 2024.

**The second shift (2026): the factory finally started filling, and the goalposts moved.** This is the genuinely new information. Bookings inflected. The one-year backlog — orders scheduled to ship within twelve months — went from $176.9 million at the end of 2025 to $300.6 million in March 2026 to $379.7 million in June 2026, an increase of about 145% against the $155.2 million reported a year earlier. Management abandoned its long refusal to guide and put out real numbers: roughly $126 million for the June quarter (delivered $143.4 million), then "over $600 million" for 2026 as a whole.

At the same moment, the destination changed. The $1 billion revenue and 65% gross-margin target set in 2023 — a target the company has not yet reached, with trailing twelve-month revenue of $474.0 million — was replaced in July 2026 by a new one: "$2.5 billion in revenues at 70% gross margins" and a 40% operating margin. No date was attached. That is a roughly five-fold revenue target introduced before the previous target was met, and a director must read it as ambition rather than as a plan.

**What was quietly dropped.** Two things. First, the outside-manufacturing partner. Through 2025 management repeatedly described discussions with an alternate source that would let Vicor serve a market "there is no way that Vicor alone could do." By July 2026 that had become "there will likely be more discussions," with focus shifted to a second company-owned plant instead — a slower, more capital-hungry answer to the same problem. Second, the confident tariff commentary of 2025 has simply disappeared from the script.

## 3. Tone and confidence trajectory

**Rising, and by a wide margin — with a promoter's edge that has grown alongside it.**

- *April 2025 (defensive):* "the quarterly and annual outcome... is subject to a relatively wide range of scenarios... we are unable to provide quarterly guidance." On tariffs: "that's rearview mirror. And the environment's changing rapidly."
- *July 2025 (mixed):* still no guidance, but the first assertive competitive language appears, dismissing rivals' 800-volt announcements as "a lot of announcements there, but there's a long way from that to having a real high performance... solution" and citing "a good deal of naivety" in the industry.
- *October 2025 (offensive):* "our past portfolio is land mine. We began to see the effect of people stepping over the perimeter of that land mine field." Licensing framed as scaling "from $90 million... to a couple of $100 million dollars. We have line of sight to that within a couple of years."
- *February 2026 (specific):* first hard numbers in two years. "We see the demand getting to a run rate that would utilize 80% or so of the capacity in facility... that's pretty much baked in at this point based on bookings."
- *April 2026 (confident):* revenue guidance reinstated for the quarter and the year, book-to-bill above 2.
- *July 2026 (expansive):* the $2.5 billion target, a second plant sized at "as much as 2x, potentially 3x the first fab," and "the industry has no solution for these requirements."

The move from defensive language (efficiency, absorption, cost) to offensive language (capacity, expansion, enforcement) is real and is corroborated by the bookings data. What a director should mark is the parallel rise in absolutist framing — "I'm not aware of any other company that can address the road map requirements," "companies with an ethical backbone should do the right thing" — from a founder-chief executive who owns roughly 8.35 million shares, about 18% of the company, and who therefore faces no internal check on the rhetoric.

## 4. Guidance credibility

**Rated High on delivery, Medium on informativeness.**

The delivery record is genuinely good. In October 2025 the chief executive said he had "stuck my neck out" a year earlier calling 2025 a record year; it was one. In February 2026 he said the year's product revenue growth was "pretty much baked in... based on bookings that we received"; the March and June quarters both came through. The April 2026 guide of roughly $126 million for the June quarter was beaten by 14%, and the annual guide was raised from about $570 million to over $600 million within one quarter. The screener's beat record of six out of the last seven quarters is consistent with this.

Two qualifications matter. First, for six consecutive quarters (spring 2025 through early 2026) the company gave *no* quarterly guidance at all, so a large part of the "delivery" record is a record of beating the market's guesses rather than management's own. Second, the beats have leaned on items that are not shipments: a $45 million settlement in mid-2025, and a $15 million accounting-driven royalty recognition in mid-2026 that the chief financial officer explicitly warned will fall to $5 million in the September quarter before stepping to $10 million a quarter thereafter. Management deserves credit for disclosing that step-down clearly and unprompted — that is honest reporting of a lumpy line.

There is a mild sandbagging pattern: the 2026 guide is explicitly built on the assumption that "we will not enter into new licensing agreements until our second [trade case] gets to its final determination in 2027." Any new licence is therefore upside to the guide by construction.

## 5. Analyst pressure points

**The question management will not answer: who is the customer?** In July 2026 Craig-Hallum's Richard Shannon asked plainly whether the lead computing customer was a 10%-of-revenue customer in the June quarter. The answer was "I think we'll disclose that in the Q, I don't know that it would have been... I don't know that I can comment right now." In the same call D&B Capital's John Dillon said his channel checks pointed to Avago, Google and AMD, and asked whether the lead customer would be 25–30% of next year's business; the reply was "I'm not going to make specific comments with respect to customers." Six calls in a row contain no customer named by the company. Every named counterparty in this file comes from an analyst, not from Vicor.

**The question that turned into bad news.** In April 2025 Vicor said its first-quarter book-to-bill ratio was "well above 1" and backlog was up 10.4%. One quarter later the same metric was below 1, backlog fell 9.6%, and the explanation was "order cancellations from customers in China and widespread order placement hesitancy around tariffs." That is direct evidence that this backlog is cancellable, and it is the single best-documented downside precedent in the record.

**The question analysts keep circling and management deflects.** Timing of design wins. In July 2026 Needham's Quinn Bolton asked whether second-generation vertical power delivery would secure ramping designs with a hyperscaler or other large customer "over, say, the next 12 to 18 months." The answer: "I'm not going to make commitments with respect to specific days." The nearest thing to a date came from the sales chief: engagement "the rest of this year," programmes evolving into production "late third quarter, fourth quarter of next year." That is a soft, unbinding schedule for the largest part of the growth case.

**What analysts worry about that management is not addressing.** Two items. The product gross margin question had to be pulled out by an analyst rather than volunteered. And when Craig-Hallum pushed on the arithmetic of the new target — a second plant two to three times the first should support far more than $2.5 billion — the answer was about staging construction, not about the target.

## 6. Red flags and green flags

**Red flags**

- *Product margin moving the wrong way (July 2026).* Consolidated margin rose 280 basis points while product margin fell roughly 200 basis points. In a supply-constrained niche, expanding margin on growing volume is pricing power; compressing product margin on growing product volume is volume bought with price. Management's explanation — under-absorption plus one-off equipment relocation — is plausible for a plant mid-expansion, but it is an explanation, not yet a reversal.
- *Reported profit running well ahead of cash (December 2025 through June 2026).* Three of the last four quarters carried a tax benefit rather than a tax charge. Free cash flow of $51.3 million over twelve months against $145.3 million of reported net income.
- *A new target replacing an unmet one (July 2026).* $2.5 billion and 70% gross margin, undated, replacing a $1 billion and 65% target the company has not reached.
- *Adversarial framing as strategy.* "Catch me if you can," "land mine," "unscrupulous competitors," "companies with an ethical backbone." The enforcement campaign is real and has produced real money, but the register is closer to promotion than to reporting.
- *No named customers and no confirmed concentration.* Combined with an analyst's unrebutted suggestion of 25–30% concentration in a single private wafer-scale computing customer, this is the largest unmeasured risk in the file.
- *Insiders sell, never buy.* The chief executive sold shares across 2 and 6 July 2026 at prices between about $302 and $328; a director sold on 23 July 2026 at $224.72. The screener's open-market record shows zero buys against 86 sales. The chief executive's sales are trivial against his 8.35 million-share holding, so this is a soft signal — but it points one way.
- *Growing share count.* Diluted shares went from 45.08 million in June 2025 to 47.71 million in June 2026, up about 5.8%, as options struck between roughly $19 and $68 are exercised against a $229 share price. Option proceeds of $26.4 million came in during the June 2026 quarter alone.

**Green flags**

- *Bookings are real and broad.* Backlog up 145% year over year to $379.7 million, attributed by management "relatively little" to licensing and instead to existing customers across computing, industrial and test equipment, and aerospace and defence.
- *Buybacks stopped at the right time.* Vicor repurchased about $17.5 million of stock in the June 2025 quarter and $15.4 million in September 2025, then went to essentially nothing in 2026 as the shares rose roughly five-fold. Buying when cheap and stopping when dear is the correct instinct and is rarer than it should be.
- *The balance sheet funds the expansion.* Cash of $453.6 million against about $8 million of total debt, plus a $14.3 million refund received on 13 July 2026 under the US semiconductor manufacturing tax credit with more expected. The second plant does not require outside money.
- *Capital spending is deliberately staged.* "We do not want to create unnecessary or premature depreciation" and "a couple of steps to begin with" is the language of an operator who has been burned by an under-utilised plant and does not intend to repeat it. Capital spending is 7.0% of revenue.
- *Enforcement converts to cash.* A $45 million settlement, a licence signed in May 2026 worth $60 million over two years, and royalty revenue up 23.2% in 2025. This is not a promise; it has landed.
- *Operating margin genuinely inflected.* From minus 0.4% for 2024, to 18.1% for 2025, to 24.3% in the June 2026 quarter.

## 7. Hidden signals

**The licence structure is the buried disclosure.** In July 2026 the chief executive described two licence types: proportional royalties tied to actual usage, and — more recently — "two-year deals that are, in effect, all-inclusive," short because "it would be very difficult, if not impossible, to predict their level of business five, 10 years down the road." Needham followed up: the second licence with the first licensee and the newest licence both run out around the end of 2027 and must be re-signed. So the highest-margin, fastest-growing part of Vicor is a set of two-year contracts that expire together, in a year when the leverage that produced them — a trade-case exclusion order — may or may not be renewed by a second case. That is a materially shorter-duration asset than the phrase "recurring royalty income" suggests.

**A quiet strategic retreat, dressed as a shift in emphasis.** The outside manufacturing partner that was described in 2025 as necessary to capture the market has become "a shift with respect to relative focus, not a change with respect to long-term strategy." In plain terms: the partner talks did not close, so Vicor is building the capacity itself, which takes longer and costs more.

**A new admission on the competitive frame.** In July 2026 management said two companies had approached Vicor to supply "a building block that is critical to deployment" of the competing integrated voltage regulator architecture — and called it "an incremental opportunity." Richard Shannon caught the implication immediately: supplying the rival architecture may substitute for, rather than add to, the full Vicor solution. Management's answer conceded that integrated regulators have real advantages in flexibility. That is the first acknowledgment in six calls that the competing approach may win sockets.

**No management changes.** The same three voices — founder-chief executive Patrizio Vinciarelli, chief financial officer Jim Schmidt, and sales chief Phil Davies — run every call in the file. There is no new voice offering a different framing, and with an 18% founder holding there is no governance path by which one arrives.

## 8. Capital-allocation verdict

**Trajectory: strengthening.** This is not a manufactured turnaround. Revenue is accelerating (June quarter up 26.9% sequentially), the order book is up about 145% year over year, operating margin has moved from roughly break-even to the mid-twenties in percentage terms, the balance sheet holds net cash of about $446 million with no debt, and the plant that was a margin drag for three years is now approaching full use. The patent-enforcement campaign has produced cash, not just press releases.

**The single most important thing to weigh before sizing a position: the price already assumes the plan works.** At $229.56 the shares carry an enterprise value — market value plus debt less cash — of about $10.5 billion against $474.0 million of trailing revenue and $127.6 million of trailing earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation. That is roughly 21 times sales and 78 times cash earnings. Monolithic Power Systems, the closest listed comparison and a company earning nearly twice Vicor's return on invested capital at four times the revenue base, trades at about 20 times sales and 64 times cash earnings. Vicor is the most expensive name in its own competitive set while earning a 10.5% return on invested capital. The operating case is improving; the valuation case has already been paid for and then some. The relevant question for a director is not whether Vicor is a good business — it plainly is — but whether $229 leaves anything for the buyer if the 2027 design-win schedule slips by two quarters, which on management's own soft language it easily could.

**Moat.** Narrow, and widening. The type is intangible-asset based: a patent estate the company is actively and successfully enforcing, wrapped around a proprietary three-dimensional interconnect process that is manufactured in a plant Vicor owns outright. The evidence supports "widening" rather than "wide": gross margin expanded from 51.2% to 58.0% over two years, operating margin went from negative to 24.3%, and return on equity is 19.8% and rising. It does not yet support "wide," because return on invested capital is only 10.5%, product-only gross margin is currently compressing, and the moat's cash expression — the royalty line — sits on two-year contracts that must be renegotiated in 2027.

**Secular threat.** None of the structural forces that permanently shrink an earnings base applies here. Vicor sits on the correct side of electrification and artificial-intelligence infrastructure spending, and rising demand for power density is a tailwind for its physics, not a threat to it. The genuine risks are competitive (rivals designing around the patents, or the integrated-regulator architecture winning enough sockets to cap the opportunity) and cyclical (a digestion phase in data-centre capital spending, which the June 2025 cancellations show this order book is not immune to). Those are real, and they are priced nowhere in the current multiple — but they are not terminal-value impairments. The cheapness question does not arise: this is not cheap. It is a good franchise at a demanding price, which is a different risk from a value trap and calls for patience rather than avoidance.
