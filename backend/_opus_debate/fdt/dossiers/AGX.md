# Argan, Inc. (AGX) — Forensic Dossier

**Prepared 2026-08-04. Price used throughout: $619.70 (NYSE, US dollars), the live quote as of today. Note the metrics bundle carried $592.66, which is the prior close.**

Argan is a holding company whose main asset is Gemma Power Systems, one of a short list of contractors in the United States capable of building large combined-cycle natural-gas power plants — the kind of plant that runs 24 hours a day and is being ordered to feed data centres, reshored factories and an ageing grid. It sells engineering, procurement and construction services (usually shortened to EPC, meaning the contractor designs the plant, buys the equipment and builds it for a single agreed price). Two smaller businesses sit alongside it: an industrial field-services and metal-fabrication arm, and a small telecom and data-network arm.

The transcripts run from the fiscal-2025 year-end call on 27 March 2025 through the fiscal-2027 first-quarter call on 4 June 2026 — six consecutive quarters, all read in full.

---

## 1. Narrative arc

The story has been unusually consistent, and that consistency is itself the finding.

In March 2025 the chief executive, David Watson, framed the company around a single idea: power demand is rising for the first time in decades while a generation of gas plants retires, and only a handful of firms can build the replacements. Fifteen months later, on 4 June 2026, he was still framing it exactly that way, in almost the same words. There has been no pivot, no new addressable-market slide, no adjacent business bolted on to keep the story fresh.

What did change is the mix and the scale. In March 2025 the backlog — the dollar value of signed work not yet performed — was about $1.4 billion and split roughly 54% gas and 42% renewable. By June 2026 it was $2.8 billion and split 79% gas, 13% renewable, 8% industrial. Management narrated that rotation openly and early rather than announcing it after the fact, and in June 2026 said plainly that demand for renewable work "has softened" while keeping the capability. That is a company describing its own mix shift rather than hiding it.

Two things were introduced rather than dropped. First, the industrial arm was repositioned from a sleepy agriculture-and-petrochemical service business into a data-centre supplier: a $125 million contract awarded in November 2025 to fabricate thermal-expansion and energy-storage tanks, followed in April 2026 by a groundbreaking on a second fabrication plant in North Carolina. Second, capital returns were formalised — three consecutive annual dividend increases (the quarterly payment doubled from $0.25 to $0.50 across the period) and a buyback authorisation lifted twice, from $150 million to $200 million, with the expiry pushed out to January 2030.

Nothing material was quietly dropped. The one claim that keeps being re-issued rather than retired is the promise of new project awards, discussed in section 5.

## 2. Claims versus financials (forensic)

I tested management's four biggest claims against the reported numbers. The headline: the narrative is, if anything, *poorer* than the trajectory justifies. This management under-promises.

| Claim (and where it was made) | What the numbers say | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| "We expect to exceed last year's margin profile as we move through the year" (4 June 2025) | Full-year gross margin went from 16.1% in fiscal 2025 to 20.5% in fiscal 2026. Trailing-twelve-month gross margin through 30 April 2026 is 20.9% ($217.9M of gross profit on $1,041.9M of revenue), against 17.6% for the prior twelve months | Delivered, and then some |
| "Backlog should get significantly over $2 billion" (4 June 2025) | Reached $2.0B by July 2025, ~$3.0B by October 2025, $2.9B at the January 2026 year-end | Delivered |
| "We might expect a small decrease in overall revenues for the upcoming quarter before increasing throughout the year" (27 March 2025) | Revenue did dip, then rose every quarter: $193.7M, $237.7M, $251.2M, $262.1M, $291.0M | Delivered, with the direction called correctly a year ahead |
| "Our business model ordinarily requires a low level of capital expenditures" (4 June 2026) | Trailing-twelve-month capital spending was $5.9M on $1,041.9M of revenue — six-tenths of one percent. Even the new North Carolina plant is budgeted at only $10–13M | Verified |

**The one place where the published screening data and the company's own figures genuinely disagree, and it matters more than anything else in this file.**

The screening bundle reports net cash of $974 million, a free-cash-flow margin of 43.5% and a price-to-free-cash-flow ratio of 20.5 times. Those figures are arithmetically correct and economically misleading, and management itself supplies the correction.

At 30 April 2026 the balance sheet shows $973.6 million of cash and investments — but also $565.8 million of contract liabilities, which is customer money already collected for work not yet performed. Argan's business consumes very little of its own capital because customers pay in advance; the cash pile is largely other people's money held in trust against future work. Management does not hide this. On every call they quote a second figure they call *net liquidity*, which strips the customer float out. That figure was $421 million at 30 April 2026 — and it was $421 million at 31 January 2026 too. In other words, in a quarter that reported $111 million of free cash flow, the money that actually belongs to shareholders did not move at all, because roughly $52 million of new customer advances flowed in and $33.6 million went back out in dividends and buybacks.

The same distortion runs through the twelve-month figure. Trailing operating cash flow of $492.8 million contains roughly $312 million of working-capital inflow (+$133M, +$119M, +$64M and −$4M across the four quarters). Strip it out and underlying cash earnings are closer to $175 million, against reported net income of $161 million — a normal, healthy relationship, and about a third of the headline. **A director must value this company on roughly $170 million of owner earnings and a $421 million distributable balance, not on $487 million of free cash flow and $974 million of cash.** Using the headline figures makes the stock look roughly half as expensive as it is.

To be clear about where the credit lies: this is a flaw in the screening proxy, not a deception by the company. Argan volunteers the honest number every quarter without being asked.

Two other proxy figures are worth correcting. The scan's return on invested capital of 14.4% and return on equity of 17.7% both understate the operating business badly, because they divide by a balance sheet stuffed with cash. The operating business earns roughly $156 million of pre-tax profit on property and equipment of $18 million, goodwill of $28 million and *negative* net working capital. On the capital actually at work, returns are effectively uncapped. That is the real quality signal here.

## 3. Tone and confidence trajectory

**Rising, but in a specific and unusual direction: confidence about execution has risen while confidence about forecasting has deliberately not.**

- *27 March 2025* — when an analyst pressed on an unusually strong margin quarter, Watson volunteered the downside first: "May not see margins reflecting this quarter's levels often where a lot of things went right all at the same time." He named the prior year's Killroot project as a margin loss without being asked.
- *4 June 2025* — "we expect to exceed last year's margin profile," the single most forward-leaning statement in the six calls. It proved conservative.
- *4 September 2025* — on pricing power, a deliberately unglamorous answer: "Our approach to kind of winning and dynamically pricing projects has been consistent for the past twenty years. And we really don't pay attention to what others may be doing."
- *26 March 2026* — asked about a 29% gross margin in the power segment, he attributed it to execution *and* to a one-off: finishing Trumbull early meant "you're not sitting on the job site for an extra 2 months."
- *4 June 2026* — the most careful sentence in the file, and the one a director should weigh most: "we're in the early phases of a number of major jobs with a lot of outstanding risks to account for. So visibility into where ultimate margins end up is dependent on a lot of factors. And therefore, it's a little too early to tell."

There is no drift from offensive to defensive language, because the language was never promotional to begin with. Argan issues no revenue guidance, no earnings guidance and no margin guidance. What it guides is capacity ("10 to 12 jobs") and it has repeated that number, unchanged, for six consecutive quarters. In a sector where the standard failure mode is a contractor talking up a pipeline it cannot staff, a management team that publicly caps its own capacity and refuses to raise the cap is the opposite behaviour.

## 4. Guidance credibility

**High.**

The specific forecasts made on earlier calls and testable on later ones — the fiscal-2026 margin improvement, the path past $2 billion of backlog, the industrial segment bottoming and recovering, the shape of the revenue year — were all met or beaten. The screening data records earnings beats in seven of seven quarters. Diluted earnings per share went $1.60, $2.50, $2.17, $3.47, $3.24 across the five most recent quarters, for a trailing total of $11.38.

Is there sandbagging? Mildly, and honestly so. Watson pre-warns about weak quarters ahead of time ("we might expect a small decrease in our overall revenues for the upcoming quarter"), which lowers the bar. But he also pre-warns about *good* results being unrepeatable, which is not what a manager gaming expectations does. The pattern reads as conservatism rather than expectation management.

One important credibility marker on the sector's classic abuse. Backlog inflation — booking non-binding letters of intent as though they were contracts — is the standard way construction companies flatter their order books. Argan does the opposite. On 27 March 2025, asked what had to happen for a 1.2-gigawatt project to enter backlog, Watson said: "getting the full notice to proceed… once we get released on that job, we'll put the whole contract into project backlog. So it's not reflected in our current backlog." The job duly appeared in the following quarter's figure. Backlog is described as "fully committed projects" throughout. I found no memorandum of understanding, framework agreement or conditional award counted as backlog anywhere in six calls.

## 5. Analyst pressure points

Three questions recur, and only one is being genuinely deflected.

**Margins.** Asked at least four times, most sharply on 4 June 2026. Management will not quantify how much of the recent strength is one-off early-completion benefit versus structural. The honest answer given — blended rates "tend to be in the high teens and low 20s" with "meaningful variations" — is a range, not a number. This is a real information gap, though I read it as legitimate uncertainty in percentage-of-completion accounting rather than evasion.

**Timing of new awards — the deflection that matters.** The promise of new work has been re-issued on every call with a rolling window that keeps resetting:

| Call | The promise |
|---|---|
| 27 Mar 2025 | "add more over the next six months" |
| 4 Jun 2025 | "significantly over $2 billion in backlog later this year" |
| 4 Sep 2025 | "a few more power jobs… over the rest of the current fiscal year" |
| 4 Dec 2025 | "as we move through next year and into calendar 2027… a handful of projects" |
| 26 Mar 2026 | "a handful of jobs over the next 8 to 20 months" |
| 4 Jun 2026 | "a handful of new projects over the next 10 to 18 months" |

The 2025 promises were kept — spectacularly, with $2.5 billion of new contract value added in fiscal 2026. The 2026 promises have not yet been. Management is explicit that the timing is not theirs to control ("we don't get to control when projects start"), which is true and also means the commitment is unenforceable. A director should treat "10 to 18 months" as a soft, non-binding expectation, not a scheduled event.

**Capacity.** Asked repeatedly, answered with unusual candour: "we currently have 8 power jobs underway right now, Chris, 6 thermal and 2 renewables" (4 June 2026), down from nine at the March call as Trumbull finished. The 10-to-12 ceiling has never been raised, and Watson explicitly refuses to guide on when it might be: "we're not going to provide guidance as to what our capacity growth is going to be and when."

What analysts are *not* pushing on, and should be: the fact that roughly 90% of the $2.5 billion power backlog sits in four fixed-price gas plants, only two of whose customers are publicly named (Sandow Lakes Energy Company for the 1.2-gigawatt Texas plant, CPV for the 1.4-gigawatt Basin Ranch project; the 860-megawatt Texas job and the 700-megawatt U.S. job are unnamed). Nobody has asked what happens to margin if one of those four runs long.

## 6. Red flags and green flags

Concerning:

- **Book-to-bill has fallen below one for two consecutive quarters.** Backlog went $2.9 billion (January 2026) to $2.8 billion (April 2026) while $291 million of revenue was recognised — implying roughly $190 million of new bookings against $291 million burned. No gigawatt-class power award has been booked since the November-2025 quarter. Management confirmed on 4 June 2026 that recent additions came from "the regular cadence of new and add-on jobs in our Industrial and Teledata segments as well as various change orders" — $215 million in the January quarter and "over $125 million" in the April quarter. Useful, but an order of magnitude short of what replacing a gigawatt plant requires.
- **Insider selling is one-sided.** The bundle records 21 open-market sales totalling 170,619 shares and zero open-market purchases. On a stock that has roughly tripled, some of this is ordinary diversification — but not a single insider has bought a share at any price during the run.
- **The margin tailwind has now been spent.** The two projects that produced the early-completion benefits — Trumbull (substantially complete December 2025, finally complete in the April 2026 quarter) and the last Midwest solar-and-battery project (substantially complete in the April 2026 quarter) — are both finished. The four Texas and U.S. gas jobs that replace them are, in management's own words, in "the early phases… with a lot of outstanding risks to account for."
- **Fixed-price contracts carry the input-cost risk.** Copper, steel and gas-turbine prices are costs here, not revenues. Watson has said gas jobs are "higher risk, higher reward type jobs" and that the backlog has shifted toward fixed-price. That is the right trade in a tight market and the wrong one if input costs run.

Positive:

- **Gross margin is expanding while revenue grows** — 17.6% to 20.9% on the trailing twelve months, with revenue up 14.5%. In a supply-constrained niche that is the signature of genuine pricing power, and it is the single most important number in this file. Margin compressing on rising revenue would have meant the opposite: volume bought with price.
- **Two consecutive projects delivered ahead of schedule** (Trumbull in December 2025, the final Midwest solar project in the April 2026 quarter). In large-scale construction, finishing early is rare and is the hardest thing to fake.
- **A conservative backlog-booking policy** (full notice to proceed required), verified across quarters.
- **Zero debt, and a genuine $421 million of net liquidity** disclosed voluntarily alongside the flattering gross-cash figure.
- **No dilution.** Diluted share count went from 13.88 million to 14.20 million over eight quarters — under half a percent a year — with buybacks offsetting compensation. For a company in a hot sector with a soaring share price, not issuing stock is a decision, and the right one.
- **Capital returns rising into strength but staying small** — $33.6 million returned in the April 2026 quarter against $46 million of net income.

## 7. Hidden signals

Three subtle shifts are worth recording.

The word "renewable" has quietly changed status. In March 2025 the pitch was "Argan is energy agnostic… and we build them all," with renewables at 42% of backlog. By June 2026 renewables were 13% and the language had become defensive: demand "has softened," but "we plan to maintain renewable capabilities so we remain competitively positioned." That is a capability being preserved as insurance rather than sold as growth — an honest downgrade, delivered without spin.

The competitive framing has hardened in a specific way. Early calls said Argan is "one of only a few companies" able to build complex combined-cycle plants. By June 2026 Watson sharpened it to a size threshold: "there's only a handful of folks that can do the gigawatt-plus types of jobs. And so the competitive set for that is maybe a little smaller than, say, going after 100 to 200 to 300-megawatt type size jobs." The moat, in his own telling, lives at the top of the size range — which is also where the concentration risk lives.

The balance sheet is being described as a competitive weapon, not just a cushion: net liquidity "supports a book of business of $2.8 billion in backlog" and provides "bonding capacity" that makes Argan "a reliable and bankable EPC partner." This is credible and under-appreciated. Independent power producers cannot finance a gigawatt plant with a contractor who might not survive it, so the cash pile is a bidding qualification, not idle money. It also means the cash is genuinely less distributable than the headline suggests — reinforcing the point in section 2.

On management changes: the chief financial officer's name is transcribed inconsistently (Josh Bakr / Joshua Baugher) across calls, which is a transcription artefact, not a personnel change. Real changes were at subsidiary level (a new chief executive at the telecom arm in early 2025, a planned succession at the industrial arm in mid-2025) and were narrated in advance as part of stated succession plans.

## 8. Capital-allocation verdict

**Trajectory: strengthening.** Revenue, margin, earnings, cash generation and returns are all rising together, guidance has been met or beaten across six quarters, the balance sheet carries no debt, and the share count is essentially flat. On the evidence in the transcripts and the financials, this is one of the cleaner industrial franchises I have examined.

**The single most important thing the director must weigh before sizing a position:** this is a company whose quality is not in question and whose price is. Two facts have to be held together. First, the honest owner-earnings figure is roughly $170 million a year and the honest distributable cash balance is $421 million, not the $487 million and $974 million the screening proxy reports — which means the stock is roughly twice as expensive as the headline ratios imply. Second, the order book has stopped growing: bookings have run below revenue for two straight quarters and no gigawatt-class award has landed since November 2025, while a $2.8 billion backlog against a revenue run-rate approaching $1.2 billion gives only about two and a half years of visibility. The business needs to book roughly $2–3 billion of new work inside the next year and a half simply to hold the line, and management is candid that it does not control when that happens. At $619.70 the share price already assumes it does.

**Moat: narrow, and widening.** The moat type is a scale-and-reputation barrier: very few firms can price, bond and execute a gigawatt-scale combined-cycle plant on a fixed price, and Gemma's record — no lost job since acquisition, two consecutive early completions — is the qualification that wins the bid. The evidence says it is widening rather than eroding: gross margin rose from 17.6% to 20.9% on the trailing twelve months while revenue grew 14.5%, and returns on the capital actually employed are extraordinarily high because the business runs on customer advances rather than its own money. It is narrow rather than wide because it rests on scarcity of qualified capacity in one product during one build cycle, not on a contractual toll — Argan re-earns it bid by bid, and every competitor is hiring into the same shortage.

**Secular threat: manageable.** Gas-fired generation is currently the beneficiary of the energy transition rather than its victim, since it is what utilities buy when they need power available every hour of every day. The genuine long-term risk is not displacement but duration: this is a build cycle, and build cycles end. If small modular reactors, storage or a demand slowdown eventually reduce the need for new combined-cycle plants, Argan's earnings base shrinks — but that is a cyclical horizon measured in years, with a contracted backlog in between, not a terminal impairment. The cheapness question does not arise here in either direction: this is not a fear-print and not a value trap. It is a good business at a demanding price.
