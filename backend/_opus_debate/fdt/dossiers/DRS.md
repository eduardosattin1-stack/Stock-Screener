# Leonardo DRS, Inc. (DRS) — Forensic Dossier

**Prepared:** 2026-08-04 · **Price used:** $45.24 (Nasdaq, US dollars, live quote 2026-08-04) · **Market value:** about $12.1 billion
**Transcripts reviewed:** five consecutive quarterly calls — 30 July 2025 (Q2 2025), 29 October 2025 (Q3 2025), 24 February 2026 (Q4/full-year 2025), 5 May 2026 (Q1 2026), 30 July 2026 (Q2 2026)

Leonardo DRS builds the electronics inside American military hardware: infrared sensors and thermal cameras, tactical radars, ruggedized computers, and — through its other half — the electric power and propulsion systems that drive US Navy ships and submarines, including the nuclear-powered Columbia-class. It is roughly 73% owned by its Italian parent, Leonardo S.p.A.

---

## 1. Narrative arc

The story management tells has broadened steadily across these five calls, and — unusually — the broadening has been matched by results rather than substituting for them.

**Mid-2025: a supply-chain problem wrapped in a demand story.** The July 2025 call opened on bookings and backlog, then spent real time on a single raw material. Germanium — the metal used to make lenses for infrared cameras — had been export-restricted by China, and CEO Bill Lynn conceded that "germanium availability and pricing remain a thorny issue," that the company was "currently relying on our safety stock," and that "material flow must improve in the second half." Analyst Ronald Epstein pressed the uncomfortable version of the question: why DRS and apparently nobody else? Lynn's answer was honest and revealing — "we're a sensor house," so the exposure is structural, not an accident. He then volunteered a second vulnerability nobody had asked about: permanent magnets for the electric-propulsion business.

**Late 2025: the fix is named, and the leadership changes.** By October 2025 the germanium answer had specifics — recycling germanium out of old optics, strategic customer allocations, and supply agreements. Lynn announced his retirement after fourteen years, handing the company to Chief Operating Officer John Baylouny effective 1 January 2026. Notably, the incoming chief executive is a 40-year insider and former chief technology officer, not an outside change agent — a continuity handoff, not a rescue.

**Early 2026: investment intensity becomes the headline.** The February 2026 call reframed the story around spending rather than harvesting: internal research spending up 40% in 2025, capital spending up more than 60%, and 2026 capital spending guided toward roughly 5% of revenue. Management also cleared two items off the books in the same quarter — a ten-year, $100 million licence of laser intellectual property to a quantum-computing company, and a mutually agreed conclusion of a decade-old foreign ground-surveillance programme that produced an unanticipated loss. Chief Financial Officer Michael Dippold conceded the company would miss its 2026 Investor Day margin target, replacing it with a looser promise: "we think the business is structured to be in the mid-teens margins."

**Mid-2026: the pivot to software and the arrival of operating leverage.** The two 2026 calls are where the narrative and the numbers converge. Q1 delivered 6% revenue growth with adjusted profit up 28%; Q2 delivered 10% organic revenue growth with adjusted operating profit (adjusted EBITDA — earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation, adjusted for one-off items) up 33% and margin up 240 basis points. Alongside Q2 the company announced a $450 million all-cash acquisition of Raft LLC, a McLean, Virginia mission-software firm selected for the US Army's Next-Generation Command and Control architecture. Baylouny's framing — "Raft helps us own the edge" — is the first genuinely new growth vector introduced in this window.

**What was quietly dropped.** Germanium. It dominated two calls in 2025, appeared as a "contained" constraint in February 2026, and by July 2026 had been reduced to a one-line reassurance: "The germanium picture is a positive story. We have got a great flow of germanium. We are not gonna run out." What was never resolved is the *cost* half of the problem. In July 2025 Dippold explained the mechanism plainly — "we're a predominantly fixed-price shop. So the pricing fluctuations are being realized in our results" — and the fix was always "a program by program negotiation." In July 2026 the closest he came was "cost is always sometimes a little bit variable." The volume problem was solved; the price problem was retired from the narrative without being declared fixed.

|---|---|---|---|

| Margins are expanding | Q1–Q2 2026 | Gross margin (revenue less cost of sales, as a share of revenue) 24.4% over the last twelve months versus 23.2% a year earlier, +128 basis points, on rising revenue. | "Record" backlog | Every call | Funded backlog $5.1bn, up 17%. **Total** backlog $8,787m versus $8.6bn a year earlier — up only about 2% while revenue grew 10.5% | **Misleading emphasis** |

| Earnings per share have compounded | Implied throughout | The screener's three-year earnings-per-share compound rate of **negative 18.2% is an artefact** of a 2022 base ($1.88) that contained a large non-operating gain. On the operating series — $0.64 (2023), $0.80 (2024), $1.03 (2025), $1.34–1.39 guided (2026) — earnings have compounded at roughly 28% a year | **The deterministic screener materially understates the trajectory here** |

**The one qualifier that matters on margins.** Management did not hide it, which is to their credit, but it must be priced. Dippold volunteered on 30 July 2026 that the Q2 margin included a "favorable programmatic risk retirement" on a surface-ship programme, and quantified the clean number himself: "If I take out the risk retirement, I would think of the IMS margin kind of closer to the 15% range" — against the 16.2% reported for the first half. He then pre-announced the consequence: Q3 adjusted margin in the "mid-13%" range, a step down from Q2's 14.0%, reflecting "the absence of nonrecurring program risk retirement gains." Roughly a quarter of the celebrated 240-basis-point expansion does not repeat.

**Where the story is richer than the trajectory justifies.** Two places. First, "record backlog" is doing narrative work that the total order book does not support — funded backlog is rising because unfunded orders are being converted into funded ones (funded is now about 58% of total, against roughly 51% a year ago), not because the total order book is growing. Second, the cash story. The screener's 9.6% free-cash-flow margin is a favourable-window artefact: it captures an exceptionally strong second half of 2025 and a less-bad first half of 2026. Company guidance for 2026 is 75% conversion of adjusted net earnings into free cash flow — about $275m, or **7.0% of revenue** — while capital spending steps up to the "mid-4%" of revenue range. The first half of 2026 consumed $89m of cash. The full-year target requires roughly $364m of cash generation in the back half.

**Where the story is poorer than the trajectory justifies.** Returns. Return on invested capital has risen to 10.4% and return on equity to 11.8%. Management never talks about either, and both are still modest for a business the market is valuing at a premium multiple.

## 3. Tone and confidence trajectory

**Rising, and specifically rising in the 2026 calls.**

- *Q2 2025 — guarded.* "Thorny issue." "Material flow must improve in the second half." "We've seen mixed results from a customer receptive perspective." Hedged, problem-forward.
- *Q3 2025 — recovering.* "Early success in extracting adequate levels." "I am confident that the initiatives that we have put in place will successfully resolve this challenge in 2026." Still a promise about the future, but a dated one.
- *Q4 2025 — mixed, and defensive on margins.* "These constraints are contained." But also the concession of a missed multi-year margin target and the disclosure of a programme loss. The new chief executive's language is generic and slightly promotional in this first outing — "abundant as ever," "truly differentiated."
- *Q1 2026 — offensive.* "Meaningfully outperformed expectations." "Confidence to raise our expected growth and profitability."
- *Q2 2026 — most confident of the five, and most specific.* "Order flow continues to run ahead of supply. So we are aggressively adding capacity." On the risk of a stop-gap government funding measure: "I do not see that as a risk to DRS."

Guidance has become *more* granular over the window, not less — a reliable marker of genuine confidence. The July 2026 call gave a next-quarter revenue floor, a next-quarter margin range, a full-year cash-conversion ratio, a full-year capital-spending ratio, and a tax rate. Companies in trouble withdraw detail; this one added it.

The one place the language turned defensive is worth naming: the shift from "we expect these mitigation efforts to offer more meaningful relief in 2026" to the vaguer "cost is always sometimes a little bit variable" is a retreat, not a resolution.

## 4. Guidance credibility

**High.** The pattern across the window is consistent and conservative.

- **Raised revenue growth guidance twice in 2025** — to 9–11% in July, then to 10–11% in October — and delivered 13% organic. Over-delivery.
- **2026 revenue guidance has been held unchanged** at $3.90–3.975bn (implying 7–9% organic growth) through two beats, while adjusted profit guidance was raised twice: adjusted EBITDA from $515–530m to $525–540m, adjusted earnings per share from $1.26–1.30 to $1.34–1.39. Raising profit while holding revenue is the signature of a management team that does not want to be caught by a stop-gap funding measure it cannot control. That is discipline, not sandbagging.
- **The Q2 2026 beat was large** — adjusted earnings of $0.35 against a $0.27 consensus — consistent with the screener's record of beating in seven of the last seven quarters.
- **Bad news is disclosed, quantified, and pre-announced.** The programme loss, the missed Investor Day target, the non-repeating margin gain, and the Q3 step-down were all volunteered rather than extracted.

**The one credibility debit.** They hit quarters and miss multi-year frameworks. The 2026 Investor Day margin target was conceded as unreachable in February 2026, and the replacement — "mid-teens margins" — carries no date. Dippold deferred multi-year targets to 2027. Short-horizon reliability is high; long-horizon framework reliability is unproven.

**Reliability score: High** for one-to-four-quarter guidance; **Medium** for multi-year targets.

## 5. Analyst pressure points

**Germanium cost pass-through — asked four times across three calls, never answered.** Jon Tanwanteng of CJS asked the direct version twice in July 2025 ("when do you think you can get margins on products containing germanium back to the normalized range") and got "It's going to be a program by program negotiation." He asked again in October 2025 whether the alternative supply costs more; Dippold: "we're largely a fixed price shop. So the higher pricing is certainly going to be inherent in those programs as we look into 2026." That is a confirmation of the problem, not an answer to the question. In July 2026 the topic was answered on volume only. **This is the single most persistent non-answer in the file, and it is a live margin exposure on a fixed-price contract base.**

**The multi-year margin bridge.** Andre Madrid of BTIG pushed in February 2026 on the abandoned Investor Day target and got a directional answer with no date. This is the question the 2027 guidance will have to settle.

**Raft economics.** On the July 2026 call management declined to size the acquisition: "We are not going to comment on the sizing of the revenue yet." What they did say is quietly important and, so far as I can find, unremarked — the "EBITDA multiple paid net of the tax assets acquired, is in line with our own." DRS's own enterprise value is about 22 times 2026 guided adjusted EBITDA. That implies DRS paid roughly 22 times profit for a private software business whose revenue it will not disclose until 2027 guidance. It is multiple-neutral by construction, which is the point — but it is not cheap, and investors are being asked to wait five months for the numbers.

**What analysts worry about that management is not addressing:** the total backlog. Every question in this window is framed around bookings and funded backlog. Nobody on these calls asked why the total order book is flat.

## 6. Red flags and green flags

**Concerning**

- 🔴 **Total backlog is flat while the funded-backlog headline implies growth.** $8,787m versus $8.6bn a year earlier — about 2% — against revenue up 10.5%. Coverage has fallen from roughly 2.5 times annual revenue to about 2.3 times.
- 🔴 **A roughly $380m gap** between the backlog growth implied by eighteen quarters of book-to-bill at or above 1.0 and the backlog actually printed. The visible cause is the legacy foreign ground-surveillance programme concluded by mutual agreement in Q4 2025; whatever the remainder is, it has not been explained.
- 🔴 **Germanium's price exposure is unresolved on a fixed-price contract base**, and the second exposure Lynn volunteered in July 2025 — permanent magnets for electric propulsion — has never been revisited on a subsequent call.
- 🔴 **Roughly a quarter of the Q2 margin expansion does not repeat**, by management's own quantification, and Q3 is guided down sequentially.
- 🔴 **Cash conversion is weak and back-half loaded.** First-half 2026 free cash flow was negative $89m; the full-year target implies about $364m in the second half. Capital spending is rising to the mid-4% of revenue range.
- 🔴 **Insiders are net sellers on the open market** — one purchase of 25,000 shares against nine sales totalling 82,630 shares.
- 🔴 **A 73% parent shareholder with a demonstrated pattern of selling tranches into strength.** Leonardo S.p.A. sold down through a secondary offering in November 2023 and a further minority tranche thereafter; its chief executive told CNBC on 31 July 2026 that Leonardo intends to pursue further acquisitions in Europe. Acquisitions require funding.

**Positive**

- 🟢 **Gross margin is expanding on growing revenue — in both segments.** First-half 2026: sensing and computing at 11.4% adjusted margin (up 190 basis points), mission systems at 16.2% (up 280 basis points). In a supply-constrained niche this is the clearest available evidence of genuine pricing power rather than volume bought with price.
- 🟢 **Bad news is volunteered and quantified**, repeatedly and without prompting.
- 🟢 **The balance sheet is genuinely clean:** net cash of $121m at 30 June 2026 ($270m cash against $149m of total debt including $128m of capitalised leases), Altman-Z solvency score of 6.1, Piotroski quality score 7 of 9.
- 🟢 **Real contract wins with named counterparties and dates:** a $533m production contract vehicle for the Distributed Aperture Infrared Countermeasure System (Q1 2026), a Space Development Agency tracking-layer tranche-3 award, and a blanket purchase agreement for more than 50,000 thermal imaging cameras (15 July 2026).
- 🟢 **The Columbia-class submarine content is contracted on a multi-boat basis** — Dippold on 30 July 2026: "we were able to negotiate the long-term contract for the multiboat buy... we have been in that luxurious position of having the full contract already."
- 🟢 **A balanced, non-promotional capital allocation record:** dividend initiated in 2025 and maintained at $0.09 quarterly, a moderate buyback, patient acquisition behaviour ("We've exercised patience. We've looked at a lot of things"), and no equity issuance. This is not a serial diluter. Share count has been essentially flat at about 269m diluted for three years.

## 7. Hidden signals

**The chief financial officer is the credibility anchor, and his language is precise where the chief executive's is promotional.** Compare Baylouny's "the opportunities to create value... are abundant as ever" with Dippold's "If I take out the risk retirement, I would think of the IMS margin kind of closer to the 15% range." When a chief financial officer volunteers the clean number that makes his own quarter look worse, it is worth weighting his statements above the rest of the call.

**"Investing ahead of demand" replaced "capturing demand."** In July 2025 the framing was about converting an order book. By February 2026 it was about pre-building capacity — a 40% increase in internal research spending, capital spending up more than 60%. Management is spending as though the demand is durable. That is either conviction or the top of a cycle; the fixed-price contract base means the company carries the risk either way.

**The laser licence tells you what management thinks it is not.** Rather than chase a quantum-computing adjacency itself, DRS licensed the intellectual property for $100 million over ten years and stayed home. Baylouny: "we want to stay focused... we want to get it out of our portfolio." For a company in a sector where management teams routinely claim every adjacency, this is a meaningful discipline signal.

**Second-sourcing is the durable competitive theme, and it cuts both ways.** DRS is trying to become the *second* source for submarine steam turbine generators — "Right now, they have one" — which is upside. But the same Navy logic that creates a second source for someone else's monopoly can create one for DRS's electric-propulsion position. Management never addresses this.

**The parent relationship has moved from background to foreground.** In February 2026 Baylouny openly described using Leonardo's European footprint, its Iveco Defence acquisition and its Rheinmetall joint venture as a growth channel. That is commercially real. It also raises the governance question a 73% holder always raises: whose interests set the agenda if the two diverge.

## 8. Capital-allocation verdict

**Classification: STRENGTHENING.**

Revenue is compounding at roughly 10%, gross margin is expanding in both segments simultaneously, adjusted profit is growing at two to three times the rate of revenue, the balance sheet holds net cash, returns on invested capital are rising off a modest base, and management's disclosure behaviour is better than the sector norm. Nothing in five calls suggests a deteriorating franchise.

**The single most important thing to weigh before sizing a position: the price, not the business.**

This dossier finds an operationally strengthening company. It does not find a cheap one. At $45.24 the shares trade at roughly 33 times 2026 guided adjusted earnings, about 22 times guided adjusted operating profit on an enterprise-value basis, and roughly 44 times the free cash flow the company itself is guiding to for 2026. The comparable set has just been repriced brutally in the other direction — as of 4 August 2026, Kratos trades 61% below its 52-week high, AeroVironment 59% below, Leidos 37% below, BWX Technologies 28% below, Huntington Ingalls 30% below — while DRS sits about 11% above its 200-day average and roughly 10% below its own high. DRS is the last name in this complex still carrying a growth multiple. That divergence is the risk, and it is not a business risk.

Two secondary items compound it. The published analyst consensus of $53.33 is stale — the newest price target is 90 days old, only one target printed last quarter, and the most recent target of $54.00 was set when the stock was $39.70, so most of that upside is already in the price. And a 73% parent shareholder with an acquisition appetite in Europe and a track record of selling DRS tranches into strength is a live source of supply.

**Economic moat: NARROW, and WIDENING.**

The moat is two distinct things bolted together. The mission-systems half — naval electric power and propulsion, with contracted multi-boat Columbia-class content and a purpose-built Charleston, South Carolina facility — is a genuine switching-cost and qualification-barrier moat: warship power architectures are certified, sole-sourced, and effectively unswitchable for the life of a ship class. The sensing-and-computing half, which is about 65% of revenue, is a narrower intangible-asset moat resting on indigenous infrared detector capability, and it competes directly with L3Harris, RTX and others.

The evidence says the moat is widening, not eroding: return on invested capital is rising (9.5% to 10.4%), gross margin has expanded about 187 basis points over three years, and *both* segments expanded margin simultaneously in the first half of 2026. An eroding moat does not produce that pattern.

**Secular threat: manageable.** The structural forces reshaping this industry — autonomy, proliferated low-cost sensing, artificial intelligence at the tactical edge — run *toward* this company rather than against it. DRS makes the low-cost seekers that commoditise the expensive ones ("introducing lower-cost seekers to enable more symmetric countermeasures", Q1 2026), and the Raft acquisition buys the software layer that the shift to networked command and control demands. The genuine long-horizon risks here are cyclical and political — a defence budget that stops growing, a prolonged stop-gap funding regime, a second source qualified against the electric-propulsion position — not terminal. The valuation is a saturation print on a good business, not a fear print on a shrinking one, and it is certainly not a value trap: there is no trap because there is no discount.
