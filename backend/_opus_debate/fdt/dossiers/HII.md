# Forensic dossier — Huntington Ingalls Industries (HII)

*Prepared 2026-08-04. Price used throughout: $324.21 per share on the New York Stock Exchange (US dollars), the live quote as of today. Transcripts reviewed: the six quarterly earnings calls from 1 May 2025 through 30 July 2026. The 30 July 2026 second-quarter call was not in the supplied bundle and was pulled fresh; it is the single most important document in this file.*

---

## 1. Narrative arc

The story management has told over these six quarters is unusually consistent, and that consistency is the most valuable thing in the file.

**The starting position (May 2025).** Chief executive Chris Kastner opened 2025 with three numbered operational initiatives and then repeated the same three, in the same order, on every subsequent call: raise shipbuilding throughput (the volume of work physically completed in the yards), cut $250 million of annualised cost, and get new contracts awarded. Alongside them he set a frank expectation that the near term would be ugly for a specific, mechanical reason: HII was still building ships priced before the pandemic, at pre-pandemic assumptions about labour and materials, and would keep bleeding margin on them until they rolled off. "The next 1.5 years will be challenging as we transition out of ships contracted for pre-COVID to our new contracts" (second quarter 2025). He put a date on the crossover — 2027 — and then defended that date under direct questioning in every later call.

**What changed.** Nothing about the thesis; a great deal about the evidence. The first-quarter 2025 call was defensive: revenue fell 2.5% year over year, Newport News was "modestly behind plan," and CVN 80 Enterprise was stalled waiting on late engine-room components from suppliers. By the third quarter of 2025 revenue was a record and shipbuilding sales were growing 18%. By the second quarter of 2026 the company had posted its fourth consecutive quarter of double-digit shipbuilding growth, and management raised guidance twice in one breath — shipbuilding revenue to $10.2–10.4 billion from $9.7–9.9 billion, and the shipbuilding operating margin floor to 6.0% from 5.5%.

**The one thing that kept slipping, and then didn't.** The third initiative — contract awards — was the running sore. The Virginia-class Block VI and the next Columbia-class submarine contracts were expected "later this year" in the second quarter of 2025, "late this year" in the third quarter of 2025, "before the end of the first half" in the fourth-quarter 2025 call, and "in the second quarter" in the first quarter of 2026. Analyst Gautam Khanna put it plainly in February 2026: "this thing was expected at one point to be done north of a year ago." It finally landed on 29 July 2026 — the US Navy awarded $76.6 billion across nine Block VI Virginia-class attack submarines (with material bought for a tenth) and five Columbia-class Build II ballistic-missile submarines, of which roughly $25 billion flows to HII's Newport News yard plus about $5.5 billion on Columbia, and about $5 billion is earmarked across both builders for shipyard productivity, workforce and industrial-base investment. Work runs to July 2038.

**Growth drivers added.** Two genuinely new legs appeared and were not quietly dropped. First, unmanned systems: the REMUS undersea vehicle line (750th delivered in 2025), the Lionfish small undersea vehicle programme that "could scale to 200 vehicles," and the ROMULUS family of unmanned surface vessels built on HII's own Odyssey autonomy software. Second, new ship classes: an Ingalls frigate lead-yard-support contract awarded in April 2026, and a new battleship programme (the announced Trump-class, lead ship USS Defiant) with initial advance procurement in the fiscal-2027 budget request. Management explicitly refused to put either in guidance — "we will need additional details before we can include those in our guidance outlook" (second quarter 2026). That refusal is a credibility marker, not a weakness.

**Quietly dropped.** The Shipbuilder Accountability and Workforce Support proposal, discussed at length in May 2025 after it vanished from the final maritime executive order, never came back. Kastner reframed it sensibly at the time — the label may be dead but "the investments are required" — and the $5 billion industrial-base tranche inside the July 2026 award is that reframing proved correct. The HD Hyundai Heavy Industries partnership progressed from a memorandum of understanding (May 2025) to a memorandum of agreement (February 2026) and has produced nothing concrete since. Treat it as decoration.

**Where the story is richer than the trajectory justifies.** Returns. Return on invested capital is 5.3% and return on equity 12.9% — for a business with an absolute monopoly on US aircraft-carrier construction and half of the nuclear submarine franchise, those are poor. The reason is structural, not managerial: the customer is a single buyer that negotiates on cost, and the contracts are largely cost-plus or incentive-fee. Management's 9–10% margin ambition, if reached, would take return on invested capital to perhaps 9–10% — respectable, not exceptional. Nothing in the transcripts acknowledges this ceiling.

**A data discrepancy worth flagging.** Our own deterministic screener metrics report trailing-twelve-month free cash flow of $376 million and capital spending of $29 million. Both are wrong. Capital spending was $119 million in the second quarter of 2026 alone (3.5% of revenue, against a full-year guide of about 4%), and trailing free cash flow is close to zero or slightly negative once the first half of 2026 is included. Anyone sizing on the proxy figure is sizing on a number the company's own filings contradict.

## 3. Tone and confidence trajectory

**Rising, steadily and without a single lurch.**

- *First quarter 2025 — cautious.* "Newport News is modestly behind plan." Chief financial officer Tom Stiehle on the guidance: "we're just being conservative. We have some risk to burn down through the year."
- *Second quarter 2025 — still hedged.* "The next 1.5 years will be challenging." On the tailwinds, though, a first note of conviction: "The tailwinds are real. The demand is real."
- *Third quarter 2025 — first genuine confidence.* Record sales, shipbuilding up 18%. But Kastner deliberately damped it: "That's why I consider this a stable quarter. It's stable, but we need to continue to keep our head down and work." Stiehle added, "it's 13 weeks. Some of these contracts have 2 to 6 years to go."
- *Fourth quarter 2025 — the first offensive move.* Medium-term shipbuilding growth guidance raised from about 4% to about 6%. That is the first time in the sequence they raised anything.
- *First quarter 2026 — operationally assured.* "We are on plan through Q1." A new voice, Newport News president Kari Wilkinson, delivered specific milestone detail.
- *Second quarter 2026 — confident, and now with the receipts.* Guidance raised on both revenue and the margin floor, four straight double-digit growth quarters, and the submarine contract in hand. Stiehle: "I feel really comfortable about that... a nice incremental march that we have kind of forecasted."

There is no shift from offensive to defensive language anywhere in the sequence — if anything the opposite. What is notable is the *absence* of triumphalism at the moment it would have been easiest: on the call the morning after a $76.6 billion award, Kastner's answer to whether the terms surprised him was flatly deflationary — "No, nothing different or special about the terms... very consistent with the long term margin profile that we expect."

## 4. Guidance credibility

**High.** This is the strongest section in the file.

Test one: the 2025 throughput target. Set at 20% in the first quarter, revised down to about 15% in the third quarter when it was clear Newport News was behind, delivered at 14%. They cut the number in public, in the middle of the year, rather than quietly restating the base later.

Test two: the $250 million cost programme. Promised in the first quarter of 2025 for delivery "by year's end," confirmed delivered in the fourth-quarter call. Clean.

Test three: the pre-pandemic-to-post-pandemic contract crossover. Committed to 2027 in the third quarter of 2025. When Noah Poponak of Goldman Sachs asked in the first quarter of 2026 whether the internal schedule had drifted versus a year earlier, Stiehle answered without qualification: "They look on top of each other right now." That is a falsifiable claim on a schedule they have every incentive to fudge, and they did not fudge it.

Test four: the sandbagging pattern. Real and consistent. Stiehle told analysts in the second quarter of 2025 "we're just being conservative"; in the second quarter of 2026 he said again "there is probably a little conservatism in there." The company has beaten in six of its last seven quarters (86%), and the second quarter of 2026 earnings per share of $5.27 came in roughly 38% above the street. This is deliberate low-balling by a management team that got burned by aggressive booking rates before the pandemic. It is a *feature* for a director — but it means the current 2026 guide is a floor, not an estimate, and the reported "beat" carries less information than the raw rate suggests.

Test five: the contract-award promise. This one slipped by roughly eighteen months, over four consecutive calls, and management never gave a substantive reason beyond "it's a big complicated contract." The award eventually came in as expected. Score this as a *timing* miss with no *substance* miss — but it is the one place where the answers were genuinely thin.

**Reliability: High.**

## 5. Analyst pressure points

**The margin-versus-revenue disconnect.** Poponak pressed this hardest, in the third quarter of 2025: why does throughput and top-line growth improve faster than margin? Kastner's answer was technically correct but unsatisfying — estimate-at-completion accounting means a good quarter retires risk without immediately raising the booking rate. The unspoken implication is that margin recognition is a *management judgement* about the remaining years of a contract, and judgement can move either way. This is the most important thing in the file that analysts circled and management never fully opened up.

**The size of the submarine-contract adjustment.** Asked directly and repeatedly on the second-quarter 2026 call (Khanna: "is there any way you can give us some way to assess how big that was?"), Stiehle declined twice: "we normally do not provide that type of visibility." Investors therefore cannot separate how much of the 6.6% segment margin was operational improvement and how much was a one-time contract-adjustment credit. Management themselves flagged that "a piece of Q2 has incentives in there."

**Cash flow timing.** Analysts have not yet pressed hard on the fourth-quarter cash-flow cliff. They should. Management's own explanation — "timing of receipts and disbursements between quarters" — is the standard phrase for a working-capital slip, and the required fourth-quarter number is now extraordinary.

**Capital spending duration.** Asked in the second quarter of 2025 how long capital spending stays at 3.5–4% of sales instead of the 2.5% long-term norm, Stiehle would not commit: "we haven't provided a specific number." He did give the important offset — "we'll continue to get meaningful participation from our Navy customer as we invest." The July 2026 award's $5 billion industrial-base tranche is that participation, made concrete.

**Questions that turned into bad news later:** essentially none in this window. The CVN 80 supplier delays flagged in the first quarter of 2025 were resolved by the third quarter (modules received, 64% erected by mid-2026). The pattern here is problems disclosed early and then closed out, which is the opposite of the usual shipbuilding pattern.

## 6. Red flags and green flags

**Concerning**

- **The fourth-quarter 2026 cash-flow cliff.** First-half free cash flow of roughly −$614 million, third quarter guided to about +$100 million, full year reiterated at $500–600 million. The fourth quarter must deliver roughly $1.0–1.1 billion. Management leans on "contract advances and incentives" from the new submarine award plus a research-and-development tax credit. If the advances slip a quarter, the guide breaks in February 2027.
- **A $12 million cash balance** at 30 June 2026, against $1.7 billion of liquidity that is therefore almost entirely undrawn revolving credit. Net debt is $2.92 billion including capitalised leases ($2.69 billion excluding them), about 2.4 times trailing earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation. This is a *liability*, not a cushion — there is no net cash to add back in any valuation.
- **Estimate-at-completion volatility is the company's demonstrated killer.** The share price fell 26% in a single session on 31 October 2024 ($250.49 to $184.96) and a further 18% on 6 February 2025 ($195.58 to $159.75), reaching $160 by mid-February — a 36% decline in roughly three months, driven entirely by adverse contract re-estimates. Third-quarter 2025 still carried a negative 13% net estimate adjustment at Newport News. The mechanism that did that has not been retired; it has only been quiet for four quarters.
- **Insider behaviour.** Zero open-market purchases against four sales totalling 14,100 shares. Not damning, but no one inside is buying the recovery.
- **No buybacks in the second quarter of 2026** despite the shares trading well below their 52-week high, and only $43 million repurchased in the first quarter. Cash is going to the yards and the dividend ($1.38 per quarter, about $55 million), not to shrinking the count.
- **Return on invested capital of 5.3%** on a monopoly franchise. The barriers are absolute; the economics are not.
- **Thin analyst coverage.** Only two price targets published in the last quarter (Bernstein $337 on 17 July, Wells Fargo $325 on 8 July — both set *before* the second-quarter result), against nine over the past year averaging $369. The consensus of $360 is not a broad view.

**Positive**

- **The submarine award is signed.** The single largest overhang in the story — an eighteen-month contract slip — closed on 29 July 2026 with $76.6 billion of work running to 2038, roughly $25 billion at Newport News, plus about $5 billion of customer-funded industrial-base investment shared with General Dynamics' Electric Boat.
- **Four consecutive quarters of double-digit shipbuilding growth**, and guidance raised on both revenue and the margin floor in the same release.
- **The margin march is arithmetically visible**: shipbuilding return on sales 5.2% (2024) → 5.9% (2025) → 6.0–6.5% guided (2026), and quarterly 5.8% → 6.0% → 6.3%.
- **Backlog of $57.3 billion** at 30 June 2026 — about 4.3 times annual revenue — with second-quarter book-to-bill of 1.96 times, and the submarine modifications still to be added in the third quarter.
- **The labour problem is being fixed with named, dated actions**: a Newport News wage adjustment in mid-2025, an Ingalls collective bargaining agreement signed in March 2026, 6,600 shipbuilders hired in 2025 and over 3,500 in the first half of 2026, apprentice schools at full enrolment, and outsourcing to 23-plus distributed partners growing 30% in 2026.
- **Two funded new franchises**: an Ingalls frigate lead-yard-support contract (April 2026) and battleship advance procurement in the fiscal-2027 request — both deliberately excluded from guidance.
- **The unmanned business has crossed from pilot to production**: the next Lionfish production option secured, and ROMULUS advanced to US Navy medium unmanned surface vessel at-sea testing scheduled for September 2026.

## 7. Hidden signals

**"Distributed shipbuilding" replaced "outsourcing."** The word choice changed around the third quarter of 2025 and matters: it reframes sending work to third-party yards from a cost tactic into an industrial-capacity strategy. Scott Mikus of Melius pushed on quality in the second quarter of 2026, and the answer was refreshingly unvarnished — "it is not been perfect, and we still have some issues" — followed by a concrete proof point: the first two outsourced grand blocks were erected as expected.

**Two new operating voices appeared, and both were more specific than the chief executive.** Kari Wilkinson (Newport News) joined the first-quarter 2026 call and Brian Blanchette (Ingalls) the second-quarter 2026 call. Both delivered milestone-level detail that Kastner had previously summarised. Putting yard presidents in front of investors is what management does when the yards are performing, and the specificity is itself evidence.

**The pivot from "shipbuilder" to "defence technology company" is real but modest.** Partnerships with C3 AI (schedule optimisation), Shield AI, Thales, Babcock International and Applied Intuition all arrived within five quarters. Mission Technologies remains only about 23% of revenue, and its revenue actually *fell* 3.9% in the second quarter of 2026 (roughly flat organically, after adjusting for a $45 million non-recurring prior-year item). The autonomy story is optionality, not yet a segment.

**Management is honest about the limits of its own intellectual property.** Asked by Bank of America's Mariana Perez Mora whether HII could escape mid-single-digit cost-plus margins by owning the autonomy software, Kastner did not oversell: the Odyssey stack is written to open Navy standards, so "that is a different discussion on profitability." A promoter would have claimed a software multiple. He declined.

**A subtle sign that new contracts are better than the old ones.** Kastner in the second quarter of 2026 on the Block VI terms: "we had to incorporate lessons learned from coming through COVID and the economic environment we dealt with there. So I do obviously expect it to perform better than those contracts." That is the whole investment thesis stated in one sentence — and it is a forecast, not a fact.

## 8. Capital-allocation verdict

**Trajectory: STRENGTHENING.**

Every one of the three operational initiatives set out in May 2025 has now been delivered or is running ahead: throughput up 14% in 2025 with 15% targeted and 12% achieved through the first half of 2026; the $250 million cost programme banked; and the contract awards — the last and slowest — signed on 29 July 2026. Revenue growth has accelerated for four straight quarters, the margin trend is upward and small but genuinely visible in the segment numbers, and guidance was raised rather than defended.

**The single most important thing to weigh before sizing.** It is not the demand, which is about as certain as anything in equities — a sole-source monopoly, a 12-year contract, and bipartisan funding. It is that **reported profit here is a management estimate, not a measured result.** Percentage-of-completion accounting means margin depends on judgements about costs still four to six years away, and those judgements have twice in the last two years produced same-day share-price falls of 26% and 18%. Layer on the second issue: the 2026 free-cash-flow guide now requires roughly $1.0–1.1 billion in the fourth quarter alone after a first half that consumed roughly $614 million. Both risks land inside the next two reporting periods. Size for a business whose earnings are estimates and whose cash arrives in one lump at year-end — not for the backlog.

**Economic moat: NARROW, and stable.** The moat type is regulatory licence combined with scale-cost. HII is the only builder of nuclear-powered aircraft carriers in the United States and one of only two builders of nuclear submarines; the licensing, the security clearances, the dry docks and the 44,000-person skilled workforce cannot be replicated by anyone at any price on any relevant timescale. On barriers alone this would be a wide moat. But the returns evidence caps it: return on invested capital is 5.3% and return on equity 12.9%, both well below General Dynamics at 10.9% and 17.4%. A single-buyer customer negotiating on cost captures most of the surplus the barriers create. The trend is *stable to mildly improving* — gross margin edged from 12.38% to 12.56% over the past year on 14% revenue growth, and segment margin moved from 5.6% to 6.6%. That is not an eroding franchise; it is a structurally low-return one that is slowly repairing.

**Secular threat: manageable.** The one genuine long-horizon question is whether large crewed platforms — carriers above all — lose budget share to distributed, unmanned and missile-centric forces. The signals are already visible in the transcripts: a presidential review of carrier design, a proposed reversion of catapults, and a new battleship class appearing from nowhere in the fiscal-2027 request. But the displacement runs over decades, the current budget funds carriers 80, 81 and 82 simultaneously, and HII is itself among the largest suppliers of unmanned undersea vehicles. This is a slow structural drift to monitor, not a terminal impairment.

**Is the cheapness a fear-print or a value trap?** Neither, cleanly. At 10.2 times consensus 2028 earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation against General Dynamics at about 17 times and BWX Technologies at about 33 times, HII is priced for the low returns it actually earns, not for decline. The discount is a *quality* discount, and it is mostly deserved. It narrows only if the margin march reaches the 8–9% zone. It is not a trap, because the revenue base is contractually growing to 2038; but it is not a deep-value fear-print either, since the shares have already rallied roughly 25% off their 50-day average and 12% in a single session on the second-quarter print.
