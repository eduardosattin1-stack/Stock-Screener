# Sterling Infrastructure (STRL) — a deep dive on management and the setup

*Prepared 4 August 2026. Price used throughout: $541.45 (Nasdaq, US dollars), the live quote on
the morning of 4 August 2026, the day of the second-quarter earnings call. The stock is down
11.5% on the day from the prior close of $611.47, and down roughly 46% from its 52-week high of
$1,005.68. Transcripts reviewed: 6 May 2025, 5 August 2025, 4 November 2025, 26 February 2026,
5 May 2026. The 4 August 2026 call is not yet transcribed; the results it discusses are taken
from the company's 3 August 2026 release.*

---

## 1. Narrative arc

Sterling builds things in the dirt. It grades and prepares the ground for large facilities,
lays the underground pipe and electrical conduit, builds highways, and pours residential
concrete slabs. Across the five calls reviewed, the story went from "a diversified contractor
with a nice data-centre sideline" to "a data-centre and semiconductor site builder that happens
to own a highway business and a housing business."

The arc in four steps:

**Spring 2025 — diversification is the pitch.** Management led with a balanced portfolio: site
work growing 18%, highways growing 9%, residential concrete shrinking 14%. The chief executive
talked about backlog of $2.1 billion and a "pool of work approaching $2 billion" in the site
business, which he called "unprecedented for us."

**Summer and autumn 2025 — the acquisition changes the shape.** The company agreed to buy CEC
Facilities Group, an electrical and mechanical contractor, closing it in the third quarter for
roughly $450 million of cash. The stated logic was speed: bundling ground work with electrical
work "could take months out of the development of this project" (August 2025). Data-centre
revenue more than doubled, then grew more than 125%.

**Early 2026 — the highway and housing businesses are demoted.** By February the chief executive
was describing highways as "the Rodney Dangerfield of our business" and "a cash cow that throws
off great cash we use to grow our high-margin, high-growth E-Infrastructure product line." He
confirmed the deliberate transfer of equipment and crews out of highways and into site work:
"we're swapping $3 for $1 of earnings." Housing was written down to a hold: nothing improving
"until the second half of '26 at the earliest."

**Spring and summer 2026 — the vertical.** First-quarter revenue grew 92%, the site business
grew 174%, and the company won the first phase of a multi-phase semiconductor fabrication campus
worth over $500 million. By the second quarter the site and electrical segment was 78% of total
revenue and more than 90% of its signed backlog was described as mission-critical work — data
centres, large manufacturing and semiconductors.

**What quietly disappeared.** The 2025 pitch about the next federal highway bill — "a bigger,
more beautiful infrastructure bill than has ever been done before" (May 2025) — has faded from
the front of the story, which is honest given that highways are now 13% of revenue and shrinking
by design. The residential "green shoots" question, asked every quarter, has never been answered
with a yes.

## 2. Claims versus financials (forensic)

The headline claims hold up unusually well. This is not a promoter's tape; the numbers arrived.

| Management claim | Quarter | What the financials show | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Bottom line growth at roughly double top line growth" | Aug 2025 | 2025 full year: revenue +32%, adjusted earnings per share +53%. Second quarter 2026: revenue +90%, adjusted earnings per share +118% | Delivered, and then some |
| "Margins will continue to tick up in E-Infrastructure" | May 2026 | Company-wide gross margin rose from 23.3% to 24.8% in the second quarter of 2026 (first half: 22.8% to 24.3%). But the site-and-electrical segment's adjusted operating margin **fell** from 28.3% to 24.1%, a decline of 4.2 percentage points | Half true — see below |
| "If the right projects came and we had to grow our capacity 25% to 30% right now, we could do it" | May 2025 | Revenue has since grown 90% year on year with margins broadly intact. Capacity claim vindicated | Delivered |
| "We are in great shape from a balance sheet perspective" | every quarter | Cash of $464.5 million against total debt of $337 million (including $38 million of leases) at 30 June 2026: net cash of about $128 million, or roughly $4 a share. Return on invested capital of about 24.6% and return on equity of about 36.7% on a trailing-twelve-month basis | Verified |
| "We don't compete with price" | Aug 2025 | Gross margin expanded every year from 2021 through 2025 while revenue compounded at about 12% a year, then jumped. Consistent with real pricing power | Verified |
| Backlog visibility "approaching $6.5 billion" (first quarter 2026), then "over $7.0 billion" | May and Aug 2026 | Signed backlog $4.33 billion at 30 June 2026, up 116%; combined backlog $5.62 billion, up 150%. But $1.28 billion of the combined figure is **unsigned awards**, and $1.24 billion of that came from the two acquired businesses | Real, but the framing flatters |

**The one place the story is richer than the numbers.** Management's margin language is
company-wide when the company-wide number is flattering and segment-level when the segment number
is flattering. In the second quarter of 2026 the truthful summary is: total gross margin up
1.5 percentage points, and the crown-jewel segment's operating margin down 4.2 points because a
lower-margin acquired electrical business is now a large part of it. Both facts are true. Only
one of them has been leading the script.

**The second place.** The raised full-year outlook lifted the revenue midpoint by 8.7% but the
adjusted profit-before-depreciation midpoint by only 5.3%. That implies the incremental business
being added carries roughly a 14% margin against the 22% the company just delivered. Management
had flagged mid-teens margins for the Stone Ridge acquisition, so this is disclosed rather than
hidden — but it is dilution, and the market read it as such.

**The third place — the shape of the second half.** First-half revenue was $1.99 billion.
Full-year guidance of $4.00 billion to $4.15 billion implies second-half revenue of $2.01 billion
to $2.16 billion, or roughly $1.00 billion to $1.08 billion a quarter against the $1.17 billion
just printed. For a construction business whose summer and autumn are seasonally the strongest,
guiding to a sequential step **down** is either habitual sandbagging or a genuine cooling. The
company has beaten in all eight of the last eight quarters, which argues for sandbagging. But
you are not paid much to take that side of the bet at 27 times this year's adjusted earnings.

**Where the screener's own proxy figures are stale — say so plainly.** Our deterministic screen
carries revenue growth of +17.7% and a gross margin of 23.0%. Those are the 2025 fiscal-year
figures. The company reported +90% revenue growth and a 24.8% gross margin in the June 2026
quarter, and guides to roughly +64% revenue growth for 2026. The screen's fair-value estimates
of $346.60 and $215.30 are anchored on trailing cash flow that predates the step-change and
should not be used as levels. The screen's net-debt field (−$41 million) is also superseded: the
30 June 2026 balance sheet shows net **cash** of about $128 million.

## 3. Tone and confidence trajectory

**Rising, steeply, and that is itself the risk.**

- May 2025 — measured: "we are certainly cognizant of the high levels of uncertainty surrounding
  trade policies and the economy... we will stay focused on the things that we can control."
- August 2025 — assured: "it's really nice sitting at the margins we have, knowing they're going
  up. It's a very comfortable spot right now."
- November 2025 — expansive: "we have visibility into a pool of work in excess of $4 billion."
- February 2026 — celebratory: "the fifth consecutive year we have achieved adjusted earnings per
  share growth of over 35%."
- May 2026 — the peak: "we are perfectly positioned to continue to deliver strong earnings growth
  and returns for our shareholders for many years to come," and, on whether margins are at a peak,
  "if you consider margins going higher than they are now, then they are not at a peak because
  they will continue to go up."

The guidance has become more specific over time, not less, which is a mark of confidence rather
than evasion. The language never turned defensive — no cost-cutting vocabulary, no macro
hedging. That is a genuine positive on execution and a genuine caution on expectations: the word
"perfectly" appeared in the same quarter the stock peaked near $1,005, and the chief executive's
answer on peak margins was, one quarter later, contradicted by his own segment's reported
margin.

## 4. Guidance credibility

**High.** This is the strongest part of the file.

| Set | Guided | Delivered |
|---|---|---|
| Feb 2025 initial 2025 outlook | raised in Aug 2025 to adjusted earnings per share of $9.21–$9.47 | raised again in Nov 2025 to $10.35–$10.52 |
| Nov 2025 | $10.35–$10.52 | full-year 2025 adjusted earnings per share growth "over 53%" — delivered above the top |
| Feb 2026 initial 2026 outlook | revenue $3.05–$3.20 billion, adjusted earnings per share $13.45–$14.05 | raised in May 2026 to revenue $3.70–$3.80 billion and adjusted earnings per share $18.40–$19.05 |
| May 2026 | as above | raised again on 3 Aug 2026 to revenue $4.00–$4.15 billion, adjusted earnings per share $19.70–$20.30 |

Four consecutive upward revisions, and eight consecutive quarters of beating consensus. The
initial-2026 guide of $13.45–$14.05 will finish roughly 45% too low. That is not sandbagging at
the margin; that is a management team that sets a floor it is certain of and lets the year run.
It is a reliable pattern — and a pattern the market now fully understands, which is precisely why
a beat-and-raise no longer moves the stock up.

Reliability: **High.**

## 5. Analyst pressure points

**The recurring question is always margins, and the answer is always "up."** Adam Thalhimer
(Thompson Davis) asked in May 2025 why the full-year gross margin guide equalled the first
quarter print. Manish asked directly in May 2026 whether the segment margins were structurally
sustainable or a peak. In both cases the chief executive answered with a directional promise
("they will continue to go up") rather than a bridge. One quarter after the most emphatic
version of that answer, the site-and-electrical segment's adjusted operating margin came in 4.2
points lower than a year earlier. Management will attribute that to acquisition mix, which is
fair. But the answer given was unqualified when a qualified answer was available.

**The second pressure point is the shape of growth.** Julio Romero asked in May 2026 how to get
from 102% first-quarter growth in the legacy site business to the 60% full-year figure implied by
guidance. The reply — "I have not laid it out in that level of detail... I just do not have that
here" — is the only clear non-answer in five calls, and it was on exactly the question the market
is now asking with its wallet.

**What analysts have not pushed on, and should.** First, customer concentration. The company has
never named the share of revenue from its largest customer. Meta was named once, in passing, as a
pilot. With more than 90% of the site backlog described as mission-critical and the segment at
78% of revenue, roughly seven dollars in ten now depend on one capital-spending cycle owned by a
handful of buyers. Second, the composition of the "combined backlog" headline: the unsigned
portion is almost entirely acquired, and unsigned awards are not contracts. Third, what happens
to the $1.5 billion revolving credit facility signed in July 2026 — that is a lot of borrowing
capacity for a company with no debt problem, and nobody asked what it is for.

## 6. Red flags and green flags

**Red flags**

- **Insiders have been sellers, heavily, into the whole run.** Open-market activity over the last
  year: one purchase of 8,120 shares against 28 sales totalling 477,026 shares. The specifics are
  worse than the ratio. The chief executive sold 50,000 shares at about $453 on 25 March 2026 and
  another 50,000 at about $498 on 23 April 2026, then received a 40,000-share award on 20 May
  2026. The general counsel sold 2,500 shares at $888 on 25 June 2026, within days of the high.
  Three directors also sold. No insider has bought in the open market during the drawdown. This
  is the loudest single signal in the file and it points the wrong way.
- **The core segment's margin fell 4.2 points year on year** (adjusted operating margin 24.1%
  against 28.3%) in the June 2026 quarter, in the same quarter management had promised a rising
  trend line.
- **The raised outlook is margin-dilutive**: an 8.7% revenue lift bought with a 5.3% profit lift.
- **The balance sheet is now 40% intangible.** Goodwill and intangibles are roughly 40% of total
  assets after CEC, Drake and Stone Ridge. If the data-centre cycle turns, the write-down risk is
  real even though the cash is real.
- **Order intake is decelerating even as backlog grows.** The book-to-burn ratio — new work won
  against work completed — was 2.1 times signed and 3.5 times combined in the March 2026 quarter,
  and 1.4 times and 1.3 times in the June 2026 quarter. Still above one, which means backlog is
  still building, but the rate of build fell by more than half in a single quarter.
- **Concentration.** Roughly seven dollars in ten of revenue now ride one end market at what may
  be a capital-spending peak.

**Green flags**

- **The cash is real.** Trailing-twelve-month operating cash flow of $596 million against $481
  million of free cash flow and net income that converts at about 1.33 times — earnings are backed
  by cash, not by receivables games, even with days-sales-outstanding at a high-but-normal-for-
  construction 99 days.
- **Buybacks were executed at the right prices and stopped at the wrong ones.** The company bought
  $74 million of stock in 2025 at an average of $168.72, including $26 million at $310 in the
  fourth quarter, and $35 million in the first half of 2026 at about $305. It did **not** buy at
  the June 2026 high near $888. Roughly $362 million of authorisation remains. Buying low and
  declining to buy high is the rarest form of capital discipline in this sector.
- **Capital spending is disciplined.** Guided at $100–$110 million for 2026 on roughly $4.1
  billion of revenue — about 2.6% of sales, and a meaningful share of it growth rather than
  maintenance. Returns bear this out: return on invested capital of roughly 24.6% and return on
  equity of roughly 36.7% on the trailing twelve months, both sharply higher than the 13.2% and
  24.1% the screener carries from fiscal 2025.
- **Net cash, an undrawn and newly enlarged credit line** ($1.5 billion, extended to July 2031),
  and no dividend obligation. There is no financing wall anywhere in the next five years.
- **The highway business was genuinely fixed, not just talked about.** Adjusted operating margin
  of 19.5% in the June 2026 quarter against 14.4% a year earlier, on 20% *lower* revenue — because
  crews and equipment were deliberately moved to higher-return work and a low-bid Texas operation
  was closed. Shrinking a segment on purpose to raise its returns is the behaviour of an operator,
  not a promoter.

## 7. Hidden signals

**The vocabulary shifted from "diversified portfolio" to "the best projects."** In May 2026:
"We are not looking to win all projects. We are looking to win the best projects." That is a
selective-bidding discipline, and it is the honest source of the margin expansion. It also means
reported growth will be lumpier than demand.

**"Fill-in project" was quietly redefined.** In 2024 a fill-in job was $3–$10 million. By August
2025 the chief executive noted, with amusement, that his teams now call $40–$90 million
e-commerce distribution jobs fill-ins. This is a genuine tell about scale — and a warning that
the company's revenue is now hostage to a small number of very large starts.

**The labour constraint is the real capacity ceiling, and management says so.** "If I had 2,000
more electricians, we could put them to work in a quarter" (May 2026). Combined with the push
into factory-built modules — a new 300,000 square-foot facility — this says the binding
constraint is skilled people, not demand. That is what makes the pricing power durable while the
cycle lasts, and it is also what makes wage inflation the margin risk nobody is modelling.

**A new chief financial officer arrived in the middle of the run.** Ron Ballschmiede handed over
to Nick Grindstaff in August 2025. The disclosure quality did not deteriorate — if anything the
backlog tables became more granular, including the useful decision to report figures both with
and without the acquisition. That is a credibility-positive handover.

**Self-generated power is coming, and management has pre-sold it.** "The next generation of these
projects are going to have self-power generation on them... those projects really won't start
getting until '27 or '28." This is an honest, dated statement of a future scope expansion rather
than a promise embedded in today's numbers — and it is the strongest argument that the runway
extends past the current data-centre wave.

## 8. Capital-allocation verdict (for the director)

**Trajectory: strengthening.** Every operating claim management has made has been delivered, the
returns are rising rather than falling, the balance sheet holds net cash, and the buyback was
executed with rare discipline. The business is real and it is being run well.

**The single most important thing to weigh before sizing.** The quality of the business is not
the question; the price and the position in the cycle are. Roughly 70% of revenue now depends on
one capital-spending wave that a handful of hyperscale buyers control, the signed backlog covers
only about twelve months of revenue, order intake decelerated sharply in the June quarter, and the
people who know the most sold roughly half a million shares into the way up while buying none on
the way down. A beat-and-raise that fell 11.5% on the day tells you the market has already stopped
paying for good news.

**Economic moat: narrow, and widening on the evidence.** The moat is scale-cost plus reputation:
a fleet of heavy equipment, a scarce bench of project managers, and a record of finishing large
sites on or ahead of schedule that lets Sterling charge "a small premium" for certainty. It is
reinforced, genuinely, by the bundling of ground work with electrical work — one contractor
instead of two takes months out of a schedule, and schedule is what the customer is actually
buying. The evidence says it is widening, not eroding: return on invested capital rose to roughly
24.6% and gross margin expanded 1.5 points year on year on 90% more revenue. But it is narrow,
not wide. Construction contracts are re-competed job by job; there is no contractual lock-in, no
network, no switching cost beyond the pain of changing partners mid-campus. The recent segment
margin dip is mix from an acquisition, not price competition — but it shows how quickly the
blended margin moves when the business mix moves.

**Secular threat: manageable.** No structural force is eating this earnings base. Artificial
intelligence is the demand here, not the disruptor — nobody automates away the grading of a
1,000-acre site. The genuine risk is cyclical, not secular: a data-centre capital-spending
digestion phase would hit orders immediately and revenue in 2027–2028. That is a cycle risk to be
priced, not a melting terminal value.
