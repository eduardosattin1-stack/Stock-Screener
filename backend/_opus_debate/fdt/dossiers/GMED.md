# Globus Medical (GMED) — a deep dive on management and the setup

Forensic review of five consecutive earnings calls, from the first quarter of 2025
(reported 8 May 2025) through the first quarter of 2026 (reported 7 May 2026), tested
against the reported financials. Price used throughout: $81.06 per share on the New York
Stock Exchange, quoted 4 August 2026.

---

## 1. Narrative arc

The story told across these five calls is a single continuous arc: **"we bought a lot of
revenue, we are now digesting it, and the digestion is going better than we said it
would."** What changed is which part of that sentence management chose to lead with.

**Early 2025 — apology and repair.** The 8 May 2025 call opened with a flat quarter.
Constant-currency sales fell 0.8%; then-CEO Dan Scavilla blamed "softer enabling tech
sales against difficult comp, temporary integration related supply chain disruption and
timing of international distributor orders." The emphasis was on balance-sheet repair
rather than growth: the company had "returned to debt free status," paying off the
remainder of the roughly $900 million of debt inherited from the 2023 NuVasive merger, and
had funded the $250 million all-cash purchase of Nevro (a maker of chronic-pain
neuromodulation devices) out of operating cash.

**Mid-2025 — a leadership handover and a reset of the frame.** Keith Pfeil, chief
financial officer since 2019, was promoted to chief executive effective 18 July 2025.
His first call (8 August 2025) was noticeably different in construction: he spent several
minutes restating the company's mission and strategy before touching a single number, and
he introduced a metric the prior regime had not leaned on — consecutive weeks of United
States spine implant growth ("19 consecutive weeks"). That counter became the spine of the
narrative: 19 weeks in the second quarter of 2025, 32 weeks by the third quarter, 48 weeks
by the fourth, 58 weeks by the first quarter of 2026.

**Late 2025 into 2026 — the promise-kept quarter.** On 24 February 2026 management
explicitly cashed the cheque it had written at the time of the NuVasive merger: "we
emphasized... our focus on delivering mid- to high single-digit sales growth as well as a
mid-30s adjusted EBITDA profile by the end of the third year. Our Q4 and full year 2025
results demonstrates our performance against those objectives." Base-business adjusted
EBITDA (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization — a rough proxy for
operating cash profit) finished 2025 at 33.4% for the year and 35.7% in the fourth
quarter. By 7 May 2026 the framing had turned fully offensive: "look for market share,
taking top line growth coupled with exciting product launches while increasing gross
margins and driving meaningful earnings expansion."

**What was quietly dropped.** Two threads faded. First, the ExcelsiusFlex knee robot and
the StelKast orthopaedic implant pull-through, discussed with some enthusiasm on 8 May 2025
("we're probably going to enter the market within the second quarter"), had by 7 May 2026
been reduced to a refusal: "No comments at this point... As that becomes more, I would say,
prominent, we will update on that in the future." A year on from an imminent launch, there
is still no disclosed revenue, unit count or customer. Second, Nevro's own growth
trajectory stopped being discussed as a growth asset and became a cost-reduction project.

**The one number the screener gets wrong, and it matters.** The screener's headline
earnings per share of $3.92 for 2025 and its "+422.7% year-on-year" growth rate are
inflated by a one-time item. In the second quarter of 2025 the company reported net income
of $203 million on operating income of just $76 million, helped by roughly $112 million of
other income and a **negative** tax charge of $14.7 million — the accounting effects of the
Nevro purchase and the release of a valuation allowance on Nevro's tax losses. Strip that
out and clean 2025 reported earnings per share is nearer $3.15 than $3.92. Any valuation
anchored on the reported figure — including the screener's $188 "Buffett" fair value and
its +60% Graham margin of safety — is anchored on a number that will not repeat. The
company's own adjusted figure of $3.98 for 2025, and its guidance of $4.70 to $4.80 for
2026, are the sounder handles.

## 3. Tone and confidence trajectory

**Rising, and unusually cleanly so.**

- **8 May 2025 (defensive).** "Globus had a flat quarter." "We didn't close the deals we
  planned on in Q1 in the face of market uncertainty." Repeated hedging on quantification:
  "I'm not really feeling comfortable enough to put something out there."
- **8 August 2025 (transitional).** A new chief executive spends his opening minutes on
  identity rather than results — "I'd like to take a few minutes to step back and talk
  about who Globus is" — which is what one does when the numbers are not yet the argument.
  Free cash flow that quarter was only $26 million.
- **6 November 2025 (turning).** "We are extremely pleased with our overall Q3
  performance." Record third-quarter free cash flow of $214 million. Guidance revised
  upward.
- **24 February 2026 (confident).** "Momentum seen coming out of our second quarter
  continued and accelerated." A record fourth quarter, and the explicit claim of having met
  the merger's three-year targets.
- **7 May 2026 (assertive).** "Revenue has more than tripled. We've generated 6x the
  amount of free cash flow." The tone is now promotional in places, which is where a
  reader should slow down.

The shift is from efficiency and cost language toward growth and investment language — the
opposite of the usual deterioration pattern, and it is corroborated by the margin and cash
figures rather than contradicted by them.

## 4. Guidance credibility

**High.** This is the strongest part of the file.

Management set a public, dated, multi-year target at the time of the NuVasive merger in
2023 — mid-to-high single-digit sales growth and a mid-30s adjusted operating cash margin
by the end of year three — and hit it in the fourth quarter of 2025, on time. Along the
way it did the harder thing of *not* revising the target when the first quarter of 2025 was
flat.

On the 2025 revenue guide, management held the $2.8 to $2.9 billion range through the
mid-year Nevro noise rather than chasing it up or down ("we feel comfortable about our
overall range"), and delivered $2.939 billion. For 2026 it reaffirmed revenue of $3.18 to
$3.22 billion on 7 May 2026 while **raising** adjusted earnings per share guidance to
$4.70–$4.80 from $4.40–$4.50 — a rise driven by margin, not by a revenue promise. That is
the conservative direction to raise in. The screener's record of six beats in seven
quarters is consistent with a house that guides slightly low; management itself calls this
"Globus conservatism."

The one blemish is the pattern of declining to quantify anything awkward. Asked in May 2025
to size the enabling-technology deals that slipped, Scavilla answered: "why don't we talk
that in August when we get into Q2." Asked repeatedly to frame Nevro's forward revenue,
Pfeil declined four calls running. Neither is dishonest; both mean an investor is carrying
more unquantified risk in those two lines than the disclosure suggests.

## 5. Analyst pressure points

Three questions recur, and management answers none of them with a number.

1. **What is the capital order book actually worth?** Analysts pushed on this in every
   single call — Bijou (May 2025), Cronin (August 2025), Roberts (November 2025), Hinton
   (February 2026). Management's answers are consistently qualitative: "the deal pipeline
   is robust," "we did not lose any pipeline deals to competition," "there's no shortage of
   offerings out there." **There is no disclosed backlog figure, no book-to-bill ratio, and
   no installed-unit count.** The only hard number offered is a cumulative procedure
   counter — over 100,000 (Q1 2025), 115,000 (Q3 2025), 120,000 (Q4 2025), "almost 130,000"
   (Q1 2026) — which is a metric that can only ever go up and says nothing about utilisation
   per system or the current order rate.

2. **Why is enabling-technology revenue falling, and is the lease pivot a strategy or an
   excuse?** Revenue in that line was $22 million in the first quarter of 2025 (down 31%),
   $28 million in the third quarter (down 27%), then $55.6 million in the fourth quarter
   (up 18.5%) and $26.9 million in the first quarter of 2026 (up 21%). Management's
   explanation evolved from "market uncertainty" and "red lines back and forth" (May 2025)
   to a deliberate shift toward operating leases and rentals that suppresses upfront revenue
   recognition (November 2025 onward). Both can be true. But the November 2025 answer
   contained the most important admission on any of these calls: "hospitals are requiring
   surgeons to go through the review of all the offerings that are out there... that has
   kind of elongated some of the deals." That is a description of a capital sale becoming a
   commodity bake-off.

3. **How much more cost can come out of Nevro before the top line breaks?** Asked directly
   by O'Brien in August 2025 — "how much more room do you have to go there before you're
   kind of cutting some of the muscle on the top line?" — and answered with a restatement
   of the problem rather than an answer. Nevro's revenue contribution has since fallen from
   roughly $99 million a quarter to roughly $83 million.

## 6. Red flags and green flags

**Red flags**

- **The buyback was executed at almost exactly the wrong price.** Management confirmed on
  7 May 2026 that it repurchased over 10 million shares for more than $600 million at an
  average price of **$160 per share**. The stock is $81.06 today. That is roughly $300
  million of shareholder capital — over $2 per share — destroyed on timing alone. Worse for
  the read on management's own price judgement: the company spent $190 million on buybacks
  in the first quarter of 2025 and $300 million across 2025 as a whole, then bought back
  **nothing at all** in the first quarter of 2026, while simultaneously issuing $26 million
  of stock through option exercises. They bought heavily near the top and stopped near the
  bottom. Management has said it will "look at share repurchases when the time is right"
  (February 2026); on the evidence, its sense of when the time is right is inverted.
- **Insider selling is one-directional.** Zero open-market purchases against 23 sales
  totalling 239,178 shares.
- **Research intensity fell in the quarter after management promised to raise it.** 4.8% of
  revenue in the first quarter of 2026 against 5.5% a year earlier, versus a stated
  ambition of 6%. Some of the margin expansion is a deferral, not a structural gain.
- **Two shareholder law firms opened investigations in mid-2026.** Purcell & Lefkowitz
  announced an investigation on 1 June 2026 into whether directors breached their duties
  "in connection with recent corporate actions"; Kuehn Law announced investigations on
  16 and 28 July 2026 concerning "potential self-dealing." Neither release names a
  transaction, a date, or a party. These solicitations are common, frequently amount to
  nothing, and should not be treated as evidence of wrongdoing — but the company's proxy
  statement (filed 24 April 2026) could not be retrieved for this review, so the underlying
  facts are unverified either way. Treat as an open item, not a finding.
- **The working-capital drag is severe.** 283 days of inventory and a 331-day cash
  conversion cycle. Growth in this business consumes cash in field instrument sets and
  implant trays before it produces any; free cash flow will always lag reported profit.
- **A direct, dated competitive hit.** Medtronic announced United States regulatory
  clearance of its Stealth AXiS robotic spine platform on 13 February 2026 — combining
  planning software, navigation and robotic assistance in a modular design built for both
  hospitals and ambulatory surgery centres — and claims a spine-technology installed base
  ten times the nearest competitor's. Three months later, on 7 May 2026, Globus told
  investors that "no other competitive systems, including recently introduced products,
  have been able to replicate the reliability, ease or accurate workflow attributes of our
  robot." That is promoter language, asserted without evidence, about a competitor that had
  just cleared a next-generation system. It is the single least credible sentence in the
  five calls.

**Green flags**

- **A public three-year target, met on schedule** (fourth quarter 2025), having survived a
  weak first quarter without being revised.
- **Debt extinguished ahead of plan.** Roughly $900 million of merger debt gone, the final
  $450 million repaid in the first quarter of 2025, leaving about $515 million of net cash.
- **Gross margin genuinely expanding on genuinely growing revenue** — the single most
  informative combination in a hardware business, and the opposite of buying volume with
  price.
- **The vertical-integration answer on tariffs was specific and checkable**: "95% of our
  production is U.S. based and our sourcing of materials to produce is actually U.S. based"
  (8 May 2025). Nine months of subsequent margin data have not contradicted it.
- **Nevro's cash burn was reversed within two quarters** of ownership, from minus $29
  million to plus $8.5 million.
- **The consecutive-weeks-of-growth disclosure is unusual and falsifiable.** A company
  fabricating momentum does not volunteer a counter that breaks publicly the first week it
  stops being true.

## 7. Hidden signals

- **The vocabulary around the robot changed from product to channel.** In 2025 the
  Excelsius platform was described as a revenue line. By November 2025 and after, it is
  described as "the execution layer," and the goal is stated as "driving capital placement
  and launching successful durable capital programs that enable implant sales growth." In
  February 2026: "over time, the mix of revenue may change." Read plainly, management has
  concluded the robot is no longer something you sell at a premium — it is something you
  place in order to sell implants. That is a rational response to competition and it
  protects the profit pool, but it also removes the basis for a robotics-platform valuation
  premium.
- **A finance chief became chief executive, and the metrics changed accordingly.** Pfeil's
  most revealing line came in November 2025, correcting an analyst who praised Nevro's
  margin: "the EBITDA margin is impressive, but... the thing that I focus on... is the free
  cash... because at the end of the day, you take dollars to the bank, not rate." An
  operator who prefers cash to margin rate is the right person to run a business whose
  balance sheet is full of instrument sets.
- **International ambition was trimmed in public.** The long-run international growth frame
  moved from "10% to 15%" (November 2025) to "12% to 15%" but with an explicit "we're going
  to grow into this" and a narrowing of scope — "we're not looking to suddenly add more
  countries."
- **The founder stepped back but did not step away.** David Paul remains executive
  chairman and introduced the new chief executive himself. Founder-chairman structures are
  where related-party questions usually originate, which is the context in which the
  unspecified self-dealing investigations should be held — as a question to resolve, not an
  allegation to accept.

## 8. Capital-allocation verdict (for the director)

**Trajectory: strengthening.**

The operating business is doing the difficult thing correctly. It is taking share in a
market growing about 3% a year, expanding gross margin while it does so, converting that
into cash, and it carries no net debt. Management set a hard three-year target in public and
hit it. On the operating line, the numbers corroborate the words.

**The single most important thing to weigh before sizing this position is that management's
judgement of its own share price has been demonstrably poor, and the market has already
noticed the operating turn.** The buyback at an average of $160 against $81 today is not a
rounding error; it is a $300 million verdict on the board's price sense, delivered by the
board itself. And with the shares up roughly 44% over the past year and sitting near the
middle of their 52-week range, the "quietly compounding, ignored by the market" phase is
over. What remains is a good business at a fair-to-slightly-cheap price, not a good business
at a cheap price. Size for that.

**Economic moat: narrow, and widening.** The moat is switching costs reinforced by scale
economics. Switching costs come from the instrument sets and implant trays physically
resident in a hospital, from surgeon familiarity with a specific screw and retractor system,
and from the Excelsius ecosystem tying planning software, navigation, imaging and implants
to one vendor. Scale economics come from 95% United States vertical integration, which is
what produces a 69% gross margin. The evidence says this moat is widening rather than
eroding: return on invested capital has risen to 9.7% from the post-merger trough (return on
tangible assets is 17.9%), and gross margin is up 850 basis points year on year. That is the
textbook signature of a widening moat, not a shrinking one. It is *narrow* rather than wide
because spine is a share-shift market with five well-capitalised competitors — Medtronic,
Stryker, Zimmer Biomet, Johnson & Johnson and Smith+Nephew — and because the robot itself,
the most defensible-sounding asset, is being repositioned by management's own words from a
premium product into a channel-access cost.

**Secular threat: material, not terminal.** The named force is reimbursement compression —
what payers will pay per procedure. It has already bitten once: the planned reduction in
third-party biologics revenue in the first quarter of 2025 came from "expected changes in
the reimbursement landscape for wound care products." Management flagged federal
Medicare and Medicaid funding uncertainty as a driver of slower hospital capital decisions in
November 2025, and the migration of spine surgery out of hospitals and into ambulatory
surgery centres compresses the price of each procedure. But this is margin pressure on a
demographically expanding base of degenerative spine disease, not the disappearance of the
end market, and the company has an ambulatory-ready portfolio. The cheapness here is a
fear-print about a lumpy capital-equipment line and a poorly-timed buyback, not a
structurally shrinking base.
