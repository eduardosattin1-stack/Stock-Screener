# Forensic Dossier — Fortuna Mining Corp. (FSM)

Prepared 2026-08-11. Sources: five consecutive quarterly earnings calls (Q1 2025 through Q1 2026),
company news releases dated through 2026-08-05, and the screener's financial extract.
Share price used throughout: $10.755 on the New York Stock Exchange, in United States dollars.

---

## 1. NARRATIVE ARC

The story told on these calls changed shape twice in fifteen months, and both changes were real
rather than cosmetic.

**Phase one — shrinking on purpose (Q1 and Q2 2025).** The opening theme was subtraction. In the
first quarter of 2025 management announced the sale of the San Jose mine in Mexico and, days after
the quarter closed, the sale of Yaramoko in Burkina Faso. Both were described in the same terms:
short remaining reserve life, high cost, and a capital burden. "San Jose has become our highest
cost mine and was nearing the end of its mineral reserves," the chief executive said in the first
quarter of 2025. The pitch was that selling the two mines freed roughly $50 million of mine-closure
spending plus management attention. The honest cost of that decision was stated plainly in the
second quarter of 2025: annual output fell from a record 460,000 gold-equivalent ounces in 2024 to
"roughly 330,000 ounces."

**Phase two — rebuilding, on assets already owned (Q2 2025 onward).** From the second quarter of
2025 the entire narrative reorganised around a single number: half a million ounces of gold a year.
The delivery vehicles never changed — expand the Séguéla mine in Côte d'Ivoire, and build the
Diamba Sud project in Senegal. The repeated framing is that this growth "is fully under our
control," meaning it needs no acquisition, no equity issuance, and no exploration luck. That claim
has held up. By the first quarter of 2026 the same two projects were still the whole story, and by
mid-2026 both had cleared their decision gates.

**What was quietly dropped.** Silver. The company renamed itself from Fortuna Silver Mines to
Fortuna Mining, and by the 2025 fiscal year the segment disclosure shows gold doré at $820 million,
or effectively all of revenue. Caylloma in Peru, the original silver mine, now gets roughly ninety
seconds per call. Mexico was also let go: in the first quarter of 2026 the chief executive said
"we will likely be reducing dramatically our presence in Mexico," redirecting that spending to
Guyana and Suriname. Neither retreat was hidden, but neither was announced as a strategy change
either — they arrived as asides inside longer answers.

**What was added.** A widening exploration footprint — Guinea, Guyana, Suriname, Argentina's Salta
province, plus a 15% equity stake in Awalé Resources in Côte d'Ivoire. This is the one place where
the story has expanded faster than the capital behind it, and it deserves watching.

**The cost divergence is the most important line in this table.** The screener's deterministic
proxy reads an earnings-before-interest-taxes-depreciation-and-amortisation margin (EBITDA — a
rough measure of operating cash profitability) of 67.8% over the trailing twelve months, which
places Fortuna in the top cost band. That reading is a gold-price artifact, not evidence of cost
leadership. With gold at $4,443 per ounce today, a company with an all-in sustaining cost of
$2,157 still prints a 68% margin. Measured against the industry rather than against the metal,
$2,157 per ounce is a high-cost consolidated position. The company's own guidance for 2026 was
$1,830–$1,975, and both reported quarters have come in above it.

Management's explanation is granular and partly persuasive: of the second quarter's $2,157, roughly
$49 per ounce is attributed to outside forces (price-linked royalties, Argentine inflation, diesel)
and $115 per ounce to one-time items (a crusher refurbishment at Lindero, contractor mobilisation
at Séguéla). Strip both and you get about $1,993 — still above the top of the annual range. The
cost guide for 2026 is, on the evidence of two quarters, more likely to be missed than met.

**The buyback asterisk.** In June 2024 the company sold $172.5 million of 3.75% convertible senior
notes due 2029 with a conversion price of about $6.59 per share. Those notes are now well in the
money and represent roughly 26 million shares — the gap between the 301.0 million basic and 328.6
million diluted share counts in the second quarter of 2026. So the company sold a claim on its own
equity at $6.59 and has spent the first half of 2026 buying shares back at an average of about
$9.87. That is not a scandal; the notes funded the balance sheet through a weak period. But the
net reduction in the ownership base is materially smaller than the $106.6 million headline
suggests, and a reader comparing per-share figures should use the diluted count.

## 3. TONE & CONFIDENCE TRAJECTORY

**Rising, and specific rather than atmospheric.**

- *Q1 2025:* Defensive-constructive. The emphasis is on what is being removed — closures, high-cost
  ounces, Burkina Faso security. "A prudent exit from a country where we are no longer investing."
- *Q2 2025:* The inflection. Production has just fallen by a quarter and the chief executive
  confronts it head-on: "Naturally, this reduces our near-term production… But the key message
  today is this, rebuilding production to 0.5 million ounces per year… is fully under our control."
  He closes with "In 20 years, I have never seen Fortuna as strong as it is today."
- *Q3 2025:* Confident and increasingly numerical — a preliminary economic assessment for Diamba
  Sud with a stated internal rate of return of 72% at $2,750 gold, and a net cash position of $266
  million.
- *Q4 2025 (Feb 2026):* The most expansive call in the set. Reserves up 50% after depletion,
  Diamba's indicated resource up 73%, a $100 million Diamba budget approved. This is also where the
  promotional language peaks — the aggregated seven-million-ounce figure and an unprompted
  observation that "everybody is happy right now about $4,000, $5,000 gold."
- *Q1 2026 (May 2026):* Records across the board, but the tone tightens noticeably around costs.
  The chief executive volunteers the all-in sustaining cost number before anyone asks and
  immediately partitions it into controllable and external components. That is what a management
  team does when it knows a number is going to be challenged.

The shift from offence to defence is visible only in the cost paragraphs. Everywhere else the
language became more concrete over time, which is the healthier direction.

## 4. GUIDANCE CREDIBILITY

**Score: Medium-High.**

Where they promised and delivered:

- **Séguéla production.** Guided to 140,000 ounces for 2025; delivered 152,420 — above the top of
  the range for a second consecutive year. In the first quarter of 2026 it produced 42,016 ounces,
  14% above the prior quarter and again ahead of the mine plan. This is the single most reliable
  forecast in the company.
- **Diamba Sud permitting and study timetable.** In the first quarter of 2025 the chief executive
  said the exploitation concession would be pursued "by midyear 2026" with a construction decision
  "second half of 2026." The environmental decree arrived 2026-06-15, the feasibility study
  2026-06-29, and the supporting technical report was filed 2026-07-13. Delivered on schedule.
- **Séguéla expansion study.** Promised "early this year" in February 2026; the board approved a
  30% capacity expansion on 2026-07-29 — a modest slip of a few months, then delivered.
- **Capital projects.** The tailings lift, the rock breaker, the transmission-tower replacement and
  the six-megawatt solar plant were each flagged in advance and each reported complete or
  commissioning on the stated schedule.

Where they missed:

- **Lindero 2025 production.** Full-year output of 87,489 ounces came in about 6% below the bottom
  of guidance, entirely because of two mechanical failures in the fourth quarter. The engineering
  review then found "structural fatigue risk in the primary crusher foundations" — a 35-day
  shutdown and $2.2 million repair booked for March 2026. This is the asset that keeps breaking.
- **2026 consolidated cost guidance.** Covered above; running above the range for two quarters.
- **The 500,000-ounce date.** Stated as "the next 24 months" in February 2026 and again in May
  2026, now described as mid-2028. The target has not been abandoned, but the clock has been reset
  at least once without being called out.

There is no evidence of sandbagging. If anything the pattern is the reverse: Séguéla is
consistently under-promised while consolidated costs are consistently under-forecast.

## 5. ANALYST PRESSURE POINTS

Coverage is thin — a single price target printed in the last quarter, and the question queues on
several calls emptied early, with private investors and a newsletter writer filling the gap. That
matters: it means the checks below have not been applied by many professional eyes.

- **Cost trajectory** is the one topic analysts push hard on. In the second quarter of 2025 an
  analyst pressed twice on the shape of second-half capital spending and the fourth-quarter cost
  level, and had to ask a clarifying follow-up because the first answer conflated two different
  years. Costs remain the least crisply answered subject on every call.
- **Capital returns.** Asked directly in the third quarter of 2025 how buybacks rank against
  growth, the answer put growth first, buybacks second, and disclosed that the repurchase programme
  had been paused for two quarters. Fortuna pays no dividend. Investors seeking income from a
  cash-rich gold producer are not being served, and management has not pretended otherwise.
- **The Lindero crusher** was raised and given a reassuring answer ("we don't expect our cost to be
  significantly impacted in Q4"). The fourth quarter then missed guidance on exactly that issue.
  That is one documented instance where a management reassurance did not survive the next quarter.
- **Not being pressed at all:** the concentration of value in Séguéla, the jurisdiction mix
  (Côte d'Ivoire, Senegal, Argentina, Peru, and now Guyana), and the widening exploration budget.
  Nobody on these calls asked what the company would look like if gold returned to $3,000.

## 6. RED FLAGS / GREEN FLAGS

🔴 **Consolidated costs are running above the company's own 2026 guidance** — $2,107 and $2,157 per
ounce against a $1,830–$1,975 range, for two quarters, with the excess explained by a mix of
external and one-time items.

🔴 **Two contractor fatalities in eighteen months at the flagship mine** — one in February 2025 and
another reported with the second quarter of 2026 results — sit uncomfortably beside repeated
records for lost-time-injury-free hours. A company can set a lost-time record and still be killing
contractors; that combination usually means the headline metric is measuring the wrong population.

🔴 **The aggregated seven-million-ounce resource figure** (Q4 2025 call) bundles inferred resources
with proven reserves. To his credit the chief executive flagged that regulators dislike the
practice as he did it, but he did it anyway, on the record.

🔴 **Lindero is a recurring mechanical liability** — two failures in one quarter, then a structural
fatigue finding in the crusher foundations, then a 35-day planned outage. It is also the
highest-cost mine at $2,265 per ounce and sits in Argentina.

🟡 **A rolling growth horizon.** "The next 24 months" was used in February 2026 and again in May
2026 for the same 500,000-ounce goal.

🟢 **Portfolio pruning that actually happened, at a price.** San Jose and Yaramoko were sold for
$84 million of gross proceeds plus a $57.5 million pre-closing dividend, removing roughly $50
million of future closure spending. Both were near reserve exhaustion. This is the hardest thing
for a mining management team to do and they did it early.

🟢 **The share count is shrinking, not growing.** For a sector whose defining sin is serial
dilution, the weighted-average count fell from 306.6 million to 301.0 million between the first
quarter of 2025 and the second quarter of 2026, with $106.6 million repurchased in the first half
of 2026 at an average near $9.87 — bought during a drawdown, not at the top. The last equity-linked
issuance was the June 2024 convertible.

🟢 **Growth funded entirely from internal cash flow.** Approximately $330 million of 2026 capital
spending, 56% of it growth and exploration, against $756.7 million of liquidity and roughly $435
million of net cash as the company counts it. No project financing, no streaming deal, no equity.

🟢 **Reserves grew 50% year over year after depletion** to 3.0 million gold ounces (April 2026),
with indicated resources up 56% to 2.1 million ounces. Sunbird reserves rose 34% and its resources
55%. Reserve replacement is the scarcest commodity in this industry and Fortuna is currently
producing it.

🟢 **Diamba Sud's feasibility study is genuinely strong** — a 60% after-tax internal rate of return
and a $1.0 billion net present value at a 5% discount rate using $3,500 gold, on $398 million of
initial capital, with an all-in sustaining cost of $1,056 per ounce over the first four years. If
built as designed it would be the company's lowest-cost mine and would pull the consolidated cost
position down materially.

## 7. HIDDEN SIGNALS

- **The word "frontier" is now used as a positive.** In the first quarter of 2026 the chief
  executive said "mining has always been a frontier business, and we are happy to play in the
  frontier," then explained the trade explicitly: Fortuna accepts higher perceived political risk
  in exchange for speed to cash flow — seven to eight months from environmental submission to
  approval in Senegal. That is an unusually honest articulation of a business model, and it is also
  a warning label. The company is structurally over-weighted to jurisdictions the market discounts.
- **A vocabulary shift from ounces to value.** "We have no need to chase ounces. We are chasing
  value" (Q2 2025) and, on Diamba, "the most accretive growth we can provide our shareholders is
  organic growth." Alongside that, the recurring "free cash flow per share" and "per share value
  creation" phrasing from the fourth quarter of 2025 onward. Managements that start talking in
  per-share terms usually mean it or are about to be held to it.
- **Equity stakes are becoming a habit without a budget.** Awalé Resources (15% owned, topped up in
  July 2026), a joint venture with DeSoto in Guinea, an option in Salta, and now Quartz Stone in
  Guyana. Asked directly, the chief executive said these are made "by appointment rather than as a
  specific strategy and budget." That is candour, but an unbudgeted line item that keeps appearing
  is how exploration spending drifts.
- **The shareholder base is audibly retail.** The Q1 2025 call featured a private investor saying
  "I've been buying all the way down… this is a fantastic deal" and a newsletter writer arranging a
  podcast appearance on air. Combined with a legacy name that said "Silver" on a business that is
  now essentially all gold, this is a holder base assembled for reasons that no longer match the
  asset.

## 8. CAPITAL-ALLOCATION VERDICT (for the director)

**Trajectory: STRENGTHENING.** Over fifteen months this management team sold its two worst mines
near the end of their lives, used the proceeds and a rising gold price to build roughly $435
million of net cash, bought back stock during a drawdown rather than at the peak, grew reserves 50%
after depletion, and delivered a feasibility study and a mine expansion on the timetable it
published a year earlier. Every one of those is a checkable fact, and each one checks out. This is
not a promoter's balance sheet.

**The single thing to weigh before sizing.** Fortuna is not a low-cost producer; it is a
high-margin producer *because gold is $4,443*. Consolidated all-in sustaining cost is $2,157 per
ounce and running above the company's own annual guidance. One mine — Séguéla, at $1,765 — is
genuinely excellent. One mine — Lindero, at $2,265 — is marginal, mechanically unreliable, and in
Argentina. The whole investment case rests on Séguéla expanding and Diamba Sud being built on
budget, because those two assets are what drag the consolidated cost position down toward the
industry middle. Until Diamba pours gold, roughly two years away, the company's earnings are a
leveraged bet on the metal, and that leverage cuts both ways: a 10% move in gold swings operating
cash profitability by about 14%.

**Moat.** In a business that sells an undifferentiated commodity at a price it does not set, the
only durable advantage is position on the cost curve plus the reserve life to keep occupying it.
Fortuna's is **narrow but real and currently widening**: Séguéla sits in the industry's cheapest
quartile with a decade of reserves, Diamba Sud is designed to be cheaper still at $1,056 per ounce
in its first four years, and reserve replacement has been running well ahead of depletion. Against
that, the consolidated position today is high-cost, and the moat is geographic as much as
geological — it depends on Côte d'Ivoire and Senegal remaining hospitable. Returns support the
widening read rather than contradicting it: return on invested capital of about 18.8% and return on
equity of about 22.2% over the trailing twelve months, with margins expanding year over year.

**Secular threat: manageable.** Gold mining faces no structural displacement of the kind that
threatens terminal value in media, retail or payments. The genuine long-run risks here are
political — resource nationalism in West Africa and capital controls in Argentina — and depletion,
which is a permanent condition of the industry rather than a new force. The cheapness on display is
a fear-print on jurisdiction and cost, not a melting earnings base.
