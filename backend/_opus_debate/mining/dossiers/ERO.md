# Ero Copper Corp. (ERO) — Forensic Dossier

*Transcripts reviewed: Q4 2024 (7 Mar 2025), Q1 2025 (6 May 2025), Q2 2025 (1 Aug 2025), Q3 2025 (5 Nov 2025), Q4 2025 (6 Mar 2026). Financials cross-checked against filed quarterly statements through the quarter ended 30 June 2026 (filed 5 Aug 2026) and the company's second-quarter 2026 results release of 5 Aug 2026. Price and commodity marks as of 11 Aug 2026.*

Ero Copper is a mid-sized Brazilian copper miner. It runs three operations — Caraíba (an underground copper complex), Tucumã (a newer open-pit copper mine), and Xavantina (a small underground gold mine) — and is earning into a 60% interest in Furnas, a large undeveloped copper-gold deposit held with Vale Base Metals. It sells copper concentrate, so its revenue is a direct function of the copper price, with gold as a meaningful side-earner.

---

## 1. Narrative Arc

The story across these five calls is unusually linear, and management deserves credit for not moving the goalposts. In March 2025 the new chief executive, Makko DeFilippo, laid out a four-step plan on his first call: get Tucumã to commercial production, pay down debt, advance the Furnas growth project, and then start returning cash to shareholders. Every subsequent call has been scored against that same list. There is no thesis drift here, no quietly abandoned growth driver, and no new narrative bolted on when the old one got hard — which is itself notable in a sector where story-swapping is the norm.

What did change is the *tone of difficulty*. The first two calls of 2025 were spent explaining problems: a multi-week power outage at Tucumã, a damaged tailings filter, "teething pains" in the plant, low mined grades at Caraíba, and ground-support delays at Xavantina. By the August 2025 call the framing had shifted to a deliberate reset — management described 2025 as "a year of 2 halves" and a "back-to-basics" transformation, and warned plainly that the first half had been rebuilt rather than harvested. By November 2025 the language turned to records, and by March 2026 to positioning: "Ero is exiting a major investment cycle with an exceptional long-term growth asset, increasing cash generation capacity, declining consolidated capital requirements and three operating mines with the right mix of metals at exactly the right time in the commodity price cycle."

Two genuinely new value drivers appeared along the way, and both are real rather than rhetorical. The first is the Xavantina gold concentrate stockpile — a decade of small, high-grade concentrate piles that nobody had bothered to characterise. Management sampled about 20% of the volume, declared a maiden inferred resource of roughly 29,000 ounces of gold in November 2025, and began shipping. This has since delivered about 15,000 ounces in the fourth quarter of 2025 and another 11,860 ounces in the second quarter of 2026, at a cash cost of $633 to $715 per ounce against a gold price above $4,000. That is close to free money found inside the existing fence line. The second is the Furnas preliminary economic assessment, published in late February 2026: an initial 24-year mine life, more than 1.2 million tonnes of copper plus 2 million ounces of gold, roughly $2 billion of after-tax net present value and a 27% internal rate of return on $1.3 billion of initial capital.

The one item that has *not* moved is step four. Shareholder returns were promised in March 2025 "once we make meaningful progress on deleveraging." Seventeen months later, with leverage down to 0.8 times, the second-quarter 2026 release still carries no dividend and no buyback.

Where the story is **richer than the numbers**: cash generation. Adjusted earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation (a rough proxy for operating cash profit) reached $533 million on a trailing twelve-month basis, and management talks freely about "increasing cash generation capacity." But free cash flow — what is left after the mines are paid for — was only about $154 million over the same twelve months. Capital spending consumed roughly 65% of operating cash flow. That is a 4.1% free cash yield on a $3.7 billion market value, earned at an all-time-high copper price. The cash-generation story is a 2027 story, not a 2026 one.

Where the story is **poorer than the numbers**: the balance sheet and the share count. Management is almost apologetic about the pace of debt reduction, yet leverage has fallen by 1.8 turns in eighteen months, and — critically for this sector — it was done without printing shares. Weighted average diluted shares went from 103.5 million at the end of 2024 to 105.9 million at 30 June 2026, an increase of about 2%, essentially all of it employee incentive plans. The company funded a mine build, a shaft, a mechanisation programme and a drill campaign using a $200 million revolving credit facility, a copper prepayment facility, and a $50 million extension of an existing gold stream with Royal Gold. That is the single most important fact in this dossier, and management undersells it.

## 3. Tone and Confidence Trajectory

**Rising, and earned.** The arc is easy to trace in the language.

- *March 2025 — defensive and explanatory.* "I acknowledge we've had several challenges that impacted production, both outside and inside our mine gate." The call is a list of fixes with dollar amounts attached ("on the order of $2 million") and a stated production cadence.
- *May 2025 — cautious.* "It is still early in May and we are taking a measured approach here." Management refused to declare commercial production until it was sure the throughput would hold. That restraint is a credibility marker, not a weakness.
- *August 2025 — candid.* This is the most honest call of the five. Management cut guidance, said the first half was "not without its challenges," and used a memorable, slightly self-deprecating line: "we arrived a bit late to the station, but... late to the station, but full steam ahead."
- *November 2025 — confident and specific.* "Every single operation in our portfolio achieved not just 2025 calendar year monthly records for productivity and production, but they achieved all-time historic monthly records in October." Note the specificity: 3,500 tonnes of copper at Caraíba in October, 3,300 at Tucumã, just under 7,000 ounces of gold at Xavantina. Specific claims are falsifiable claims.
- *March 2026 — strategic.* The frame widens from operations to cycle positioning: "many companies, including most of our peers, are jumping into major project builds within the next year. We like this dynamic."

Guidance got *more* specific over time, not less, which is the opposite of the usual pattern when a story is deteriorating. Management now volunteers per-mine cash costs, monthly production figures, and explicit statements about what is *not* in guidance.

The one thing that grates is superlative density. "Ero is an incredible business." "An incredible development asset." "An incredible partnership." "An incredible year of transformation." "Firing on all cylinders." "Energizing." In isolation this is exactly the promoter vocabulary that should raise an eyebrow in a mining company. Here it is offset by the fact that the numbers arrived behind the adjectives. The tell to watch is if the adjectives ever start arriving *without* the numbers.

## 4. Guidance Credibility

**Medium-to-high, trending up. Score: Medium-High.**

The record splits cleanly into two years.

**2025 was a miss.** Guidance was reaffirmed twice — in March and again in May — before being cut materially in August. Management's own framing of the cut was unusually blunt: the new *high* end sat at the old *low* end. The company then landed at the low end of the reduced range across all three operations. An investor who sized a position on the March 2025 guidance was wrong by a wide margin.

**2026 has held so far.** Consolidated copper production guidance of 67,500 to 77,500 tonnes and cash cost guidance of $2.15 to $2.35 per pound were both maintained at the half-year mark, with second-quarter production of 17,315 tonnes at $2.42 per pound and management explicitly weighting the year to the second half. Two revisions went the wrong way and both are small: Xavantina's mined-gold cash cost guidance rose to $1,100–$1,350 per ounce from $1,000–$1,250, its all-in sustaining cost guidance to $2,200–$2,700 from $2,000–$2,500, and capital guidance rose $10 million for a new power line.

What lifts the score above "medium" is a pattern of *deliberate conservatism* introduced in 2026. Management explicitly excluded from guidance the additional tailings filtration equipment being installed at Tucumã this year — equipment they expect to pay back in one to two quarters — with the stated reason that "guiding to steady state was important for us this year." Excluding a probable positive from guidance is the structural opposite of sandbagging a negative. It is early to call this a habit, but it is the right habit.

There is no earnings-beat history in the supplied financial block to reconcile against, so this score rests on operational guidance versus operational delivery.

## 5. Analyst Pressure Points

**Capital returns.** Dalton Baretto of Canaccord asked directly in March 2025 whether returns would come as buybacks or a dividend. The answer was a deflection: "it's a bit too early to talk about the playoffs." Guilherme at Goldman Sachs pushed again in August 2025 and got the chief financial officer's version: "the first focus is obviously to get the balance sheet back to where we want it to be." Leverage is now 0.8 times — comfortably inside management's own 1.5-times target — and there is still nothing. This is the question that has been deflected the longest, and it is now the one with the least remaining cover.

**Unit costs.** Roald Ross of Clarksons pressed in November 2025 on an 8% rise in mining costs and a 28% rise in processing costs at Caraíba. The answer — normalise for volume, blame grade mix and Brazilian inflation, point at currency hedges — was reasonable but not quantified. Anita Soni of CIBC asked what Xavantina's processing costs actually are and was told "I don't have that right in front of me." Cost transparency is the softest part of the disclosure, and the subsequent upward revision to Xavantina's cost guidance in August 2026 shows the analysts were right to keep pushing.

**Furnas resource confidence.** Anita Soni pushed hardest here in March 2026, on drill density, dilution assumptions, and the split between measured, indicated and inferred material. Management's answer is the most important disclosure in the entire transcript set and deserves to be quoted: "about 60% of the material that we have, including the [preliminary economic assessment] is inferred." Inferred material is the lowest-confidence resource category — it is an estimate, not a reserve, and it cannot legally be used in a feasibility-grade mine plan. Management volunteered this rather than burying it, and separately clarified that while the resource statement carries no dilution, the mine plan is fully diluted. That is honest disclosure of a genuine weakness.

**Reserve dilution at Xavantina.** Soni also flagged that the Matinha vein converted from resource to reserve at far worse than the stated 23% dilution assumption — grade fell from about 11 grams per tonne to 6.65, roughly a 40% haircut against a 23% assumption. Management explained it as a stope-geometry effect and offered to follow up offline. This is a small asset, but it is a data point that the geological models run optimistic.

**What analysts are not asking about.** Nobody is pressing on how a company with a $3.7 billion market value funds its 71% share of a $1.3 billion mine build — the 60% earned interest plus Vale's free carry of up to 11 percentage points of construction capital. That question arrives in 2027 or 2028, and it is the one that decides whether Furnas is an asset or a claim on shareholders.

## 6. Red Flags and Green Flags

**Red flags**

- *Capital returns promised since March 2025 and still absent (as of 5 Aug 2026).* Leverage has cleared management's own 1.5-times target with room to spare. The stated reason has shifted from "delever first" to "pay down the revolver first." Each restatement of step four without action erodes it.
- *2025 guidance was reaffirmed twice and then cut hard (August 2025).* The pattern was: confident reaffirmation, then a single large capitulation. That is the sequence to watch for in 2026's second half.
- *Cost guidance drifting upward (August 2026).* Xavantina's mined-gold all-in sustaining costs and cash costs were both revised higher, and capital guidance rose. Small revisions, wrong direction, immediately after a mechanisation programme that was supposed to cut costs 30%.
- *The "last big capital year" claim is untested (March 2026, restated August 2026).* Capital spending has risen every year through this cycle and was raised again in August. A capital cliff that keeps being scheduled for next year is a forecast, not a fact, until the 2027 guidance is actually published.
- *Superlative-heavy language.* "Incredible" appears repeatedly across five calls, applied to the business, the assets, the partnership and the year. This is standard promoter vocabulary. It has been backed by results so far; that is the only thing keeping it off the serious-concern list.
- *Furnas rests on 60% inferred material and a preliminary economic assessment* — the lowest-confidence study tier there is. The pre-feasibility study has moved out to 2027. Between now and then the project's value is an estimate built on estimates.
- *Single-country concentration.* Every operating asset, and the growth project, sits in Brazil. The currency has been a persistent headwind and the company runs a large currency-collar programme to manage it. Management named "seaborne shipping freight" and the Brazilian real as active headwinds in March 2026.

**Green flags**

- *No equity dilution through a full build cycle.* Diluted shares rose from 103.5 million to 105.9 million between end-2024 and mid-2026 — roughly 2%, essentially all employee plans. A mine, a shaft, a mechanisation programme and a 75,000-metre drill campaign were funded with debt, a prepayment facility and a stream. In a sector defined by serial share issuance, this is the strongest single signal in the file.
- *Deleveraging delivered on schedule.* Net debt down from $552 million to $452.7 million; leverage from 2.6 times to 0.8 times.
- *Value created from within the existing footprint.* The Xavantina concentrate stockpile went from an unexamined pile to a declared resource to invoiced shipments in about twelve months, at a cash cost of $633 to $715 per ounce against gold above $4,000. Management laid out the work plan in late 2024 and delivered it.
- *Operating turnaround is measurable, not asserted.* A 50% reduction in unplanned infrastructure downtime, a 10%-plus improvement in equipment availability, record mill throughput at Caraíba of just over 400,000 tonnes in October 2025, debottlenecking achieved "at effectively 0 cost."
- *Deliberate under-guiding in 2026.* The Tucumã filtration expansion was excluded from guidance on purpose.
- *Honest disclosure on the weakest asset.* Volunteering the 60%-inferred figure at Furnas, and clarifying the dilution treatment, are things a promoter would not do.
- *A named, credible partner.* Vale Base Metals is the counterparty at Furnas, and the agreement is a definitive executed earn-in, not a memorandum of understanding.

## 7. Hidden Signals

**The vocabulary shifted from problems to systems.** The 2025 calls talk about specific broken things — a valve, a weld, a filter. The 2026 calls talk about frameworks: predictive maintenance, dispatch and tracking systems, fleet management, an "operational excellence framework," and by August 2026 a named cost programme ("OneEro") credited with $10 to $15 million of annualised contract savings plus more than $20 million of improved smelting terms. Moving from anecdote to system is what a maturing operator sounds like.

**Management refreshed at the top and in the middle.** DeFilippo took over as chief executive in early 2025 and was, by his own account, "the first employee at Ero Copper 9 years ago" — so this is continuity, not a rescue hire. Farooq Hamed joined as head of investor relations in July 2025. Management repeatedly references hiring "new leadership in critical roles all throughout the company." A new investor-relations voice arriving right before the record-setting quarters is worth noting for what it says about how the story is now being packaged.

**A quiet retreat from mergers and acquisitions.** In March 2025 the chief executive said acquisitions were not an immediate focus and would not happen before Tucumã was delivered — "no, it won't happen." By May 2025 the framing was regional land consolidation around existing operations. By 2026 it has vanished entirely. Discipline, and the right call.

**Treatment charges are the buried lever.** In March 2026 management was asked about smelter treatment charges — the fee a miner pays a smelter to process its concentrate — and said what they hear in the market is "well below zero," while confirming they are "not reflecting that at either of our operations" because their own contracts are long-term. Negative treatment charges mean smelters are paying miners to take concentrate, which is a structural signal that mined copper supply is genuinely scarce. Ero has chosen contracted stability over that spot upside. That is the correct risk decision and a quiet forgone gain.

**"The right mix of metals at exactly the right time in the commodity price cycle" (March 2026).** Management is explicitly telling shareholders that the cycle is working in their favour. Read the other way, this is an acknowledgment that a large part of the current earnings power is price, not operations.

## 8. Capital-Allocation Verdict

**Trajectory: strengthening.**

This is a genuinely improving operator. The four-step plan was stated, held to, and three of its four steps have been executed. The business was rebuilt in 2025 and is harvesting in 2026. Two of the three mines are running at records, leverage has fallen from 2.6 times to 0.8 times, and none of it cost shareholders a share.

**The single thing to weigh before sizing a position: almost none of the improvement in reported earnings came from cost reduction, and a great deal of it came from the copper price.** Copper trades at $6.63 per pound today, against a 200-day average of $5.86 and a 52-week low of $4.41. Consolidated cash costs were $2.42 per pound in the second quarter of 2026, which is a mid-range position on the global cost curve — not first quartile, and above the level the company itself is guiding for the full year. Revenue nearly doubled year over year while the gross margin held roughly flat at 41% to 43%; volume and price did the work. The company's own sensitivity proxy says a 10% move in copper swings operating cash profit by about 14.5% — and that cuts both ways. At the 52-week low copper price, the same production and the same costs produce roughly 45% less operating cash profit, against $285 to $330 million of committed capital spending. Size this as what it is: a leveraged claim on the copper price, run by a capable and honest management team.

**Economic moat: narrow, and widening.** A copper concentrate producer is a price-taker; whatever durable advantage exists lives in the ore body and the cost position. Ero's is real but modest: a 20-year reserve life at Caraíba, roughly 12 years at Tucumã, and a returns profile that is genuinely strong — return on invested capital of 15.6% and return on equity of 30.1% on a trailing basis, with gross margins rising rather than falling. The direction is widening, on three specific mechanisms: Tucumã is a structurally lower-cost mine ($2.10 per pound) that is displacing higher-cost tonnes in the mix; the Pilar shaft, due in 2027, cuts a one-hour ramp haul to a five-minute hoist and should take a real bite out of Caraíba's $2.76 per pound; and Furnas, if it is ever built, is modelled at a first-quartile $0.24 per pound. The caveat is that the returns evidence is measured at a record copper price, so some of the "widening" is the metal, not the moat.

**Secular threat to terminal value: none.** Copper is on the right side of electrification, grid build-out and data-centre demand — the structural forces run toward this business, not against it. The risks here are cyclical (the copper price), operational (Brazilian execution and cost inflation), and financial (funding a $1.3 billion build), not terminal. This is not a shrinking base dressed up as a cheap stock. But nor is the cheapness a fear-print: the equity has roughly tripled from its 52-week low and trades at a market multiple, so the discount that would make the narrow moat worth underwriting is not currently on offer.
