# Forensic dossier — Cameco Corporation (CCJ)

*Prepared 2026-08-04. Transcripts reviewed: Q4 2024 (2025-02-20), Q1 2025 (2025-05-01), Q2 2025
(2025-07-31), Q3 2025 (2025-11-05), Q4 2025 (2026-02-13), plus the Q2 2026 call (2026-07-31)
verified from published coverage. Financials are Cameco's reported figures, which are in Canadian
dollars; the shares quoted here trade in US dollars on the New York Stock Exchange. The exchange
rate used throughout is 1.408 Canadian dollars per US dollar (live, 2026-08-04).*

---

## 1. Narrative arc

The story Cameco told across these six quarters barely moved — and that consistency is itself the
most useful finding. Chief executive Tim Gitzel opened the Q4 2024 call saying market fundamentals
"are stronger than they've been for decades," and the Q4 2025 call closed on the same note. In an
industry where management teams routinely re-invent the pitch to match the tape, Cameco did not.

What *did* change is where the weight of the story sits. Three shifts are visible:

**From uranium to the whole fuel cycle.** In Q4 2024 the emphasis was the uranium contract book:
"commitments to deliver an average of about 28 million pounds of uranium over the next five years,"
against "approximately 220 million pounds" under long-term contract. By Q4 2025 that book had grown
to "approximately 230 million pounds" and the same 28-million-pound annual commitment — meaning the
contract portfolio grew about 5% in a year while the delivery commitment did not move at all. In the
same window the narrative expanded outward to conversion ("historic pricing"), fuel fabrication, and
laser enrichment. That is a deliberate re-framing of Cameco as a fuel-cycle company rather than a
miner, and it is fair: the conversion business genuinely is earning historic prices.

**From Westinghouse-as-investment to Westinghouse-as-the-story.** In Q4 2024, Westinghouse was
discussed as a 6%-to-10% core growth rate with new-build reactors framed as "upside." By Q3 2025 the
company was announcing a partnership with the US government "backed by at least USD 80 billion in
planned investments." By Q2 2026 (2026-07-31) Westinghouse had confidentially filed a draft
registration statement for its own stock-market listing. In eighteen months the 49% stake went from
a footnote to the load-bearing element of the equity story.

**Supply discipline hardened into an identity.** The phrase "we will never front-run the market"
(Q2 2025) became "we're not going to make heroic decisions with our operating assets when the market
is not yet valuing it" (Q3 2025) and "we are simply not prepared to satisfy that demand at today's
economics" (Q4 2025). Read charitably, this is a producer refusing to destroy its own price. Read
sceptically, it is also a convenient framing for an operation that has repeatedly missed its own
production targets — a point I return to in section 4.

One driver was quietly dropped. In Q4 2024, chief financial officer Grant Isaac walked through a
detailed layered growth case for Westinghouse (6% core, 10% with energy systems, more with Korean
collaborations, more again with the small AP300 and eVinci designs — "it gets silly really fast").
By Q2 2026, with the listing filed, management said almost nothing: "consistent with the US
Securities and Exchange Commission rules governing the process, we are extremely limited in what we
can say." That silence is legally required, but it means the most valuable part of the company is
now the least discussed part.

The most important divergence is not in any single line but in the shape of the whole: the narrative
describes a company entering its harvest, while the financials describe a company whose cash
generation halved, whose costs rose 36%, whose output fell 15%, and whose return on invested capital
is 3.9% on a trailing basis. Both can be true — a contract book repricing upward takes years to
reach the income statement — but a director should be clear that the harvest has not arrived.

There is one claim the numbers flatter rather than contradict. The screen's deterministic proxy reads
a 24.9% trailing EBITDA margin. Cameco's own adjusted EBITDA was C$899M on C$1,659M of first-half
2026 revenue — a 54% margin, and the uranium segment alone earned C$252M on roughly C$661M of
second-quarter uranium sales (38%). The proxy understates because Cameco's 49% of Westinghouse is
accounted for as an equity investment and sits outside consolidated EBITDA entirely. Anyone reading
the screen figure alone would badly underrate the operating business.

## 3. Tone and confidence trajectory

**Stable — unusually so, and deliberately flat.**

- *Q4 2024:* "the outlook for nuclear power and nuclear fuel fundamentals is more favorable than it
  has been for decades." Confident, but paired with a very specific defence of why spot-market
  volumes are misleading: of 46 million pounds traded in spot in 2024, "only about 15% was bought by
  utilities."
- *Q1 2025:* Defensive tilt appears for the first time, entirely around trade policy — the US
  Section 232 investigation naming uranium as a critical mineral. Gitzel: "we take nothing for
  granted. That was a different time and a different trade environment."
- *Q2 2025:* Back on the front foot, and the discipline language sharpens: "Cameco will never
  front-run the market."
- *Q3 2025:* The most combative moment in the set, aimed at competitors rather than the market —
  Isaac on why uranium has not repriced: "everybody who's invested in a producer who is
  undisciplined, who is over promotional and sensational needs to tell that management team to
  understand how the market works."
- *Q4 2025:* Steadiest tone of the six. Notably, Gitzel pre-recorded his remarks and skipped the
  question-and-answer session to fly to Washington — an unusual choice that signals where the
  company thinks value now sits.
- *Q2 2026:* Confidence intact on the market ("we found ourselves back into a mid-'90s long-term
  uranium price on its way to three digits likely") and conspicuously absent on Westinghouse, for
  regulatory reasons.

Guidance became *less* specific over the period in one respect that matters: the Westinghouse growth
architecture laid out in such detail in Q4 2024 has not been updated with comparable granularity
since, and the listing process now forbids it. Elsewhere, disclosure got sharper — the Q2 2026
disclosure of contract terms ("floors in the high 70s per pound and ceilings around $160, both
escalated") is the most concrete pricing detail management has given in years, and it is genuinely
useful.

## 4. Guidance credibility

**Medium — split cleanly by function.**

*Marketing and contracting: high.* Every commitment made about the contract book was delivered.
The 220-million-pound portfolio guided in Q4 2024 became roughly 230 million pounds by Q4 2025.
The five-year commitment of about 28 million pounds a year has been restated identically five times
and reaffirmed again in the second quarter of 2026. Realized-price guidance was *raised* mid-2026,
not cut.

*Mining execution: low, and repeatedly so.* This is where the record breaks.

- In Q4 2024, McArthur River and Key Lake were guided to 18 million pounds for 2025 (on a
  100%-owned basis). In August 2025 that was cut to 14–15 million pounds. Actual 2025 output was
  15.1 million pounds — the top of a range that had already been reduced by about 17%.
- Asked directly in Q2 2025 whether wildfires were behind cautionary language on Cigar Lake and
  McArthur River, Gitzel answered "the fires are not really the principal cause" and listed ground
  freezing, labour and equipment commissioning instead. Three weeks later the guidance was cut.
- In July 2026 Cigar Lake was suspended for roughly two weeks because of problems at a mill Cameco
  does not operate.
- Second-quarter 2026 production was 3.9 million pounds, down 15% year over year.

*Cost guidance: recently missed.* Full-year unit-cost guidance was raised in the second quarter of
2026. Chief financial officer Heidi Shockey attributed it to currency: the change "was really as a
result of the impact of the foreign exchange, mainly on our purchases." That is an honest answer,
and partly a genuine pass-through — a stronger US dollar inflates the Canadian-dollar cost of
purchased pounds while also inflating Canadian-dollar revenue. But it is still a raise.

*Earnings delivery: weak in the most recent print.* Second-quarter 2026 adjusted earnings of
about US$0.13 per share came in roughly half of the US$0.26–0.28 the street expected.

No sandbagging pattern is evident. If anything the reverse: production guidance is set optimistically
in February and trimmed mid-year, which has now happened often enough to be a pattern rather than
bad luck.

## 5. Analyst pressure points

**Where floors and ceilings sit.** Craig Hutchison of TD Cowen pushed on this in Q1 2025 and got a
partial answer that was then cut off by a technical fault ("we're still holding out for floors that
are in the $70 escalated"). It took until the second quarter of 2026 to get the full disclosure —
floors in the high US$70s and ceilings near US$160, both escalated. This matters enormously and was
under-disclosed for five quarters.

**Whether production delays roll forward.** Lawson Winder of Bank of America asked directly in
Q3 2025 how to handicap McArthur River delays spilling into 2026. Isaac's answer never engaged the
operational question and pivoted to strategy: "it would require a change of our strategy, which
would require more demand in the market for us to do anything different." That is a non-answer.
Nine months later Cigar Lake was suspended and second-quarter output fell 15%.

**When utilities will actually contract.** Gordon Johnson pressed in Q3 2025 for the precursor to
serious long-term contracting. Isaac's reply blamed rival producers for selling into spot rather
than naming an observable trigger. Analysts have now been told for six straight quarters that
contracting is below replacement rate and that this is bullish. At some point the absence of
contracting stops being a coiled spring and starts being the market's verdict — no analyst has put
that to management directly.

**Westinghouse's economics.** The most valuable asset is the least examined. On the Q2 2026 call,
questions were "primarily clarificatory regarding timing and mechanics"; no analyst challenged the
valuation. Cameco carries roughly C$2.9B of long-term investments on its balance sheet — the
Westinghouse stake, plus the Inkai joint venture and the laser-enrichment interest — against a market
that appears to be paying an order of magnitude more for the Westinghouse piece alone.

**What analysts worry about that management isn't addressing:** the widening gap between the
realized price (US$67.79 per pound) and both the spot price (about US$86.63 on 2026-08-03) and the
long-term indicator (about US$93 per pound). Cameco's own book is being delivered *below* the market
it is telling investors is tightening. That is the arithmetic of price floors and ceilings agreed
years ago, and it is the single biggest gap between the story and the cash.

## 6. Red flags and green flags

**Concerning**

- Free cash flow halved, from C$1,075M in 2025 to C$534M on a trailing basis to 2026-06-30, while
  capital spending rose to C$386M. The company is spending more and generating less. *(Q2 2026)*
- Unit cost of sales up 36% year over year to C$62.21 per pound, with full-year cost guidance
  raised. A producer sliding *up* the cost curve while the commodity price rises is not compounding
  an advantage. *(Q2 2026)*
- Gross margin fell to 21.1% in the second quarter of 2026 from 29.3% a year earlier, on revenue
  down about 7%. Margin compressing on a shrinking top line is the least attractive combination
  there is. *(Q2 2026)*
- Three separate operational interruptions inside twelve months (Key Lake/McArthur road access,
  Cigar Lake suspension, McArthur development delays), one of them at a mill Cameco does not
  control. *(Q3 2025 – Q2 2026)*
- Proven and probable reserves fell to 433 million pounds (Cameco's share) at the end of 2025 from
  457 million a year earlier — 25 million pounds mined, essentially nothing added. At current output
  that is roughly a twenty-year life, which is excellent, but the direction is depletion.
- Westinghouse is increasingly described in opportunity counts rather than contracted backlog: "at
  least USD 80 billion in planned investments" *(Q3 2025)*, "91 identified AP1000 opportunities"
  *(Q2 2026)*. Pipelines are not orders. In this sector that distinction is where investors get hurt.
- Return on invested capital is 3.9% and return on equity 5.1% on a trailing basis. Those are not
  the returns of a business the market is valuing at roughly 33 times its look-through earnings
  before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation.

**Positive**

- Capital discipline is verified through a full down-cycle, which is rare in mining. Capital
  spending was cut from C$217M in 2016 to C$55M in 2018 and C$58M in 2019; the dividend was cut from
  C$158M in 2016–17 to C$24M in 2019–20 rather than funded with debt; McArthur River was idled for
  four years rather than run into a weak market.
- No share buybacks at the top of the cycle, and no serial dilution: shares outstanding went from
  435.2 million in early 2025 to 435.6 million in mid-2026. The one meaningful equity raise, about
  C$963M in 2022, funded the Westinghouse purchase. This is the single most common way uranium
  investors are destroyed, and Cameco does not do it. *(Balance sheet, 2016–2026)*
- Net cash: C$1,113M of cash against C$1,009M of total debt at 2026-06-30, plus an undrawn C$1.0B
  credit line. The company cannot be forced to sell anything.
- Management is actively anti-promotional, publicly criticising rivals who are "over promotional and
  sensational" *(Q3 2025)*, and refuses to guide Westinghouse growth beyond evidence-based triggers
  *(Q4 2024)*.
- The contract structure disclosed in Q2 2026 — floors in the high US$70s, ceilings near US$160,
  both escalated — is genuine downside protection on newly layered volumes. A uranium price collapse
  does not immediately collapse Cameco's realized price.
- Long-term uranium pricing reached decade highs in the first half of 2026, and Cameco raised
  realized-price and revenue guidance on the strength of it.

## 7. Hidden signals

**"Supply discipline" is doing two jobs.** It is a real and defensible commercial strategy — refusing
to sell uncommitted pounds into a market that has not yet paid for them. It is also, increasingly,
the frame in which operational shortfalls are presented. In Q3 2025 Isaac answered a direct question
about McArthur River delays affecting 2026 by saying "we're not going to take any heroic actions."
That reframes an inability into a choice. A director should watch whether 2027 guidance, due in
February 2027, treats the reduced run-rate as strategy or as the new baseline.

**The purchased-pound tell.** Cameco sold 7.1 million pounds in the second quarter of 2026 while
producing 3.9 million. The gap was filled from inventory (8.7 million pounds carried at C$58.05) and
from purchases at C$91.40 per pound — within 2% of the C$93.13 realized price. Pounds bought at the
selling price earn nothing. The more often production misses, the more the contract book becomes a
pass-through rather than a margin.

**Terminology drift on Westinghouse.** The Q4 2024 language was underwriting language: growth rates,
triggers, "we require evidence based triggers." By Q3 2025 it was deal language, and Isaac introduced
a new concern — "the last thing we want to have is trapped value within this family of assets." That
sentence, nine months before the listing was filed, was the tell. He also said plainly that the
partnership "does not force us to leave Westinghouse in 2029" and that "we will just maximize the
optionality." Cameco has been preparing to surface this value for at least a year.

**New voices, same script.** Grant Isaac moved from chief financial officer to president and chief
operating officer during 2025; Heidi Shockey moved from deputy to chief financial officer. Isaac now
answers the operational questions as well as the strategic ones, which is why operational answers
increasingly arrive in strategic language. Shockey's contributions are the most narrowly factual in
the set — her explanation of the fuel-services price uplift in Q1 2025 ("just the timing of one
particular contract that had a higher price") is a useful corrective to the more expansive framing
around it.

**What the founder-level confidence is attached to.** Note carefully what management is confident
about: the *market*. Nuclear demand, contracting cycles, the structural deficit. They are markedly
less confident, and much vaguer, about their own mines. That asymmetry has been consistent for six
quarters and it has been right about the market and wrong about the mines.

## 8. Capital-allocation verdict

**Classification: STABLE.**

Cameco is not deteriorating and it is not pivoting. It is executing a strategy it has held for years,
inside a commodity cycle that has moved in its favour, with mining execution that is materially
weaker than its marketing execution.

**The single most important thing to weigh before sizing:** the market is not paying for the uranium
business. Back out a reasonable value for the mines, the conversion and fuel-fabrication business,
and the net cash, and what remains implies the market values Cameco's 49% of Westinghouse at roughly
two to three times the US$30 billion threshold that the US government partnership itself uses as the
trigger for its warrant. And Cameco does not keep all of the upside above that level: under the
October 2025 agreement, the US government is entitled to 20% of distributions above US$17.5 billion,
converting into a warrant for 20% of the listed entity's public value above US$17.5 billion if the
listing values Westinghouse at US$30 billion or more by January 2029. Every marginal dollar of
Westinghouse value above that threshold is worth only eighty cents to Cameco's owners. A director
sizing this position is underwriting a nuclear-services listing, not a uranium mine — and is doing so
before the registration statement is public and before anyone outside the company has seen
Westinghouse's audited numbers.

**Economic moat: WIDE.** The moat is the combination of a regulatory licence and an irreplaceable
scale-and-grade cost position. McArthur River is the highest-grade uranium mine in the world and
Key Lake the largest mill; those ore bodies cannot be replicated, and the permits to build anything
comparable take a decade. Port Hope is one of a handful of licensed Western conversion facilities,
which is precisely why conversion pricing is at historic levels. The 49% Westinghouse stake adds the
only Western third-generation-plus reactor design with an operating reference fleet. Isaac's Q3 2025
line — "how grateful we are that we don't have a greenfield project that we have to try to build
right now" — is the moat stated plainly.

**Moat trend: STABLE.** It is not widening. Unit costs rose 36% year over year, gross margin
compressed about eight percentage points, and reserves depleted by 24 million pounds without
replacement. Those are the marks of a business holding its position, not extending it. But the
erosion is cyclical and currency-driven rather than structural, and the contract floors disclosed in
the second quarter of 2026 protect the earnings base on the downside.

**Secular threat: none.** This is the rare resource business where the structural force runs
*toward* the asset. Reactor life extensions, restarts, approvals in China at ten a year, the World
Bank lifting its financing ban, and electricity demand from artificial-intelligence data centres all
enlarge the terminal market. There is no substitute for uranium in a fuel bundle and no elasticity in
the demand for it. Nothing in this file suggests a shrinking base.

**Therefore: not a value trap, and not cheap.** The cheapness question does not arise — this is an
expensive stock attached to a durable franchise. The risk is not that the terminal value melts; it is
that the price already contains it.
