# Forensic Dossier — Vale S.A. (VALE)
**Prepared 2026-08-04 · Interrogator seat · Mining Lane A (profitable operator in a physical value chain)**

Price used throughout: **$14.875 per share, US dollars, NYSE listing (the American depositary share, which represents one Vale ordinary share)**. Source: live quote pulled 2026-08-04; day range $14.81–$15.06, 52-week range $9.67–$17.94, market value about $63.4 billion.

Transcripts reviewed: five consecutive quarterly earnings calls, 2025-04-25 (first quarter 2025) through 2026-04-29 (first quarter 2026). The most recent quarter — the second quarter of 2026, reported 2026-07-31 — is **not** in the transcript bundle, so it has been verified separately from company filings and press coverage and is flagged as such wherever it appears below. That gap matters, because the newest quarter is where the story changed.

## 1. NARRATIVE ARC

Vale spent the five quarters in this bundle telling one story with unusual consistency: *we are the low-cost operator, we will prove it by cutting unit costs every quarter, and we will convert that into cash returned to owners rather than into new mines.*

**Early 2025 — cost discipline as the headline.** The first-quarter 2025 call opened on the "Vale 2030 vision" and then went straight to the number that mattered: iron ore C1 cash cost (the mine-gate cash cost per tonne, before freight and royalties) of $21 per tonne, down 11% year on year, against full-year guidance of $20.50–$22.00. Management also flagged the sale of 70% of Aliança Energia to Global Infrastructure Partners for $1 billion in cash — an asset-light move, not an empire-building one.

**Mid-2025 — the base-metals leg gets promoted.** By the second and third quarters of 2025 the emphasis widened. Nickel production rose 44% year on year in the June quarter; copper's all-in cost fell 60% to $1,400 per tonne, largely because gold by-product credits (Vale's copper mines produce gold alongside the copper, and the gold revenue is subtracted from copper's cost) were unusually rich. Management cut copper cost guidance mid-year — an unusual direction of travel in this industry. The "Novo Carajás" programme was introduced as the vehicle for doubling copper output, with the Bacaba project's capital intensity of $5,400 per tonne of annual capacity held up as proof that Vale can grow cheaply.

**Late 2025 — the victory lap.** The fourth-quarter 2025 call (2026-02-13) reported that Vale exceeded production guidance in all three commodities, landed C1 at $21.30 per tonne (the midpoint of guidance), cut iron ore all-in cost by $2 per tonne to $54, and paid a **16% dividend yield** for the year. Long-term capital spending guidance was reset *below* $6 billion. The chief executive's framing was explicit: "if we are doing 360 million tons in iron ore, C1 below $20, all-in below $50 per ton, and we are doing 700 kilotons in copper. This is certainly a very valuable portfolio."

**2026 — the cost story reverses.** The first-quarter 2026 call was the first crack: C1 jumped 12% year on year to $23.60 per tonne, and management shifted from "we will deliver guidance" to "we are working to achieve the **top end** of our original guidances." Then, in the quarter that is *not* in this bundle — the second quarter of 2026, reported 2026-07-31 — Vale formally **raised** 2026 iron ore cost guidance: C1 to $22.50–$23.50 per tonne (from $20.00–$21.50) and all-in cost to $58–$62 per tonne (from $52–$56). Roughly 70% of the increase was attributed to the Brazilian real and diesel prices.

**Net arc:** the growth and capital-discipline half of the story has strengthened and is verifiable. The cost-leadership half — the part management leaned on hardest through 2025 — has visibly reversed in 2026, for reasons that are largely external but that nonetheless cost real money.

**The screen-versus-reality gap you must not miss.** Our deterministic proxy prints a trailing EBITDA margin (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, as a share of revenue) of **25.0%** and net debt of 1.50 times EBITDA. Both are artefacts. In the fourth quarter of 2025 Vale booked a **$3.5 billion non-cash impairment on its Canadian nickel assets** (long-term nickel price assumptions were cut) plus a **$2.8 billion write-off of deferred tax assets**, producing a $3.8 billion reported quarterly loss. The mechanical calculation sweeps that charge into trailing EBITDA. Vale's own "pro forma" EBITDA — the operating measure the company and the street use — was $4.4 billion, roughly $4.6–4.8 billion, $3.9 billion and $4.066 billion in the last four quarters, i.e. **about $17 billion on roughly $40.5 billion of revenue, a ~42% margin**. Leverage on that honest base is about **0.97 times**, not 1.50. Anyone screening Vale on the mechanical numbers will conclude it is a mid-margin, mid-leverage miner. It is neither.

The same distortion runs through the multiple. On the mechanical figure Vale prints 8.39 times enterprise value to EBITDA — *more expensive* than Rio Tinto at 7.20 and barely below BHP at 8.64. On pro forma EBITDA it prints roughly **5 times**. The screen inverts the ranking.

## 3. TONE & CONFIDENCE TRAJECTORY

**Rating: Stable, with a specific and recent softening on costs.**

- **Q1 2025 — confident, hedged on macro.** "The current trade war only reinforces the importance of building a competitive business." Guidance language is firm: "we're even more confident in achieving our C1 cost guidance."
- **Q2 2025 — most confident of the five.** "We remain highly confident in achieving our full year guidance for both our C1 and all-in costs, implying year-over-year cost reductions despite inflationary pressures." Cost guidance was *cut* for copper mid-year — companies do not do that unless they are sure.
- **Q3 2025 — expansive.** The tone broadened from operations to reputation: dam-safety milestones, the removal of the last emergency-level-3 dam, and an estimate that roughly $1.5 trillion of environmental, social and governance-mandated assets can now buy the shares again.
- **Q4 2025 — celebratory but disciplined.** "2025 was simply an outstanding year." Notably, management *declined* to chase acquisitions: "we still trade at a discount to peers of about 20%. So for us, from a value accretion standpoint, it is certainly better to develop the endowment that we have."
- **Q1 2026 — the first defensive quarter.** The guidance verb changes: "we are working to achieve the top end of our original guidances." The cost slide becomes a *sensitivity* slide — a tell that management expects to be judged against variables it does not control. "While external variables such as exchange rates and oil prices can introduce volatility to our cost structure..."

The shift is from offensive (we will beat our cost target) to defensive (here is why the target moved). It is honest, quantified, and it happened one quarter before the formal guidance reset — which is the right order.

## 4. GUIDANCE CREDIBILITY

**Score: Medium-High.** The 2025 record is genuinely good; the 2026 record is a clean miss, disclosed early with a quantified cause.

**Delivered or beat:**
- 2025 C1 guidance $20.50–$22.00 → landed $21.30 (the midpoint). Verified against the Q4 2025 call.
- 2025 production guidance exceeded in all three commodities.
- 2025 capital spending guidance of $5.9 billion met; long-term guidance cut below $6 billion after a review that found more than $500 million of annual savings.
- Copper cost guidance cut twice and beaten both times (to negative all-in cost).
- Onça Puma's second furnace delivered on schedule and **13% below planned capital cost** — a specific, checkable, favourable variance in an industry where the chief executive of the base-metals arm himself estimated sector-wide capital overruns "in the order on average of about 40%."
- Serra Sul +20 tracked publicly through the bundle — 73% physical progress (March 2025), 80% (Q3 2025), 86% (Q1 2026), start-up "second half of 2026" — and **commenced operations in July 2026**, verified from the 2026-07-31 release. Guidance kept and delivered on a multi-year project.

**Missed:**
- 2026 iron ore C1 and all-in cost guidance, both formally raised on 2026-07-31, roughly one quarter into the year. All-in cost moved $6 per tonne, which on roughly 330 million tonnes of annual sales is close to **$2 billion of annualised EBITDA**. Management attributes about 70% to the Brazilian real and diesel.

**No sandbagging pattern.** Vale guides to ranges it usually lands inside, and when it beats (copper) it re-guides rather than banking the beat. The one systematic caution: the cost guidance is quoted at assumed exchange-rate and oil levels, so a "miss" can be entirely a currency event. That is disclosed, but it means the cost-leadership claim is partly a claim about the Brazilian real.

## 5. ANALYST PRESSURE POINTS

**The recurring three, and how management handles them:**

1. **"When do you step up buybacks?"** — asked by Jefferies (Q1 2025), Morgan Stanley (Q4 2025), and implicitly every quarter. The answer never changes and is admirably non-committal: returns depend on where expanded net debt sits versus the $15 billion midpoint *and* on the share price. This is not evasion; it is a stated policy, and the June-2026 authorisation of a 100-million-share buyback is the policy firing.

2. **The "expanded net debt" definition** — pressed by Bank of America twice (Q3 2025 and Q1 2026). This is the sharpest analyst line of attack in the bundle and it deserves attention. Vale's headline leverage measure *adds* the present value of the Mariana and Brumadinho disaster reparations to financial debt. Roughly one-third of the $16.7 billion is reparation obligations. The chief financial officer's answer is unusually candid: "the obligation related to reparations is different from a regular debt because it cannot be refinanced. We need to pay as they mature." He also pre-announces that the definition will likely be revisited "not until the end of '27" — i.e. once the payments have largely run off. **Read this as a disclosed, dated, non-refinanceable cash claim on the next two years of free cash flow, not as accounting games.**

3. **The "caves decree"** — Brazil's regulation protecting natural caves, which constrains where Vale can mine. Asked in Q2 2025; the answer was hopeful ("it's certainly an initiative that is led by the government") with a contingency ("even under a new caves decree scenario, we will make sure that we deliver"). This is a genuine open regulatory item with no date attached.

**Where management gave a real number under pressure and it later mattered:** in Q1 2026 an analyst pushed the commercial head on how much supply exits at lower prices. The answer — a $10 per tonne fall takes "more than 50 million tons of iron ore production" to negative margins — is the single most useful sentence in the bundle for pricing the downside, because it defines where the cost curve pushes back.

**What analysts worry about that management does not fully address:** the structural demand question. Vale's answer on Chinese domestic supply (falling from ~260 to ~160 million tonnes) and scrap ("very gradual") and Simandou ("offset by depletion in the industry") is coherent but is the answer every seaborne producer gives. It is an assertion about the next decade, not evidence.

## 6. RED FLAGS / GREEN FLAGS

**🔴 Red flags**

- **The cost streak broke, and the break is only one quarter old.** C1 up 12% year on year in Q1 2026, up 9% in Q2 2026, and both 2026 cost guidance ranges formally raised on 2026-07-31. The single loudest claim of the 2025 story is currently not true.
- **A $3.5 billion nickel impairment (Q4 2025) is an admission the Canadian nickel base was carried too high.** It was triggered by cutting long-term nickel price assumptions. This is the same asset base whose "asset review" and cost improvements management spent 2025 praising. The operations improved; the orebody's value did not.
- **A $2.8 billion deferred-tax write-off in the same quarter** — a Brazilian tax-position reversal, and a reminder that Vale's tax and royalty base is contested. In Q1 2026 an analyst raised the Federal Attorney General's office overturning an injunction on the royalty calculation base; the chief financial officer's answer was "there are some things that don't depend on us."
- **Governance instability, state-adjacent.** Verified outside the bundle: Previ, the pension fund of employees of state-controlled Banco do Brasil and a roughly 7% holder, moved to remove chairman Daniel Stieler. The board voted against the proposal on 2026-06-22; Stieler resigned on 2026-07-07, days before the shareholder vote; the item was pulled from the 2026-07-22 meeting and Previ backed a replacement. There is also reported regulatory scrutiny of the outgoing chairman's payout. Vale is a private company with a permanent political shareholder problem.
- **Costs are partly a currency bet.** Management's own framing — 2026 guidance assumes a Brazilian real of 5.25 and oil at $90 — means "lowest cost curve position" is contingent on a weak local currency.
- **Labour costs stepped up.** Two United Steelworkers agreements ratified in June 2026 (Sudbury Local 6500, Port Colborne Local 6200) carry guaranteed wage increases of 20.5%–25.7% over the contract life. Small relative to group costs, but it is a structural cost floor in the Canadian nickel business that was just impaired.

**🟢 Green flags**

- **Capital discipline through a strong tape — the sector's besetting sin, avoided.** Long-term capital spending guided *below* $6 billion after a review that found over $500 million of savings; a 16% dividend yield paid in 2025; and a flat refusal to buy growth. Q4 2025: "M&A, yes, a lot of people are doing M&A. But we don't need to do... we still trade at a discount to peers." Compare this with the 2011–2014 cycle, when Vale built fertilisers, Mozambican coal and New Caledonian nickel at the top and wrote all three down. This management is not repeating it.
- **Growth at a fraction of peer capital intensity, with third-party evidence.** Serra Leste expansion at **$20 per tonne** of annual capacity; Bacaba copper at **$5,400 per tonne**, roughly half what it was a year earlier, with the internal return moving "from mid-teens to over 50%"; Salobo particle flotation adding ~30 kt of copper at "nearly half the capital intensity." Onça Puma's furnace came in 13% under budget. These are checkable, and the pattern is consistent.
- **"Value over volume" is practised, not just said.** Vale withdrew 8 million tonnes from the market in Q4 2024 because prices did not justify shipping. A producer that will shrink volume to protect price is behaving like an owner.
- **Commercial strategy is adding real money.** Portfolio and premium initiatives added "over $500 million... on an annualised basis" (Q3 2025) and all-in premiums rose $2.60 per tonne quarter on quarter in Q1 2026, worth "around $800 million in annualised revenue."
- **Freight risk was pre-empted.** Q4 2025: "our exposure to the freight spot market today is very low" — said *before* the freight market ran, and it held.
- **Base metals is now a real second leg.** Copper 382 kt in 2025 (+10%), 102 kt in Q1 2026 (best since 2017); copper all-in cost negative; base metals is now roughly a quarter of revenue and grew ~42% year on year in Q2 2026.
- **Base-metals transparency is deliberate.** Stand-alone asset reports were published after the March 2026 base-metals investor day. Management is openly trying to close the sum-of-the-parts gap.
- **Real disaster-risk reduction.** All emergency-level-3 tailings dams eliminated by the 2025 commitment date; structures at any emergency level down 80% since 2020; the global tailings standard implemented. This is the tail risk that destroyed roughly two-thirds of Vale's market value in 2019, and it is being retired on schedule.

## 7. HIDDEN SIGNALS

- **The verb change on guidance.** "We remain highly confident in achieving our full year guidance" (Q2 2025) → "we are working to achieve the top end of our original guidances" (Q1 2026). One quarter later the guidance formally moved. When this management softens a verb, believe it.
- **A new voice with a different framework.** Shaun Usmar, who took over base metals in October 2024, does not talk like a miner — he talks like a capital allocator. "We have to look at this as both miners and investors to find the best path forward"; "the sector's ability to deliver new projects has been pretty bad... capital overruns, particularly on large projects has been in the order on average of about 40%." He arrived, looked at Bacaba in his second week and halved its capital intensity. This is the most valuable new input in the bundle.
- **Hu'u is deliberately excluded from guidance.** The Indonesian copper-gold project — "300,000 to 400,000 tons of copper over 400,000 ounces of gold, first half of the cost curve, give or take $5 billion or so and nearly half a century of life" — is under strategic review and Usmar states plainly: "We have not built that into the guidance or in the projections." An asset of that scale sitting outside guidance is an unpriced option, not a promise.
- **A quiet structural change in the pricing benchmark.** Rogério Nogueira flagged that the price agencies are migrating the reference grade from 62% iron content to 61%, because competitors' products have degraded. Vale's products sit above both. If the benchmark falls to the peer group's grade, Vale's realised premium mechanically widens — a slow, underdiscussed positive.
- **Inventory build framed as strategy.** Q3 2025 built roughly 4 million tonnes of inventory, explained as ore in transit to twenty overseas distribution and blending centres. Plausible and consistent with the commercial strategy — but it is also the classic place a bulk producer parks unsold volume, and it is worth tracking rather than accepting.
- **A cost line that is genuinely a gold bet.** Copper's negative all-in cost depends on gold by-product credits: "for every $100 an ounce moves in gold price, what that does for volume cost for copper is about $135 a time." Base-metals earnings currently carry an embedded long-gold position that nobody underwrote.

## 8. CAPITAL-ALLOCATION VERDICT (for the director)

**Trajectory: STABLE.**

Vale is not a turnaround and not a deterioration. It is a first-quartile-cost bulk producer that spent 2025 executing well, is spending 2026 absorbing a currency-and-diesel cost shock it disclosed early, and is simultaneously building a genuine second earnings leg in copper at roughly half the industry's capital intensity. The strengthening (capital discipline, base metals, dam-risk retirement) and the weakening (unit costs, nickel carrying value, governance) are running at the same time and roughly cancel.

**The single most important thing to weigh before sizing:** the operating leverage cuts both ways and the tape is currently cutting the wrong way. Vale realised **$95.00 per tonne** on iron ore fines in the June 2026 quarter, when the 62% iron benchmark averaged **$108.27**. That benchmark sits at **$93.70 on 2026-08-04** — down about 4.7% in a month and 7.0% year on year. Vale's realisation runs roughly 88% of the benchmark, so at today's price the realised figure is closer to $82 per tonne against an all-in cost guided at $58–$62. Roughly 330 million tonnes of annual sales means each $10 per tonne of price is about $3.3 billion of EBITDA, against pro forma EBITDA of about $17 billion. **The entire question is whether $94 or $105-plus is the right iron ore assumption**, and the answer is worth roughly $6 per share. Do not size this position on the mechanical screen metrics — they are corrupted by a one-off impairment and they make the cheapest of the three diversified majors look like the most expensive.

**Economic moat: WIDE.** The moat type is **scale-cost**, and it is the durable kind — an irreplaceable orebody (Carajás, decades of reserve life at the highest iron grades in the seaborne trade) welded to Vale's own railway and port network, which competitors cannot rent. The evidence is in the cost line, not the rhetoric: C1 of $21.30 per tonne in 2025 against a global cost curve where, on management's own arithmetic, a $10 per tonne price fall drives more than 50 million tonnes of competing supply to negative margins. Vale is nowhere near that boundary. Return on capital employed of 13.8% and return on invested capital of 6.9% are respectable but not spectacular — and both are depressed by the same impairment and tax write-off that distort the margin, so they understate the operating franchise.

**Moat trend: STABLE.** Unit costs rose in 2026, which looks like erosion — but roughly 70% of the increase is the Brazilian real and diesel, not competitive position, and the copper and nickel cost curves moved the *other* way (copper all-in cost is now negative). Meanwhile Serra Sul +20, which started up in July 2026, adds 20 million tonnes at "mid-teens" C1 — below the group average, which mechanically improves the mix. The moat is not widening, but it is not being competed away either.

**Secular threat: MATERIAL, not terminal.** The structural risk is the seaborne iron ore price deck itself: Chinese steel output has plateaued, Simandou in Guinea is adding new low-cost tonnes, and scrap supply grows every year. Management's rebuttal — Chinese domestic ore falling from ~260 to ~160 million tonnes, Simandou "offset by depletion in the industry" — is coherent but is an assertion about the next decade. That force compresses the *price* Vale earns, and therefore its terminal value; it does not impair Vale's *position*, because Vale is at the bottom of the cost curve and would be among the last producers standing. Two partial offsets are real: steel decarbonisation favours high-grade ore, which is Vale's product, and the benchmark grade migrating from 62% to 61% iron mechanically widens Vale's realised premium.

**Fear-print or value trap?** Neither, cleanly. The optical cheapness on the mechanical screen is an artefact and should be discarded. The genuine discount — roughly 5 times pro forma EBITDA against Rio Tinto at 7.2 and BHP at 8.6 — is *partly* real mispricing (the impairment distortion, the base-metals leg the market still values as an iron ore stub) and *partly* a rational Brazil discount for non-refinanceable disaster reparations, a contested royalty and tax base, and a board that a state-adjacent pension fund can reshape at will. It is not a melting business. It is a good business at a price that already reflects a good deal of the good news.
