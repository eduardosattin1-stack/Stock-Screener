# Freeport-McMoRan (FCX) — Forensic Dossier
**Prepared 2026-08-04 · price used: $67.50 (New York Stock Exchange, US dollars, intraday 2026-08-04, up 6.1% on the day) · transcripts: five calls, January 2025 through April 2026 · financials: screener scan plus company filings through the quarter ended 30 June 2026**

---

## 1. Narrative arc

The story management tells has been remarkably consistent for five quarters, which is itself worth noting in a sector where the pitch usually changes with the metal price. The tagline — "foremost in copper" — has not moved. What has moved is the balance between the three legs of the story.

**January 2025.** The pitch was execution plus optionality. Four priorities: hit the plan, scale the low-cost leach programme to a 300 million-pound annual run rate by the end of 2025, finish repairing the Indonesian smelter after a fire, and keep building a growth pipeline. Copper had averaged $4.15 a pound on the London Metal Exchange in 2024. The tone was that of a company waiting for its market to arrive.

**April and July 2025.** Two new threads entered. First, United States trade policy: a February 2025 executive order named copper a critical material and opened a national-security investigation into imports, and management began talking openly about the premium American buyers were paying over the international benchmark. Second, delivery — the smelter started up in May 2025, roughly a month ahead of the revised schedule, and by July management was reporting a quarterly cash operating cost of $1.13 a pound and $3.2 billion of earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation (a rough proxy for cash operating profit, hereafter cash operating profit). This was the high-water mark of confidence in the sample.

**January 2026.** The tone changed. On 8 September 2025 a mudflow of roughly 800,000 tonnes of wet material swept through the Grasberg Block Cave, the underground mine that is the company's single most valuable asset. Seven workers died. Production stopped, force majeure was declared, and roughly 10% of the year's planned copper volume was lost. Richard Adkerson opened the January 2026 call by calling the recovery plan "impressive"; Kathleen Quirk called the incident "humbling." Crucially, the four priorities did not change — execution, leach, technology, growth — but the first one now meant "restore Grasberg."

**April 2026 and after.** The story is now a restoration story with a price tailwind. In February 2026 the company signed a memorandum of understanding with the Indonesian government to extend its operating rights in the Grasberg district for the life of the resource, in exchange for transferring a further 12% of the Indonesian subsidiary to government interests in 2041 (the company's stake falls from 48.76% to roughly 37% from 2042). Copper, meanwhile, went from $4.51 a pound average in 2025 to a record — it traded at $6.65 a pound on the COMEX exchange on the day this note was written, and Freeport realised $6.17 a pound in the June 2026 quarter.

**Quietly dropped or stretched.** One growth driver did slip. The leach programme — extracting copper from waste rock stockpiles the company once considered worthless — was promised at a 300 million-pound annual run rate "by the end of 2025" in January 2025, restated as the same target for the same date in April and July 2025, and then re-set in January 2026 as "a 40% increase in 2026." By the June 2026 quarter the language is "approximately 300 million pounds per year by year-end 2026." The endpoint — 800 million pounds a year — never moved, but the road to it lost a full year. That pattern, a distant target held constant while the near-term milestone slides, is the single clearest piece of narrative inflation in the file.

**Three corrections the screener's own numbers require.**

First, and most important: **the reported free cash flow figure is wrong, and by a wide margin.** The scan carries trailing free cash flow of $5.92 billion, which implies a 6.1% free-cash-flow yield on a $97 billion company. That number is actually *operating* cash flow — the data feed records capital spending as zero for the trailing period. Freeport spent $2.1 billion in the first half of 2026 and guides to $4.3 billion for the full year. Subtracting realistic capital spending of about $4.2 billion, trailing free cash flow is closer to **$1.7 billion**, a free-cash-flow yield of roughly **1.8%**, not 6.1%. Every discounted-cash-flow style model in the scan is therefore fed a number more than three times too large — and yet still prints a fair value of $7.90 and a margin of safety of −100% on all five methods. Those model outputs should be ignored entirely for this name; they are not a signal, they are noise from a broken input.

Second, **profit attributable to Freeport shareholders is far smaller than consolidated profit.** In the June 2026 quarter, profit from continuing operations was $1.386 billion but only $984 million belonged to Freeport shareholders — outside partners took 29%. Across the trailing year the leakage was about 36%. The Indonesian government owns 51.24% of the Grasberg subsidiary, Sumitomo owns 28% of Morenci, and minorities hold 46% of Cerro Verde in Peru. Any valuation built on consolidated cash operating profit without deducting that claim overstates the company by tens of billions of dollars.

Third, **the reported unit cost is net of by-product credits and is flattered by a record gold price.** The $1.90-a-pound 2026 guide assumes gold at $4,000 an ounce for the second half. Freeport realised $4,520 an ounce for gold in the June quarter. On a gross basis this is not a low-cost producer: the company's own 2027 target for its United States mines is $2.50 a pound, which management itself described as challenging given energy and acid costs. The deterministic cohort proxy places Freeport's 34.8% trailing cash operating margin in the *highest-cost* quartile of its copper peer group (12th percentile of eight names). That proxy and the company's own guidance agree: Freeport is a mid-to-high-cost producer whose headline cost looks good only because gold and molybdenum are expensive.

## 3. Tone and confidence trajectory

**Rating: Stable, with one genuine dip and a recovery.**

- *January 2025 — measured.* "We don't take this as a given. It requires that we stay disciplined each day." Confidence in execution, caution about the market.
- *April 2025 — rising.* "Team Freeport is executing well in a complex environment." The smelter repair was ahead of plan; the company had bought back 2.3 million shares in the volatility.
- *July 2025 — peak.* "Copper is currently in the spotlight… prices are hitting all-time highs." A net operating credit of $1.00 a pound at Grasberg. This is the most offensive, growth-forward language in the sample.
- *January 2026 — defensive, and honest about it.* "The Grasberg incident was humbling." Adkerson: "PTFI has a well-designed plan to recover. Now we must execute, and we will." The shift is unmistakable — from capturing upside to restoring a base.
- *April 2026 — recovering, with specificity.* Management gave a dated ramp schedule (65% of capacity in the second half of 2026, 80% by mid-2027, near-full by end-2027) and named the exact bottleneck: ore chutes that could not handle wet material. Naming a mechanical bottleneck and quantifying its cost at $60–70 million of extra capital spending is the language of people who understand their problem.

Guidance has become **more** specific over time, not less — the opposite of the pattern that usually precedes bad news. The one exception is the leach programme, where the language moved from a hard number and date to a range and a window ("300 million to 400 million pounds per annum in the 2026, 2027 time frame").

## 4. Guidance credibility

**Reliability: High, with one qualified exception.**

What was promised and what arrived:

- *Smelter restart by mid-2025* (promised January 2025) → started May 2025, about a month early. **Over-delivered.**
- *Indonesian export duty falls to zero in 2026* (promised January 2025, in answer to a direct analyst question) → confirmed in January 2026: "we're no longer exporting concentrates. And so we don't have any export duties in our numbers." **Delivered.**
- *2025 sales and unit costs* → copper sales and unit net cash costs finished "slightly better than our adjusted guidance," and full-year unit cost came within 3% of the original January 2025 guide despite a disaster that removed a tenth of the year's volume. **Delivered.**
- *2026 unit cost* → guided at $1.95 a pound in April 2026, cut to $1.90 in July 2026. **Improving.**
- *Leach at 300 million pounds by end-2025* → not achieved; now end-2026. **Missed by a year.**

This reconciles with the earnings-beat record of 6 of 7 quarters (86%). There is a mild pattern of setting achievable operational targets and clearing them, paired with genuinely ambitious innovation targets that slip. That is a healthy asymmetry — the sandbagging is in the numbers that matter for cash, the optimism is in the story.

One item deserves credit rather than suspicion. In July 2025, when analysts pushed on a 200,000-ounce reduction in the five-year Grasberg gold plan, Adkerson pushed *back*: "don't forget to take into account the tax effect of that and also the noncontrolling interest effect… I've observed some people overstating the impact of it." A management team that corrects analysts for being too bearish, using the correct minority-interest arithmetic, is not managing a narrative.

## 5. Analyst pressure points

**Recurring, and largely well answered:**

- *Indonesian export duties and the permit* (Brian MacArthur, Raymond James, four calls running). Management answered precisely each time, and the answers proved right.
- *The Grasberg ramp and its bottlenecks* (Orest Wowkodaw, Scotiabank; Daniel Major, UBS; Liam Fitzpatrick, April 2026). Management named the ore chutes, the wet-to-dry ore ratio, and a $60–70 million capital fix. Specific.
- *Capital allocation and the discretionary spending bucket* (Michael Dudas, January 2025). The answer — that roughly $1 billion of the $3–4 billion net-debt target is earmarked for discretionary projects and excluded from the cash available for shareholder distributions — is transparent, but it is also the mechanism by which capital spending rises without appearing to compete with the dividend.

**Where the answers thin out:**

- *Idle-facility costs.* Wowkodaw asked in April 2026 why costs excluded from reported unit cash costs at Grasberg had risen from $900 million to $1.3 billion — "is that incremental dollars going out, or is that you're just shielding more of that from being included in cash costs?" Quirk's answer was accounting-correct (unabsorbed costs are expensed rather than capitalised into inventory during a ramp) but it does not change the substance: $284 million of real cash costs were kept out of the June 2026 quarter's headline unit cost. The headline flatters until the ramp completes.
- *Leach technology.* The January 2025 exchange with Cory Stevens is the most promotional passage in the file: an artificial-intelligence model generating additive candidates, "we're betting on the come that that's going to work and then go big very quickly." Eighteen months later the near-term target has slipped a year. Analysts have never pressed hard on why.
- *What happens if copper falls.* Across five calls, not one analyst asked management to describe the business at $4.00-a-pound copper. That is a market-wide blind spot, not a management failing — but it is exactly the question this company's shareholders will eventually need answered.

## 6. Red flags and green flags

**Red flags**

- *Seven fatalities, September 2025.* The mudflow at the Grasberg Block Cave killed seven workers and cost roughly a year of the company's most valuable production. Management's handling afterwards was creditable, but the event demonstrates that a single asset carries roughly half the company's earning power and a genuine tail risk.
- *A serially extended innovation target.* The 300-million-pound leach milestone slipped from end-2025 to end-2026 while the 800-million-pound endpoint was held fixed (January 2025 through July 2026).
- *Insiders sell, and only sell.* In the open market: zero purchases against twelve sales totalling 632,274 shares. The people who know the ramp schedule best are not buying it near a record share price.
- *Capital spending is climbing into the price peak.* $4.3 billion in 2026 rising to $4.8 billion in 2027, of which $1.6 billion in 2026 is explicitly discretionary. First-half 2026 free cash flow was only about $1.4 billion (operating cash flow of $3.5 billion less $2.1 billion of capital spending). This is the sector's oldest mistake in its earliest stage — though from a much stronger balance sheet than in past cycles.
- *Buybacks at rising prices.* Freeport repurchased 2.9 million shares at an average of $36.41 in the first half of 2025 — excellent timing. It then bought 1.7 million shares for $110 million in the June 2026 quarter, an average near $64.71. The programme is calendar-driven, not value-driven.
- *A permit that is agreed but not issued.* The February 2026 memorandum on extending Indonesian operating rights is a memorandum. The amended special mining licence that makes it real has not been issued, and no date has been published.

**Green flags**

- *The smelter was delivered early after a fire*, and export duties went to zero exactly as forecast a year in advance.
- *Cost guidance was cut, not raised,* in July 2026 — rare in this sector at this point in a cycle.
- *A genuinely conservative balance sheet.* Net debt of $6.28 billion including leases, 0.7 times trailing cash operating profit; the company's own preferred measure, which excludes $3.2 billion of debt at the Indonesian smelter, is $2.1 billion against a $3–4 billion target.
- *A 25-year reserve life.* Roughly 86.8 billion pounds of proven and probable copper reserves at 31 December 2025 against consolidated sales of 3.1 billion pounds in 2026 rising to 4.1 billion in 2028. Very few businesses of any kind can see that far.
- *A structural United States position.* Freeport produces more than 70% of America's refined copper and owns the smelting and refining capacity that new domestic projects lack. With semi-finished copper carrying a 50% national-security tariff since April 2026 and the COMEX-versus-London premium running near 19 cents a pound — roughly eight times its twenty-year average — this is a real, if policy-dependent, margin advantage on about a third of volumes.
- *Management corrects analysts in both directions* and volunteers the arithmetic that works against a bullish read.

## 7. Hidden signals

- **"Innovation" quietly replaced "growth" as the lead adjective** between January 2025 and January 2026. Growth now requires $4.8 billion a year of capital and a permitting queue; innovation is cheaper to talk about. Watch whether the leach programme delivers the 2026 milestone — if it slips again, the innovation leg of the story is decoration.
- **The word "optionality" appears in every call** and is doing heavy lifting. El Abra, Bagdad, Lone Star sulphides and Kucing Liar are all described as optionality rather than as projects with dates and rates of return. Management is honest that this is deliberate: on Bagdad, "the main caution that we've had with it is just wanting to make sure that we convince ourselves that we can execute the project efficiently within our capital budget." That is discipline. But investors should not pay a project multiple for an option.
- **The Kucing Liar re-sequencing (January 2026) is presented as a positive and mostly is** — moving production rates from 90,000 to 130,000 tonnes a day defers spending on pyrite handling. But it also quietly pulls value from beyond 2041 into the pre-2041 window, which is where Freeport still owns 48.76% rather than 37%. That is value-accretive to today's shareholders and worth understanding as such.
- **Substitution is now acknowledged out loud.** Quirk, January 2026: "There will be substitution and thrifting as prices rise." Adkerson: "that's inevitable, but it will be in the context of a higher copper price." That is the correct answer, and it is also an admission that the demand curve bends.
- **The company does not hedge.** Nothing in five calls describes fixed-price offtake, forward sales, or price collars. Sales are made at benchmark-linked prices with provisional pricing. Shareholders get the whole price move, in both directions.

## 8. Capital-allocation verdict (for the director)

**Trajectory: Stable.**

This is a well-run, honestly managed, conservatively financed owner of irreplaceable long-life copper assets. Management says what it will do and mostly does it. The forensic file is clean by the standards of a sector where it usually is not: no serial equity issuance, no dilution, no reserve write-ups timed to financings, no promoter language beyond one enthusiastic passage about leach additives, and a balance sheet that was not levered up at the top.

**The single most important thing to weigh before sizing a position is this: how much of the company do you actually own, and at what price are you buying it?** Roughly 36–40% of consolidated cash operating profit belongs to the Indonesian government, Sumitomo and the Cerro Verde minorities, not to Freeport shareholders. Strip that out, and at $67.50 the shares are already paying roughly nine to ten times the 2027–2028 cash operating profit the company itself projects at $6.00-a-pound copper — a fuller multiple than BHP (8.6 times), Teck (7.4 times) or Rio Tinto (7.2 times) command on today's trailing numbers at today's strong prices. The quality is genuine. The discount is not there.

The second consideration is symmetry. Copper closed at a record $6.65 a pound on the day of this note and the shares rose 6.1% with it. Freeport's measured sensitivity to copper is 0.91 — it moves almost one-for-one with the metal — and the company's own published sensitivity is roughly $390 million of cash operating profit for every ten cents a pound. A return to the 2024–2025 average of about $4.50 a pound would remove close to half of projected earnings. The same torque that makes the bull case makes the bear case, and today the price embeds only one of them.

**Economic moat: narrow.** The moat type is a mix of scale-cost and regulatory licence — enormous, permitted, long-life orebodies that cannot be replicated, plus a United States smelting and refining position that new entrants do not have. But it is narrow rather than wide because Freeport is not a low-cost producer: its 34.8% trailing cash operating margin sits in the highest-cost quartile of its copper cohort, roughly half of Southern Copper's 63%, and its 2027 United States cost target of $2.50 a pound is acknowledged as challenging. A mine's moat is its position on the cost curve, and Freeport's is respectable, not commanding.

**Moat trend: stable.** Return on invested capital has slipped to 8.8% on a trailing basis from 9.7%, and gross margin contracted through fiscal 2025 — but both are explained by a one-off disaster and the associated idle-facility costs, not by structural erosion. The resource base is being extended, not depleted: the Indonesian life-of-resource agreement, El Abra's permitting, and the leach stockpiles all add years.

**Secular threat: manageable.** Copper is on the winning side of electrification, not the losing side. The only structural threat is substitution and thrifting — aluminium in transmission, thinner copper loadings — which management acknowledged directly in January 2026 and which historically bites only after sustained high prices, capping the metal rather than shrinking the business. The terminal value is not melting. The cheapness question is therefore not a value-trap question at all; it is simply that, at $67.50, the shares are not cheap.
