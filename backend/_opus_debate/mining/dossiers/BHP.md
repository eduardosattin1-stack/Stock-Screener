# BHP Group Limited — Forensic Dossier

**Prepared 4 August 2026.** Price used throughout: **US$87.41 per New York-listed share (NYSE: BHP), the quote as of 4 August 2026**, up 4.7% on the day and within 7% of the 52-week high of $93.83, against a 52-week low of $51.13. One New York share represents two Australian ordinary shares, so every per-share figure below is stated per New York share unless flagged. Transcripts reviewed: the half-year and full-year results calls of February 2024, August 2024, February 2025, August 2025 and February 2026. Everything after February 2026 is verified from company releases and news reporting, and dated in place.

## 1. Narrative arc

BHP has told one story for four years — own the biggest, cheapest, longest-life mines in the commodities the world will need, run them better than anyone else, and let the cash compound. What has changed, quietly, is *how the company proposes to grow*.

**February 2024** was a builder's call. Mike Henry sanctioned Stage 2 of the Jansen potash project in Canada, promised Western Australia Iron Ore would climb "to over 305 million tonnes per annum in the medium term", and flagged studies for 330 million tonnes. Nickel was already visibly sick.

**August 2024** was the first crack. Western Australia Nickel was suspended and written down by $2.7 billion, a $3.8 billion charge was booked for the 2015 Samarco dam failure in Brazil, and the failed pursuit of Anglo American was dismissed in a sentence — "they elected to choose their own to pursue their own plan. We've continued on with our Plan A." Jansen Stage 1 was still described as "ahead of its initial schedule, with first production forecast for late 2026". A new copper joint venture with Lundin Mining in Argentina, later named Vicuña, was introduced.

**February 2025** was the high-water mark of the growth narrative: copper at 39% of earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation (EBITDA — a rough proxy for operating cash profit), the Samarco liability settled at 170 billion Brazilian reais on a defined 20-year schedule, and Jansen Stage 1 "on track for first production by the end of next calendar year."

**August 2025** broke the pattern. Jansen Stage 1 slipped and Henry said plainly, "I'm disappointed. Clearly, this is not where we want to be." Stage 2 was pushed out two years. Group capital spending was *cut* by about $1 billion a year, and the net-debt target range was *raised* from $5–15 billion to $10–20 billion — presented as a sign of strength, but in substance more permission to borrow.

**February 2026** completed the substitution. The growth language was still there — 3% to 4% compound copper-equivalent growth to 2035, "around 5% per year on average in our copper business" — but the genuinely new material was financial, not operational: a $4.3 billion silver streaming agreement over BHP's share of Antamina's future silver, a $2 billion payment for a 25-year tariff on Western Australia Iron Ore's inland power use, and "potential for up to $10 billion in capital that could be unlocked". The interim dividend rose 46% half-on-half to $0.73 per Australian ordinary share (about $1.46 per New York share).

**What has happened since the last transcript** — and it is material:

- **18 June 2026:** BHP disclosed a cost overrun on Jansen Stage 2, lifting the budget to **$6.9 billion** and booking a **$2.3 billion write-down**, with first production pushed to **late financial-year 2031**. The Australian shares fell 5.6%, their worst day in fourteen months (Reuters, 18 June 2026; Proactive, 19 June 2026).
- **1 July 2026:** **Brandon Craig replaced Mike Henry as chief executive**, having already split the Americas leadership role into North and South America — a copper-first organisational design (Reuters/WSJ, 25–26 June 2026).
- **16 July 2026:** the operational review for the year to 30 June 2026 showed **record iron ore of 265 million tonnes** and copper of **1,952,800 tonnes, down about 3%** on the prior year — and guided **financial-year 2027 group copper to 1,650,000–1,800,000 tonnes**, a fall of 8% to 15%, on lower Escondida grades. **Net debt fell to roughly $9 billion** from $14.7 billion six months earlier, helped by $5.2 billion of asset disposals.
- **June–August 2026:** rolling industrial action at Port Hedland, the world's largest iron ore export hub, including a **two-day strike scheduled for the weekend of 8–9 August 2026**, with roughly A$120 million a day of shipments exposed (Reuters, 4 August 2026).

The arc, honestly stated: an outstanding operator whose *build* programme keeps slipping and whose most concrete recent value creation has come from selling forward pieces of its own future revenue.

## 2. Claims versus the financials

Two points of housekeeping first, because they change the arithmetic.

**The input bundle's trailing-twelve-month block is a two-year double count.** BHP reports half-yearly, not quarterly. The bundle's "trailing twelve month" figures — revenue $107.6 billion, EBITDA $52.2 billion, operating cash flow $39.9 billion, free cash flow $20.9 billion, diluted earnings per share $8.52 — are the sum of *four half-year periods*, which is two full years. The correct trailing twelve months to 31 December 2025 is **revenue $54.0 billion, EBITDA $26.9 billion, operating cash flow $19.8 billion, free cash flow $10.3 billion, and diluted earnings per share of $4.04** (the December-2025 half of $2.22 plus the June-2025 half of $1.82). Every valuation below uses the corrected figures.

**The deterministic margin proxy understates the company.** The bundle's EBITDA margin of 48.5% sits below the company's reported underlying margin of 58% for the December-2025 half and 53% for financial-year 2025, because the proxy divides by total revenue including low-margin third-party trading. On a like-for-like basis BHP's margin is genuinely sector-leading — the proxy is conservative, not wrong in direction.

**The caveat on unit costs matters more than anything else in this table.** Management's own words in February 2026: Escondida's cost improvement came "along with higher prices for byproducts", and Copper South Australia's "more than 50% reduction in unit costs" was attributed to "strong gold production". Unit costs at a copper mine are quoted *after* deducting the revenue from gold, silver and uranium sold alongside the copper. As of today gold trades at $4,157 an ounce and silver at $60.17 an ounce — both at or near records. A meaningful slice of BHP's advertised cost leadership in copper is therefore a by-product *price* effect, and it reverses mechanically if precious metals fall, with no change in how well the mine is run. The iron ore cost lead has no such dependency and is the more durable of the two.

## 3. Tone and confidence

**Rising, and by February 2026 close to exuberant — with one genuine break of character.**

- Feb-2024, guarded and safety-focused after a fatality: "we are redoubling our efforts to reinforce our culture and controls."
- Aug-2024, defensive: nickel suspended, "the very tough market conditions for that industry"; the Anglo failure brushed aside.
- Feb-2025, steady: "we are in good shape."
- Aug-2025, the break: "I'm disappointed. Clearly, this is not where we want to be." That is unusually plain language for a chief executive discussing his own flagship project, and it counts in management's favour — they did not bury it.
- Feb-2026, confident: "This is an exciting position to be in"; "Stability plus growth equals value"; a 46% dividend increase.

The more telling shift is in vocabulary rather than volume. Through 2024 the operative verbs were *sanction*, *grow*, *expand*. By 2025–26 they are *sequence*, *optimise*, *unlock*, *recycle* — with the new chief financial officer, Vandita Pant, introducing the "capital productivity" and "unlock capital" framing after her March-2024 appointment. That is defensive substance delivered in an offensive tone.

## 4. Guidance credibility

**Split, and the split is the point. Score: Medium overall — High on the mine, Low on the build.**

*Operating guidance is excellent.* "We met final production and unit cost guidance at all of our assets" (Aug-2024) is verifiably true, and the pattern held through financial-year 2025 and again in financial-year 2026, where the 16 July 2026 review reported unit costs at the bottom of the range at Escondida, Spence and Copper South Australia, within range at Western Australia Iron Ore, and toward the top of the range at the steelmaking coal business. Management's claim of "hitting our guidance more often than our competitors" is the kind of boast that is usually hollow and here is not.

*Project guidance is poor.* Jansen Stage 1 has been reset three times and Stage 2 twice, the latest reset carrying a $2.3 billion write-down and a five-year wait to first cash. A company that has told investors for two years it "executes projects more closely to plan than the industry average" has now missed on the single largest project in its pipeline, repeatedly.

*Reported earnings have undershot the street more often than not.* Statutory earnings per New York share missed consensus in the December-2024 half ($1.74 against $1.98 expected) and again in the December-2025 half ($2.24 against $2.41), and matched or narrowly beat in the June halves. There is no sandbagging pattern; if anything the reverse — the gaps come from impairments the market had not modelled.

## 5. Analyst pressure points

Three recurring questions have never received a straight answer.

**Capital intensity.** Paul Young of Goldman Sachs pressed in August 2024 on how BHP judged the Anglo American assets against building its own copper. The answer — "it's hard to adopt a rule of thumb around these things" — is a non-answer, and the same evasiveness now applies to the Escondida new concentrator, for which no capital number has been published despite a final investment decision flagged for 2027 or 2028.

**The steelmaking coal business.** Rob Stein of Macquarie asked in August 2024 whether the business should shrink toward value rather than volume. Henry redirected to productivity and Indian steel demand. Two years on, coal remains a small and volatile earner without a stated strategic destination.

**Jurisdiction and cost inflation.** Lachlan Shaw of UBS asked in August 2024 about Australian versus offshore project returns; the answer was largely a policy complaint about industrial relations and Queensland royalties. That complaint has now become an operating fact: Port Hedland has been under rolling industrial action since roughly March 2026, with a two-day strike set for 8–9 August 2026.

**What analysts were not warned about:** the scale of the financial-year 2027 copper decline. In February 2026 investors were shown a slide promising roughly 5% a year of copper growth. Five months later group copper was guided down 8% to 15%. Grade decline at a 30-year-old orebody is not a surprise to the geologists; it was a surprise to the presentation.

## 6. Red flags and green flags

🔴 **Jansen is a serial cost problem, not a one-off.** Stage 1 restated to $8.4 billion (Jan-2026); Stage 2 to $6.9 billion with a $2.3 billion write-down and first production late financial-year 2031 (18 Jun-2026).

🔴 **The flagship copper asset is entering a grade trough.** Financial-year 2027 group copper guided to 1.65–1.80 million tonnes, down 8–15%, "driven primarily by lower forecast grades at Escondida" (16 Jul-2026).

🔴 **Advertised cost leadership in copper is partly a by-product price artifact** (management's own attribution, Feb-2026), against gold at $4,157 an ounce and silver at $60.17 an ounce today.

🔴 **Value is increasingly being "unlocked" by selling future revenue.** The Antamina silver stream ($4.3 billion) hands over a future revenue line for cash today; the Western Australia Iron Ore power deal ($2 billion) takes cash now against a 25-year tariff obligation. Both are legitimate and well-timed. Neither is operating performance, and both flatter the current-year balance sheet at the expense of later years.

🔴 **The net-debt ceiling was raised, not the debt reduced** — the target range moved from $5–15 billion to $10–20 billion in August 2025, framed as a reflection of a stronger business.

🔴 **Live industrial action at the single most important export node**, with a two-day stoppage scheduled for 8–9 August 2026.

🟢 **The iron ore cost lead is real and widening** — six consecutive years as lowest-cost major producer, costs cut in real terms since the pandemic ("the only major Pilbara producer to do so"), record 265 million tonnes in financial-year 2026.

🟢 **The balance sheet is genuinely strong** — net debt around $9 billion at 30 June 2026 versus $14.7 billion six months earlier, roughly 0.7 times EBITDA, now below the company's own target range.

🟢 **The copper mix shift actually happened.** Copper went from 39% of group EBITDA (Dec-2024 half) to just over half (Dec-2025 half). Management promised this and delivered it.

🟢 **Loss-making nickel was suspended rather than subsidised** (Aug-2024) — the discipline the sector usually lacks.

🟢 **Samarco was settled** on a defined 20-year schedule (Oct-2024), converting an open-ended legal risk into a scheduled cash outflow.

🟢 **Safety improved materially** — no fatalities in financial-year 2025 or the December-2025 half, after two in the preceding eighteen months.

## 7. Hidden signals

**The most important one is the change of subject.** Compare the February 2024 call, where the news was a sanctioned potash expansion and a study to grow iron ore to 330 million tonnes, with February 2026, where the headline items were a silver stream, a power tariff sale, and "up to $10 billion" of further capital to be unlocked. When a miner's freshest value creation is balance-sheet engineering, the project pipeline is not delivering what was promised for it.

**Softening commitment language on Vicuña.** A project described as potentially "a global top 5 copper and top 5 gold producing asset" carries only "we *could* make a final investment decision on Stage 1 as early as the end of this calendar year" (Feb-2026). That is a hedge, and it sits alongside a still-unpublished capital number for the Escondida concentrator.

**A new chief executive changes what the next results can contain.** Brandon Craig took over on 1 July 2026, having already split the Americas role in two. A new chief executive's first full-year result — 17–18 August 2026 — is the conventional window for resetting the capital plan, re-basing project budgets, or clearing the decks with a write-down. That cuts both ways: it is also the window for a larger-than-expected capital return, with net debt sitting below the target range.

**A quiet portfolio clean-up is running underneath the headlines** — the San Manuel property in Arizona sold to Faraday Copper (definitive agreement 2 July 2026, shareholder vote 25 August 2026), and reported preparations to sell Chilean power transmission lines for roughly $1.5 billion (Reuters citing Diario Financiero, 17 June 2026). These are sensible and small; collectively they confirm that the balance sheet, not the drill bit, is where value is currently being created.

## 8. Capital-allocation verdict

**Trajectory: stable.**

BHP remains one of the two or three best-run mining businesses on earth. Its returns on capital employed — 21% in financial-year 2025, 24% in the December-2025 half — are roughly double what Vale and Freeport-McMoRan earn, and its iron ore cost position is verifiably the best in the industry and still improving. Nothing in the last two years challenges that.

**The moat is a scale-and-cost moat, and it is wide.** The mechanism is physical, not contractual: 30 billion tonnes of Pilbara iron ore with 95% of it within 50 kilometres of existing rail and port, and the world's largest copper resource. Nobody can replicate either. On the width test, the evidence points to stable-to-widening rather than eroding — returns on capital employed rose from 20% (Dec-2024 half) to 24% (Dec-2025 half) and margins from 51% to 58% over the same span. But be honest about why: that improvement is overwhelmingly price-driven, and management said so — "the 25% EBITDA growth… enabled us to fully capture the benefit of higher copper, gold and iron ore prices". Strip the price move out and the underlying trend is flat, with a genuine physical headwind at Escondida as grades decline.

**The secular threat is manageable, not terminal.** Copper and potash are structural winners of electrification and food demand; iron ore faces a real long-run headwind as Chinese steel plateaus and the Simandou project in Guinea adds new low-cost supply, but BHP is the lowest-cost survivor of exactly that squeeze; steelmaking and energy coal are a shrinking single-digit share of earnings. There is no artificial-intelligence displacement, no disintermediation, no channel shift here. The terminal value is cyclical, not melting.

**The single most important thing to weigh before sizing:** the quality is not in question — the *entry price* is. BHP's current earnings are being set by copper at $6.65 a pound (the 52-week high, against a $4.36 low) and gold at $4,157 an ounce, while its own copper volumes are guided down 8–15% next year. The share price has risen about 71% from its 52-week low and sits within 7% of its high. The deterministic cash-flow torque in the input bundle says a 10% move in the commodity deck swings EBITDA by about 17.5% — and that arithmetic runs in both directions. Any position taken here is a bet on the copper price staying at a record while the company's own copper output falls. That is a different bet from owning a great miner cheaply, and it should be sized as such.
