# Forensic dossier — Rio Tinto Group (RIO)

**Prepared 4 August 2026. Price used throughout: $99.08 on the New York listing (the American depositary share, which represents one Rio Tinto plc ordinary share), up 3.3% on the day. The London line closed at 7,313 pence. Every figure below is in US dollars unless stated.**

Transcripts reviewed: 30 July 2024 (2024 half-year), 19 February 2025 (2024 full year), 19 February 2026 (2025 full year), 28 July 2026 (2026 half-year).

---

## 1. Narrative arc

Across four calls the story does not merely evolve — it changes owner and changes register.

**2024 (Jakob Stausholm, chief executive).** The framing is abstract and values-led: four objectives ("best operator, impeccable ESG, excel in development, social licence"). Growth is asserted rather than counted — "we are profitable, and we are growing… this is stable, reliable growth." The hard numbers are modest: half-year underlying earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation (EBITDA — a rough measure of cash operating profit) of $12.1B, up 3%; free cash flow of $2.8B; net debt of $5.1B. The single concrete event is the July 2024 sanctioning of Simandou, the Guinean iron ore project, described as "the largest greenfield mining and infrastructure project in the world."

**Early 2025.** The word that arrives is *diversification*: "the diversification towards copper, aluminium and lithium is happening at pace." Full-year EBITDA of $23.3B was down 2% on an 11% lower iron ore price, and management leans hard on the offset from copper and aluminium. Capital spending steps up to $9.5B and net debt drifts to $5.5B. The Arcadium lithium acquisition is folded in as evidence the portfolio is being reshaped.

**February 2026 — the handover.** Simon Trott presents his first full-year result and the vocabulary is replaced wholesale. Out go the four objectives; in comes "stronger, sharper and simpler," a "management operating system," and a running productivity tally. Full-year EBITDA rises 9% to $25.4B on 8% copper-equivalent production growth and a 5% cut in copper-equivalent unit costs. Two genuinely new commitments appear: $5B to $10B of cash proceeds from selling non-core assets, and 85% of the exploration budget redirected to copper. He also discloses, unprompted, that Rio held merger discussions with Glencore and walked away: "strategic rationales don't pay the grocery bills."

**July 2026 — the evidence arrives.** Half-year EBITDA of $14.8B, up 28%; free cash flow of $3.8B, up 75%; an interim dividend of $3.4B (211.0 US cents per share), up 43%. Copper, aluminium and lithium together supply more than half of group EBITDA for the first time. The productivity programme, promised at $650M, has banked $870M by June, and the year-end target is nearly tripled to $1.8B.

**The quiet drop.** Iron ore growth has left the story. In July 2024 management pointed to "medium-term guidance of 345 to 360" million tonnes from the Pilbara, backed by four replacement mines. The 2026 guidance is 323–338 million tonnes from the Pilbara, with the group total of 343–366 million tonnes made up by Simandou and the Canadian operation. The Pilbara growth promise was not withdrawn loudly; it was diluted into a group number. Simandou is now doing work that four Australian replacement mines were once meant to do.

## 2. Claims versus the financials

An important correction first, because it governs everything below. **The deterministic metrics bundle supplied with this name is wrong by exactly a factor of two, and it is wrong in the flattering direction.** Rio reports twice a year, not quarterly, so the automated "trailing twelve months" calculation summed four reporting periods — which is twenty-four months of trading, not twelve.

| Bundle field | Bundle value | Verified true trailing twelve months | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $115.63B | **$61.67B** | $30.65B (H2 2025) + $31.02B (H1 2026) |
| EBITDA | $41.85B | **$23.35B** statutory / ~$28.6B underlying | FMP statements + company releases |
| Free cash flow | $10.97B | **$5.76B** | $2.53B + $3.23B |
| Diluted earnings per share | $13.67 | **$7.37** | $3.31 + $4.06 |

The four half-year figures sum to the bundle values to the decimal, so this is arithmetic, not estimation. The practical consequence: at $99.08 the shares trade on roughly **13.4 times trailing earnings and a 3.6% free-cash-flow yield**, not the 7.2 times and 6.8% the bundle implies. Anyone sizing this position off the raw bundle is looking at a company half as expensive as the real one.

With that fixed, management's claims test well.

A large slice of that cost improvement is a by-product credit — a gold-price artifact wearing a cost-performance costume. Brent is back to about $79 as of 4 August 2026, so this headwind is now reversing. |

Recomputed from the balance sheet the figure is $13.35B including leases; the difference is definitional and immaterial. Single-A credit rating maintained. |

**Where the story is richer than the numbers:** the copper cost position, for the gold reason above; and the framing of Simandou as pure growth when a meaningful part of it substitutes for Pilbara tonnes that did not arrive.

**Where the numbers are richer than the story:** margins. First-half gross margin widened to 28.0% from 25.0% on revenue up 14.5%, and the underlying EBITDA margin widened to about 47.8% from 42.9%. Expanding margins on rising revenue is the healthiest combination a resource business can print, and management under-sells it.

## 3. Tone and confidence trajectory

**Rising, with a clear discontinuity at the chief-executive change.**

- *July 2024 (Stausholm):* confident but qualitative. "This is stable, reliable growth." Peter Cunningham, the chief financial officer, is candid where it matters — "Kennecott remains the biggest challenge but also a real opportunity."
- *February 2025 (Stausholm):* defensive framing creeps in. "Our financial results are not the product of a global economy firing on all cylinders." Cost control is presented as a recovery from inflation rather than an offensive lever: "we really feel we have turned the corner."
- *February 2026 (Trott, first result):* an offensive turn, and specificity replaces adjectives. "Rio was entering a new era." Costs are now a programme with a number attached. The Glencore disclosure is volunteered rather than extracted.
- *July 2026 (Trott):* the most confident of the four, and confident in a checkable way. "I told you I'd deliver $650 million in productivity benefits, and we've delivered. We've already banked $870 million." When Glyn Lawcock of Barrenjoey pushes for a multi-year cost-out number, Trott declines cleanly and says why: "the program will mature… inevitably, you start with some of the decisions in front of you." That is a more credible answer than a number would have been.

Safety language moves the other way, and honestly so. Trott opens the July 2026 call with two colleague deaths in the half and says, "Nothing we report today means anything if our people do not go home safely." He opened February 2026 with a fatality at Simandou and announced an independent investigation and an external safety panel. This is a company building the largest greenfield project in its industry and paying for it in lives; the disclosure is not being managed away.

## 4. Guidance credibility

**Score: High.**

The record over the reviewed window:

- **Productivity.** Promised $650M by the end of Q1 2026; banked $870M by 30 June 2026; raised the year-end run-rate target to $1.8B. Clear over-delivery, and the individual initiatives are itemised ($80M at Oyu Tolgoi from redesigned underground development, roughly $55M in the Pilbara, roughly $40M across the Atlantic aluminium operations).
- **Dividend.** The stated policy is 40–60% of underlying earnings; the company has paid the top of the range for ten consecutive years. When Alan Spence of BNP Paribas pressed on why the 40% floor exists at all, Cunningham refused to formalise a higher floor — "All I'd say is our policy." That is a deliberate reservation of flexibility, not evasion, and it is the correct answer for a business entering a heavy capital phase.
- **Production.** Copper-equivalent growth of 1% (2024), 8% (2025) and 3% (first half 2026) against an ambition of about 3% compound to 2030 — delivered, with 2025 well ahead.
- **Capital spending.** This is the one place discipline has been tested. Half-year capital expenditure of $4.0B in 2024 became $9.5B for full-year 2024 and is now guided at up to $11B in both 2026 and 2027, easing to $10B in real terms from 2028, with sustaining capital about $4B. That is close to a doubling from the early-2020s base. It is being spent on Tier-1 assets rather than on marginal ounces, and the profile is guided down rather than up — but it is the number to watch, because overspending at the top of a cycle is the sector's defining failure mode.
- **Sandbagging.** No evidence of it. If anything the productivity target was set low and beaten, which is a mild and benign form of the same instinct.

## 5. Analyst pressure points

Four themes recur, and management's handling of each is informative.

1. **The dual-listed structure.** Asked repeatedly (July 2024, February 2025) whether the London and Australian listings should be unified, Stausholm gave the same answer twice: about a $5B tax cost, and "you have to sit yourself and think how on Earth can you do this and never get your Australian shareholders to pay a premium." Myles Allsop of UBS pushed for the build-up of the $5B and was refused on the grounds that disclosing tax exposure is "not in the interest of the shareholders." An activist holder, Palliser, forced a shareholder resolution on it. The structure persists, unresolved, and it is a plausible standing reason the shares trade below a sum-of-the-parts value.

2. **The Pilbara replacement mines and heritage approvals.** Lachlan Shaw of UBS and Kaan Peker of RBC both pressed in July 2024 on depletion (about 90 million tonnes to 2028 against about 120 million tonnes of new capacity) and on Greater Nammuldi. Mark Davies conceded plainly: "We're just flagging at this stage it's a risk to Greater Nammuldi." Two years later Pilbara shipment guidance sits below the 345–360 million tonne medium-term band. **The analysts were right and the risk converted.**

3. **Kennecott, the Utah copper operation.** It has been the named problem child on every call: "the biggest challenge" (July 2024), a smelter shutdown and conveyor breakdown (2023), a fatality in early 2026, and a furnace breach in late June 2026 that pushes metal sales into 2027. In July 2026 Rahul Anand of Morgan Stanley asked directly how Rio would de-risk an asset it now expects to grow production 40–50% and to underpin copper beyond 2030. Trott's answer led with safety and optionality rather than with an engineering plan. **This is the pressure point where management is least specific and the stakes are rising.**

4. **Mongolia.** Kate McCutcheon of Bank of America put the tax dispute at roughly $900M in July 2026 and said, fairly, "optically, that's not a great place to be." Cunningham confirmed formal arbitration. Oyu Tolgoi is the single asset carrying the copper growth thesis to 500,000 tonnes a year between 2028 and 2036, and its host government is in arbitration with its operator.

**What analysts are not asking about, and should be:** how much of the copper cost improvement survives a lower gold price, and what Simandou's 60 million tonnes a year does to the iron ore price Rio receives on its other 330 million tonnes.

## 6. Red flags and green flags

**Concerning**

- Safety has deteriorated during the largest build in company history: a fatality at Simandou in February 2026, two colleague deaths in the first half of 2026. Management's response — stopping site works, an independent investigation, an external advisory panel — is the right one, but the trend is the wrong direction (Feb 2026, Jul 2026).
- The copper unit-cost cut on 15 July 2026 rests substantially on record gold by-product credits. Cost guidance that moves with a metal price is not a cost position.
- Kennecott's operating record is poor and its assigned growth is large (every call reviewed).
- The Mongolian tax dispute, roughly $900M, is in formal arbitration against the operator of the crown-jewel copper ramp (Jul 2026).
- Capital spending has roughly doubled from the early-2020s base to up to $11B a year, during a period when commodity prices are historically high. This is precisely the pattern that has destroyed capital in this sector before.
- The dual-listed structure remains unresolved after activist pressure, and management has ruled out unification on cost grounds without showing the arithmetic (Jul 2024, Feb 2025).
- Pilbara growth guidance quietly stepped down while the group number was maintained with new supply from Guinea (Jul 2024 versus 2026 guidance).

**Positive**

- Productivity over-delivered and the target nearly tripled, with named initiatives rather than a top-down budget cut. Trott: "This is not a top-down exercise where we simply squeeze budgets" (Jul 2026).
- Rio walked away from a merger with Glencore on valuation grounds and said so publicly: "we concluded that we could not reach an agreement that would deliver value for Rio Tinto shareholders" (Feb 2026). Declining a transformative deal is the rarest form of capital discipline.
- The Arcadium lithium acquisition was made near the bottom of the lithium cycle; lithium EBITDA has since quadrupled from a small base and realised prices rose to $18,960 a tonne of lithium carbonate equivalent from $15,580.
- Explicit refusal to be a forced seller of the titanium dioxide and borates businesses: "if we don't get the sort of value that we see in the business, we won't progress them" (Feb 2026).
- The balance sheet absorbed the Arcadium purchase and still sits at about half a year's EBITDA of net debt, with net debt falling in the first half despite $5B of capital spending and $4.2B of dividends paid.
- Diversification is now arithmetic rather than aspiration: more than half of first-half EBITDA came from copper, aluminium and lithium.

## 7. Hidden signals

- **The vocabulary swap is the story.** Stausholm's register was moral and institutional — social licence, impeccable environmental and social standards, four objectives. Trott's is industrial and countable — "banked," "run rate," "management operating system," "$870 million." Investors should read the change as a shift in what the board is rewarding.
- **"85% of exploration budget towards copper"** (February 2026) is the most consequential sentence in four calls. Management is telling you, with money, that it does not believe the next decade of value creation is in iron ore.
- **Iron ore's adjective changed.** In 2024 it was the engine. By February 2026 it is "resilient" and "structurally balanced," defended by a steep cost curve and "over 100 price-sensitive producers from more than 20 countries." That is the language of a business being defended, not grown.
- **Cunningham's dividend answer.** Pressed twice to lift the payout floor, he would not. Read alongside up-to-$11B annual capital spending, the message is that the growth programme has first claim on cash and the dividend will grow with earnings, not with the payout ratio.
- **The marketing hint.** Post-Glencore, Trott twice returned to "that marketing front end" and "ways we can generate greater value around those flows." A trading and commercial margin initiative appears to be forming that has not yet been quantified.
- **Simandou's schedule language tightened but its ramp shape never did.** Asked in February 2025 for the ramp profile, Stausholm said, "I'm not able to offer you anything better than a straight line at this point in time without promising you that it is a straight line." Eighteen months later the project is more than three-quarters complete with first high-grade sales in April 2026 and completion targeted for end-2027, but the ramp shape is still unquantified.

## 8. Capital-allocation verdict

**Classification: STRENGTHENING.**

The trajectory is genuinely improving on evidence rather than on narrative. A new chief executive set a measurable cost target, beat it, and raised it; the portfolio has diversified away from iron ore in fact and not merely in intention; margins are widening on rising revenue; the balance sheet absorbed a $6.7B acquisition and is still at about half a year's earnings of net debt; and management walked away from a transformative merger on price.

**The single most important thing to weigh before sizing.** Not the operations — the price of the things it digs up. Copper is at roughly $6.65 a pound, aluminium realisations at $4,343 a tonne, and gold at roughly $4,152 an ounce, all at or near historic extremes; iron ore at about $93 a tonne is merely normal. The internal torque measure says a 10% move in the commodity basket swings EBITDA by roughly 25%, and a bottom-up check of Rio's own volumes puts it near 20% — either way the swing on a 10% move is roughly $5.5B of EBITDA, and **that arithmetic works identically in both directions**. The shares have risen about 66% from their 52-week low of $59.49 and sit within 12% of their 52-week high. You are being asked to buy a first-quartile cost position at a moment when the prices it earns are not first-quartile normal. Size to the commodity risk, not to the quality of the assets.

**Economic moat: WIDE.** The moat type is scale-cost, and it is real rather than argued. Pilbara unit costs of $23.5–25.0 a tonne sit against a seaborne price of about $93 and a cost curve management describes as supported "at the top end by over 100 price-sensitive producers from more than 20 countries." Sixty years of integrated rail, port and power infrastructure across eight billion tonnes shipped cannot be replicated by a competitor with capital alone; the orebodies and the corridors are not for sale. Copper net unit costs of 30–50 cents a pound are first-quartile even after discounting the gold credit. Returns support the width and are improving, not fading: return on capital employed of about 14% and return on equity of about 19% on the trailing twelve months, with EBITDA margins widening about 490 basis points year on year. That is a widening picture on margins and a stable one on returns, so the trend is best read as **STABLE** — the cost-out is real, but heavier capital spending is temporarily diluting returns while Simandou and Oyu Tolgoi are built and not yet earning.

**Secular threat: material, not terminal.** The named structural force is the erosion of Chinese steel demand — down as much as 30% from its 2020 peak on management's own repeated telling — compounded by Rio adding 60 million tonnes a year of new Guinean supply into that same market. Roughly 46% of first-half EBITDA still comes from iron ore, so this is not a small exposure. But it is a shrinking-market problem rather than a disappearing-market one, and Rio is positioned at the bottom of the cost curve, which is where survivors sit. Set against it, the other half of the business — copper, aluminium and lithium — is levered to electrification, grid build-out and data-centre power demand, which are structural tailwinds rather than headwinds. The cheapness on offer is therefore **not** a value trap; the risk here is cyclical mispricing at the top of a price cycle, not terminal erosion of the earnings base.
