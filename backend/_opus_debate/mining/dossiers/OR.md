# OR Royalties Inc. (NYSE/TSX: OR) — Forensic Dossier

*Prepared 4 August 2026. Price used throughout: US$31.19 on the New York listing (the company reports in US dollars and the New York and Toronto listings share the ticker "OR"; all figures below are US dollars unless a Canadian-dollar amount is explicitly marked).*

OR Royalties is not a miner. It owns royalty and streaming contracts — legal claims that entitle it to a fixed slice of the gold and silver that other companies dig out of the ground, without paying any of the mining costs. It has 23 to 24 paying assets, its single biggest being a 3% to 5% net smelter return royalty (a percentage of the revenue a mine earns from selling its metal) on Agnico Eagle's Canadian Malartic complex in Quebec.

---

## 1. Narrative arc

Across the six calls in evidence — February 2025 through February 2026 — the story moved through three distinct chapters, and the company was honest about each transition as it happened.

**Chapter one, "fix the balance sheet" (February 2025 to August 2025).** The opening call was a deleveraging story. Management had paid down almost $85 million of a revolving credit facility during 2024 and finished the year with $59 million of cash against just under $35 million of net debt. By the first quarter of 2025 net debt was "just over $10 million"; by the second quarter the company was in net cash "for the first time in several years"; by the third quarter it was, in the chief executive's words, "in a debt-free position for the first time in over 10 years." Three consecutive calls each delivered exactly the next step management had promised on the prior one. That is the single most credible sequence in the file.

**Chapter two, "discipline as a virtue" (August 2025 to February 2026).** As precious-metal prices ran, the emphasis shifted from repairing the balance sheet to refusing to spend it. Jason Attew, the chief executive, told the November 2025 call he continued "to espouse an internal culture of capital allocation discipline, where returns on new transactions must exceed our internal hurdle rates at what we believe internally to be more realistic commodity pricing scenarios." The February 2026 call turned that into a boast: the company transacted on "only just $25 million in royalty and stream acquisitions" in 2025, against more than $9.3 billion of deals across the sector. The phrase used was "we prioritize value over volume," and the justification offered was structural: "Because our near-term growth is already secured and fully funded, we possess a strategic advantage many peers lack, the luxury of walking away from bad deals."

**Chapter three, "spend it all" (January 2026 onward).** This chapter is not in the transcripts — it is in the press releases, and it inverts chapter two. In a single quarter the company committed $438.5 million: an additional 1.0% royalty on the Namdini mine in Ghana for up to $103.5 million (29 January), a portfolio of eight royalties bought from Gold Fields for $115 million, anchored by a 1.5% royalty on Buenaventura's San Gabriel mine in Peru (18 February), and the Terraco Gold subsidiary holding royalties over the Spring Valley project in Nevada for $168 million cash (24 February). A fourth commitment followed on 14 April 2026: up to $96 million of project financing into Canadian Copper Inc., a Canadian Securities Exchange-listed developer of a base-metals complex in New Brunswick.

The growth drivers quietly dropped along the way are worth naming. Diamonds — the Renard mine — disappeared entirely between 2024 and 2025. Direct equity ownership of Osisko Development, the affiliated developer, was deliberately diluted from close to 50% down to 14.3% over 2024 and 2025, which management framed as a positive ("we were no longer going to be funding the Osisko Development, Cariboo through equity placements"). The growth drivers added are copper — first through the CSA mine stream, now through Canadian Copper — from a management that has said repeatedly and in plain terms, "we are a precious metal vehicle."

The most important divergence is the last one. Everything else in the file is a company describing itself accurately. The 2 July statement is a company describing away a number its own operating partner had just published.

A second, quieter divergence: the trailing-twelve-month earnings figure in the screener bundle carries an operating margin above 100%, which is arithmetically impossible for an operating business. It happens because reported earnings include non-cash gains — most visibly a $54 million mark-up on the Osisko Development stake in the third quarter of 2025, triggered by an accounting reclassification when OR's influence over that company fell away. Stripping those out, the honest cash figure is the 96.8% cash margin management quotes, and the trailing enterprise value to cash earnings multiple moves from roughly 16 times to roughly 20 times. Anyone screening on the reported figure is getting a materially cheaper-looking company than the one that exists.

## 3. Tone and confidence trajectory

**Stable, with a distinct hardening into 2026.**

*February 2025 — cautiously optimistic, with a hedge.* "Not only does our long-term growth trajectory still spell great things coming for Osisko five years out… However, the slope of that growth is likely less steep than we previously anticipated." That volunteered "however" is a good sign; it is a management team telling you the bad half before an analyst asks.

*May 2025 — confident, promotional at the margins.* The name change to OR Royalties was announced with a flourish: "Commodity prices are elevated, our assets are performing well, the balance sheet is strong, and we're just getting started." No analyst asked a single question on that call — a fact worth noting in itself.

*August 2025 — specific and measured.* Management quantified its own shortfall unprompted: "approximately 1,200 GEOs that were not realized compared to our internal budget due to the higher gold/silver ratio." Volunteering a miss of that size, in that detail, is the behaviour of a team that expects to be checked.

*November 2025 — defensive in the good sense.* The language turned toward what the company would not do: "internal red lines regarding structural security and contract terms," "we have the fortunate luxury to be able to walk away." Attew gave Tanya Jakusconek of Scotiabank an unusually concrete answer on the cost of capital — "approximately, it's about 4.5% in terms of our cost of capital or cost of debt" — and named a real precedent, the mid-teens internal rate of return on the Cascabel transaction.

*February 2026 — the most promotional call in the file.* "The enviable triple crown of record annual revenues… record operating cash flow… and record earnings." "For us, 2025 prove that boring is good." A "50% growth trajectory through to 2030." This was delivered two days before the shares set a 12-month high on the Toronto listing at C$65.31.

The shift is not from offence to defence. It is from a repair story told plainly to a growth story told with more adjectives, arriving exactly at the top of the price cycle.

## 4. Guidance credibility

**High, with one live exception.**

The delivery record is genuinely good and covers three separate promise types.

*Volume guidance.* 2024: 80,740 ounces against 77,000–83,000, slightly above the midpoint of a range that had itself been revised down during the year. 2025: 80,775 against 80,000–88,000 — inside, at the bottom on the raw number, mid-range once adjusted for the gold-to-silver ratio actually realised versus the 83-to-1 assumed. Management flagged that ratio drag twice before the year ended, in August and again in November, rather than explaining it after the fact. That is the opposite of sandbagging: they told you the number would look soft and why, and it did.

*Balance-sheet guidance.* Every step promised was delivered on the call it was promised for. See section 2.

*Dividend guidance.* In February 2025 management said that come May "there should be a recommendation by this management team to our Board to increase our dividend." The board approved a 20% increase in May 2025. In February 2026 management pre-announced the same intention for the first quarter results; an 18.2% increase to $0.065 per share followed on 6 May 2026. Forty-six consecutive quarterly dividends, and inclusion in the Canadian dividend-aristocrat index in January 2026.

*The exception.* The 2030 outlook published in February 2026 — 120,000 to 135,000 ounces — landed below what analysts expected. Derick Ma of TD said so directly: "Guidance came in below expectations and at least certainly below my estimates." That is the first guidance event in the file that disappointed, and it came after a year of "50% growth" messaging. Reaffirming that same outlook as "unchanged" on 2 July, hours after the operator of the cornerstone asset published a two-year production hole, is the one place where the credibility record is exposed.

**Reliability score: High**, downgraded from the top mark solely by the 2 July reaffirmation.

## 5. Analyst pressure points

Analysts pushed on three things, repeatedly, across every call.

**Deal size, and the refusal to narrow it.** Tanya Jakusconek of Scotiabank asked the same question four times across four calls and got a wider answer each time. February 2025: "$50 million and $500 million." August 2025: "somewhere around USD 35 million all the way up to close to the USD $1 billion." November 2025: her own words were "you can drive a truck through that," and the answer came back to "$50 million and $500 million." February 2026: "$100 million to $200 million, but we're also seeing opportunities from $750 million all the way up to $1 billion." A range that will not converge over a full year is a range that is not a plan. It is also the range within which they subsequently spent $438.5 million in a single quarter.

**The hurdle rate.** Jakusconek pressed in November 2025 for the actual internal rate of return required. The answer began with a deflection — "maybe we can take this conversation offline" — before landing on a genuine number (a spread over roughly 4.5%, with a mid-teens precedent). Partial credit: he did eventually answer, but the reflex was to move it off the record.

**Mantos Blancos silver grades.** This came up on five of six calls and never resolved. February 2025: throughput fixed, "Capstone has officially turned the corner." May 2025: "lower silver grades at the front of the year," grades expected to "trend back upwards." August 2025: grades still low, and the Phase 2 feasibility study slipped from the fourth quarter of 2025 into 2026. November 2025: grades improved, but for "approximately a month's worth." February 2026: "we've continued to see some quarterly variability of the processed silver grades," and 2026 was guided flat. Brian MacArthur of Raymond James put the unspoken part on the record in February 2026: "just given the reconciliation that we've been seeing or not seeing." Reconciliation is the comparison between the grade a geological model predicts and the grade the mill actually recovers. When it persistently disappoints, the model is wrong. Management has guided this asset flat to 2030 rather than fix the estimate — which is prudent, but also means a stream carrying a large share of revenue is one the operator cannot yet predict.

**What nobody asked about that mattered.** No analyst in the file pressed on wall stability or geotechnical risk at Barnat, the pit that produces the ounces underpinning the largest royalty. That risk was not hidden — Agnico later disclosed the failed area "had been previously identified as having weaker geological structures within the north wall" and was under enhanced monitoring — but it was never a topic on an OR call.

## 6. Red flags and green flags

**Red flags**

- *The discipline whipsaw (January to April 2026).* A company that spent the February 2026 call celebrating $25 million of restraint committed $438.5 million over the following weeks, plus up to $96 million more in April. The deals were struck while gold traded between roughly $4,500 and $5,600 an ounce, the top of its range; gold is $4,154 today, and silver has fallen from a 12-month high of $121 to $60. Buying royalties is buying a claim on a metal price; buying them at the top is the sector's oldest mistake.
- *Net cash to net debt in one quarter (31 March to 30 June 2026).* From $90.6 million of net cash to $139.4 million of net debt, a $230 million reversal. The screener bundle's instruction to treat net cash of $91 million as a valuation add-back is now wrong by a quarter and by a sign.
- *Buying back stock into strength, not weakness.* C$50.8 million repurchased across 2025 and a further C$17.7 million (US$12.9 million) in the first quarter of 2026 — the quarter in which the Toronto shares set a 12-month high of C$65.31 on 27 February. In 2024, by contrast, when the shares were "slightly below $23" after a disappointment, the company bought "a whopping 26,000 shares," which Attew himself conceded "wasn't much." The buyback has been countercyclical in exactly the wrong direction.
- *Drift from the stated mandate.* "We are a precious metal vehicle. We'll continue to be a precious metal vehicle" (February 2025). In April 2026 the company committed up to $96 million to a base-metals developer listed on the Canadian Securities Exchange.
- *"Outlook unchanged" on 2 July 2026.* Discussed above. It is one sentence, but it is the sentence a director should test first.

**Green flags**

- *The cash margin is real and it is the best in its peer group.* 96.7% for 2025, 96.8% in the second quarter of 2026, and it has not moved in either direction across the file. This is what a royalty is supposed to look like and many do not.
- *Every balance-sheet promise was kept, in order, on time.* Three consecutive calls, three delivered steps to debt-free.
- *Jurisdiction quality is genuinely differentiated.* Just under 75% of 2025 ounces came from Canada, the United States and Australia, closer to 90% including Chile. In a sector where the cheapest assets are cheap for reasons that show up as expropriation risk, this is a durable advantage, and it survived the 2026 buying spree (San Gabriel adds Peru; Spring Valley adds Nevada).
- *Management volunteers its own shortfalls.* The 1,200-ounce ratio miss in August 2025 and the "slope of that growth is likely less steep" hedge in February 2025 were both unprompted.
- *Growth is bought and paid for.* Whatever one thinks of the price, Namdini, San Gabriel, Dalgaranga and Spring Valley are owned. The path from 80,000–90,000 ounces this year to 120,000–135,000 in 2030 does not require another cheque.

## 7. Hidden signals

**"Producing" quietly became "producing and/or paying."** In February 2025 management drew the distinction itself: 21 producing assets, or 20 "if Namdini is excluded," because a mine can pour gold months before a royalty holder sees a payment. Namdini poured first gold in November 2024 and OR's first payment — 130 ounces — arrived in the second quarter of 2025. The asset-count metric is therefore softer than it sounds, and it has since climbed to 23 and then 24 partly by counting assets in ramp-up. San Gabriel and Dalgaranga are both described as "ramping up this year and the next and growing into a material contributor from 2028 onwards." The count is up; the ounces from those additions are two years out.

**The equity portfolio is a shrinking, unhedged asset.** Long-term investments stood at $210.5 million at 31 March 2026. The largest identified piece is roughly 14.3% of Osisko Development, whose shares trade at $2.28 today against a 12-month high of $4.795 — down more than half. Whatever that line was worth at the March balance-sheet date, it is worth materially less now, and none of it produces cash.

**Elliott Management appeared and then went quiet.** Asked in August 2025, Attew confirmed "the last public disclosure that we see is Elliott owns 2.2 million OR Royalties shares" — roughly 1.2% of the company — and no further filing has surfaced. Two board seats changed hands in early 2026 (Kevin Thomson appointed and William Murray John resigned on 30 January; Patrick Godin appointed on 23 March). That is a board refresh, not visibly a proxy fight, and nothing in the file establishes a link.

**The copper option nobody is modelling.** Carey MacRury of Canaccord asked in November 2025 whether the buydown option on the CSA copper stream — the operator's right to buy back part of the stream — transferred when Harmony Gold took over from MAC Copper. It did, unchanged, exercisable from the fifth anniversary. A stream that can be repurchased by the operator is a shorter asset than one that cannot, and its value depends on a decision by a counterparty that has just told the market it will run CSA for the long term rather than push volumes.

## 8. Capital-allocation verdict

**Trajectory: Stable.**

The operating engine is exactly what management says it is. The margin is real, the guidance record is honest, the balance sheet was genuinely repaired, and the growth to 2030 is already owned rather than promised. Nothing in the file suggests a company misrepresenting itself.

What changed is not the business. It is the price paid for the next tranche of it. A management team that spent a full year explaining, persuasively, why it would not chase the market then wrote $438.5 million of cheques in one quarter at the top of the metal cycle, turned net cash into $139.4 million of net debt doing it, and bought back its own shares at a 12-month high while it was at it. None of those is fatal. Together they say that the discipline described in November 2025 was a description of conditions, not of temperament.

**The single most important thing to weigh before sizing.** Not the gold price — the cornerstone. Canadian Malartic is the largest royalty in the book by a wide margin, and its open pit is shut. The operator says remediation finishes in the third quarter of 2026, mining resumes in the fourth, and up to 150,000 ounces a year are lost in both 2027 and 2028 regardless. The company's answer, published the same day, was that nothing changes. A director should price the two-year hole explicitly rather than accept the reassurance, and should treat the underground Odyssey mine — untouched by the incident, and the reason Agnico still expects a million ounces a year from the complex in the early 2030s — as the reason the long-dated value survives.

**Economic moat: wide, and stable.** The moat is contractual. A net smelter return royalty is an encumbrance registered against a mining title; it runs with the property, survives changes of operator (as Éléonore and CSA both demonstrated, passing to Dhilmar and Harmony Gold with no change to OR's terms), and cannot be competed away or inflated away, because OR pays none of the costs. The evidence supports width rather than erosion: return on equity of 19.9% and return on invested capital of 13.9% on the trailing year, both improved on 2024, with the cash margin flat at the top of its peer group. The honest limit on that moat is that it protects the assets already owned and does nothing to protect the price of the next one — royalty companies have no organic growth and must buy it against rivals with lower costs of capital, which is precisely where the 2026 spending sits.

**Secular threat: none.** There is no structural force eroding the terminal value of a claim on ounces in Quebec, Nevada and Western Australia. The reserve lives behind these contracts are long — management describes deploying capital "in assets with 15- to 25-year mine lives," and Odyssey alone underpins Canadian Malartic into the 2040s. The risk here is cyclical, not terminal: the metal price, and the price paid for new contracts. Cheapness in this name, where it exists, would be a cycle print rather than a melting base — but on today's numbers the cheapness is thinner than the screen suggests, because the reported earnings figure that makes it look cheap is inflated by non-cash gains.
