# Forensic Dossier — Alamos Gold Inc. (NYSE/TSX: AGI)

**Prepared 2026-08-11 · Price used: $33.42 USD (NYSE listing) · Market value about $14.0B · Transcripts covered: Q2 2025 (2025-08-01), Q3 2025 (2025-10-30), Q4 2025 (2026-02-19), Q1 2026 (2026-04-30), Q2 2026 (2026-07-30)**

Alamos is a mid-sized gold producer that digs and processes ore at three operating districts — Island Gold and Young-Davidson in Ontario, and Mulatos in Mexico — plus one large mine under construction at Lynn Lake in Manitoba. Every dollar of revenue comes from selling refined gold, so the company has no product mix to hide behind: the only things it controls are how many ounces it pours and what each ounce costs to produce.

---

## 1. NARRATIVE ARC

The story management tells has been remarkably consistent across all five calls, and that consistency is itself the finding. In every single quarter the pitch is the same three-part structure: (a) this quarter had a problem, (b) the problem is temporary and specific, (c) the long-term growth plan is untouched and is "one of the strongest outlooks in the sector."

What changed is only the name of the problem:

| Quarter | The stated problem | The unchanged promise |
|---|---|---|
| Q2 2025 | Share-based compensation, royalty expense, slow start at Magino and Young-Davidson | "significant improvement in both production and costs into the second half" |
| Q3 2025 | Magino mill capacitor failure, seismic event at Island Gold | "in no way take away from the quality of our mines" |
| Q4 2025 | Severe December weather, "other challenges at the Canadian operations" | 46% production increase and 20% lower costs by 2028 |
| Q1 2026 | Costs guided above range, Young-Davidson underperformance | "well on track to meeting our full-year production guidance" |
| Q2 2026 | Two seismic events at Young-Davidson, storm power outage, labour and contractor inflation, slow leaching at La Yaqui Grande | "stronger production and significantly lower costs in 2027" |

The growth narrative has genuinely *expanded* over the period, and this is the part that is real. In August 2025 the Island Gold District plan was 411,000 ounces a year at a mine-site all-in sustaining cost of $915 per ounce over twelve years, based on a 12,400-tonne-per-day mill. By February 2026 that had become 534,000 ounces a year at $1,025 per ounce on a 20,000-tonne-per-day mill, underpinned by a near-doubling of reserves at the district to over 8 million ounces. Company-wide reserves rose 32% to 16 million ounces, the seventh consecutive annual increase. That is a substantial, drill-bit-verified expansion of the asset base, not a slide-deck revision.

What has been quietly dropped is the *timing*. Lynn Lake first production has moved from mid-2028 (stated Q3 2025) to early 2029 (same call, revised) to the first half of 2029 (Q2 2026) — a full year of slippage. The consolidated 900,000-ounce target moved from "towards the latter part of 2028" (Q2 2025) to "by the end of this decade" (Q3 2025) to "by 2028" for 800,000 ounces and "1 million ounces by 2030" (Q1 2026). Each restatement is defensible in isolation. Stacked, they describe a plan sliding to the right at roughly a year per year.

The most telling shift is in what management stopped saying. Through Q2 and Q3 2025 the recurring phrase was production growth at "well below industry average all-in sustaining costs." By Q2 2026 the company's own guidance put its costs at or above the large-cap peer median (see Section 2). The claim did not get retracted — it just stopped being tested against a number.

## 2. CLAIMS vs FINANCIALS (forensic)

### 2.1 The guidance record — the central finding

This is where the narrative and the numbers separate most violently.

| Year | Original production guidance | Revised | Actual / current | Miss vs original |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2025 | ~595,000–620,000 oz | 560,000–580,000 oz (Q3 2025) | **545,000 oz** | **about −10%** |
| 2026 | 570,000–650,000 oz | **510,000–560,000 oz** (Q2 2026) | in progress | **−12% at midpoint** |

| Year | Original cost guidance (all-in sustaining, per ounce) | Revised | Actual / current | Overrun |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2025 | ~$1,300–1,375 | raised 12% in Q2 2025 | **$1,524** | **about +15%** |
| 2026 | $1,500–1,600 | **$1,775–1,875** | in progress | **+18% at midpoint** |

Two consecutive years of double-digit production shortfalls and mid-to-high-teens cost overruns. Set that against what management said on the calls:

> "We've been providing guidance like this for a very long time. We take it very seriously. We put a lot of effort into coming up with these numbers, and we're very confident in our forecast." — John McCluskey, Q2 2025

> "We have a reputation for taking a conservative approach to guiding the market, and we pride ourselves on providing consistently accurate guidance." — John McCluskey, Q3 2025, in the same breath as cutting guidance 6%

The self-description is not supported by the record. This is the single largest gap between story and evidence in the file.

### 2.2 The cost-curve claim — contradicted by the company's own guidance

Management's framing across all five calls is that Alamos is a low-cost producer. Its revised 2026 guidance says otherwise, measured against the peers named in our chain map:

| Company | 2026 all-in sustaining cost guidance (per ounce) |
|---|---|
| Agnico Eagle (AEM) | $1,400–1,550 (midpoint ~$1,475) |
| Kinross (KGC) | ~$1,730 (±5%) |
| **Alamos (AGI)** | **$1,775–1,875 (midpoint ~$1,825)** |
| Barrick (B) | $1,760–1,950 (midpoint ~$1,855) |

Alamos is now roughly $350 per ounce more expensive than Agnico and sits level with Barrick, a company whose cost problems are the sector's standing punchline. On about 535,000 ounces, that $350 gap is roughly $187 million of annual pre-tax cash margin foregone against the best-run peer. "Well below industry average" is no longer an accurate description of this cost base.

Critically, part of this is structural, not weather. The chief financial officer was explicit in Q2 2026 that of the cost increase, about $90 per ounce is labour and contractor inflation, and a further roughly $30 per ounce comes from a Canadian retention programme implemented in June 2026 that "will be payable in future years" and "will continue into 2027." Management is telling you, in its own words, that at least $120 per ounce of the overrun does not reverse.

### 2.3 Revenue growth is a gold-price story, not an operating story

Revenue grew 46.5% year over year to $2.21 billion on a trailing-twelve-month basis. That number flatters the operation badly. Production *fell* from 2024 into 2025 and is guided lower again in 2026. Realized gold prices went from $3,223 per ounce (Q2 2025) to $3,359 (Q3 2025) to $3,998 (Q4 2025) to $4,829 (Q1 2026) to $4,504 (Q2 2026). Essentially all of the revenue growth is the metal, and none of it is the miner.

### 2.4 The margin turn has already begun

Gross margin by quarter, computed from reported statements:

| Quarter | Revenue | Gross margin |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 2025 | $333.0M | 41.4% |
| Q2 2025 | $438.2M | 54.2% |
| Q3 2025 | $457.2M | 56.4% |
| Q4 2025 | $584.1M | 60.1% |
| Q1 2026 | $588.4M | **63.9%** |
| Q2 2026 | $579.0M | **59.2%** |

Margin peaked in the first quarter of 2026 and fell 4.7 percentage points in the second quarter on revenue that was essentially flat (down 1.6%). This is the moment the gold-price tailwind stopped being large enough to cover the cost inflation. It is the most important single number in this dossier, because it dates the inflection.

### 2.5 The cash-flow claim versus the capital budget

Management repeatedly promises free cash flow "in excess of $1 billion" annually once the projects are done. What the company actually generates today, at gold prices near record levels, is $316 million of trailing free cash flow — a 2.25% yield on the market value. The reason is capital intensity: trailing capital spending is $653 million, or 29.6% of revenue, and 2026 guided capital spending is $885–975 million excluding exploration, which is roughly 39% of expected revenue.

Against peers on the same trailing basis:

| Company | Capital spending as % of revenue | Free-cash-flow yield |
|---|---|---|
| Alamos | 29.6% | 2.25% |
| Agnico Eagle | 19.7% | 4.90% |
| Kinross | 16.4% | 9.57% |

Alamos is running the heaviest build in its peer group and converting the least cash. That is a deliberate choice, and it may prove correct — but it means the "$1 billion of free cash flow" is a 2029–2030 promise being funded by a 2026–2028 cash drought, at a moment when the metal price has already turned down.

### 2.6 What the numbers genuinely support

Balance sheet: cash and short-term investments of $676 million against total debt of $216 million including $7 million of capitalised leases, giving **net cash of about $460 million**, or roughly $1.09 per share. Net debt to earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation is −0.24×. Return on equity is 26.1% and return on invested capital 14.8%. The company can fund its build internally with $1.2 billion of stated liquidity. There is no solvency question here, and the bear case must not pretend there is one.

Reserve life: 16 million ounces of reserves at year-end 2025 against roughly 550,000 ounces of annual production is about 29 years of reserve life, or 16–18 years at the future 900,000-to-1-million-ounce rate. That is genuinely long for the sector and it is the strongest fact in the file.

## 3. TONE & CONFIDENCE TRAJECTORY

**Rating: Declining — specifically, declining in the operations and holding in the geology.**

The tone splits cleanly in two, and reading them separately is what makes this company legible.

*On the ore body*, confidence is rising and is backed by evidence. Scott Parsons, the exploration voice added prominently in the Q4 2025 and Q2 2026 calls, speaks with precise, checkable detail: "the highest grade hole ever drilled at Cline-Pick at 178 grams per tonne over 3.5 metres" (Q4 2025), a new zone "200 by 300 metres" that "remains open down plunge and to the west" (Q2 2026), the main structure grown "from less than 2 million ounces of reserves and resources in 2017, to what is now approaching 7 million ounces." This is the language of people who are finding things.

*On the mine plan*, confidence is eroding and the language is getting vaguer. Compare the arc on Young-Davidson's throughput:

- Q2 2025 (Luc Guimond): "as we move into Q4, we don't have any significant scheduled maintenance requirements... would expect to be running at 8,000 tonnes per day." A specific number, near-dated.
- Q3 2025: the mill "has been performing quite well... no issues there," with speculative upside to "probably get it up to probably 9,000 tonnes per day consistently." Note the double hedge, "probably... probably."
- Q2 2026: after the seismic events, "our expectation is to get above 7 thousand tonnes a day moving forward," with the extraction sequence below the 9,410-metre level under active review and clarity promised "by the end of the year."

In twelve months the target went from 8,000 tonnes per day as a delivered fact, to 9,000 as an aspiration, to "above 7,000" as a hope pending a review that has not concluded. Meanwhile the near-term rate is guided at approximately 5,000 tonnes per day for the second half of 2026. That is a 38% reduction against the rate management was confidently describing a year earlier.

The shift from offensive to defensive language is clearest in the Q2 2026 answer on mergers and acquisitions. Asked whether the Young-Davidson disruption had changed his appetite, McCluskey listed the internal workload — "getting our Young Davidson operation back on track... a full blown construction project at Lynn Lake that is basically a $920 million project" — and concluded "it's just not a focus for us right now." A year earlier the same executive was describing an outlook that would be "anchoring one of the strongest outlooks in our sector." The company has moved from selling ambition to managing a backlog of repairs.

## 4. GUIDANCE CREDIBILITY

**Reliability score: Low.**

The mechanism of the misses matters more than the misses themselves, and it is consistent: management guides to the operational plan, not to the plan's historical realisation rate. Every forecast assumes no unplanned downtime, and every year has unplanned downtime.

Tracking the specific forward statements against what followed:

| Promised | Where | Delivered |
|---|---|---|
| "on track to meet our full year production guidance" | Q2 2025 | No — cut 6% in Q3 2025, then missed the cut |
| Fourth-quarter 2025 would be "record production," an "18% increase" | Q3 2025 | No — full year came in at 545,000 oz, below even the revised low end |
| Costs to "decrease a further 5% in the fourth quarter"; "on track to achieve full year cost guidance, which was revised" | Q3 2025 | No — full-year all-in sustaining cost of $1,524 landed above the revised guidance |
| "well on track to meeting our full-year production guidance" | Q1 2026 | No — cut 12% three months later |
| Second-quarter costs to "decrease by approximately 5%" | Q1 2026 | No — full-year cost guidance raised 18% instead |

That is five consecutive forward assurances, five failures. There is no sandbagging pattern here — the opposite. This is a management team that systematically guides to the good case and is then surprised by the ordinary variance of underground mining, which is a business defined by its variance.

One important qualifier in fairness: the *quarterly* numbers, once re-guided, have been hit. Q2 2026 production of 130,600 ounces met the revised range set in June 2026, and Q1 2026 was "in line with quarterly guidance." Management can forecast ninety days out. It cannot forecast twelve months out. For a director sizing a position, that means the annual plan carries very little information, and the multi-year plan — the 800,000 ounces by 2028, the 1 million by 2030 — carries less still.

A further credibility marker sits outside the transcripts. Following the June 18, 2026 operational update disclosing the two Young-Davidson seismic events and the storm-related power outage, the shares fell 11.83% to $32.04 on June 22, 2026, and at least three plaintiff firms — Pomerantz, Schall, and Bronstein Gewirtz & Grossman — opened investigations into whether disclosure was adequate. These are investigations, not filed complaints, and the sector attracts them routinely after any sharp drop; no finding of any kind exists. It should not be treated as evidence of wrongdoing. It should be treated as a fair measure of how far the market's trust in this management's forecasting has fallen.

## 5. ANALYST PRESSURE POINTS

**Young-Davidson seismicity — the question management keeps almost answering.** Don DeMarco of National Bank asked the sharpest question in the entire file (Q2 2026): what is "the frequency and magnitude of seismic events over Young-Davidson's operating life... are the events occurring at a higher frequency as the mine deepens?" McCluskey's answer began "it's a normal part of mining activity," asserted "it's not that we are seeing more events overall or higher events overall," and gave no data — no event count, no magnitude trend, no comparison across depth. The honest reading is that a mine deepening past 9,410 metres of level designation is meeting more stress, that the company knows this (it is adding "longer embedded dynamic support, some cable bolting... as well as the gauge of the mesh"), and that it has not yet quantified what it costs. The $10–15 million a year McCluskey offered for added ground support is a sustaining-capital estimate given without a study behind it, and the extraction-sequence review is still open.

This same topic produced the Q3 2025 seismic event, the Q4 2025 rehabilitation drag, and the June 2026 events. Analysts have pushed on it three times and it has turned into bad news three times.

**Buyback follow-through.** Bank of America pressed on capital return in Q3 2025 ("do you have a target run rate in mind?") and again in Q1 2026 ("strong free cash flow generation in the first quarter, but there were no share buybacks"). Fisher declined to give a target — "we've never put targets in place... we always want to be opportunistic." Management then repeatedly said it "expects to be active" and, in the first quarter of 2026, bought back nothing. It did deliver $50 million of repurchases in Q2 2026, which is the first time the promise and the action lined up. Worth noting: cash was instead spent buying out legacy hedges — $43 million in Q1 2026 and $92.3 million in Q2 2026 to retire 35,000 ounces, which is roughly $2,637 per ounce of cash paid to escape a $1,840 per ounce obligation. That is a defensible bet on gold, but it is an unhedged directional wager funded from the buyback budget, and it was not framed to shareholders that way.

**Capital inflation at Lynn Lake.** DeMarco asked directly in Q3 2025 what the wildfire delay does to capital cost. Fisher's answer — "inflation on capital projects is run around 5% to 6%. So we can expect a 15% increase in our capital that we put out in the feasibility study" — is an unusually candid admission that a headline project number is 15% stale. The project is now described as "$920 million." Analysts have not yet forced a restated, current-dollar capital estimate, and until they do the Lynn Lake number in every valuation model is a floor, not an estimate.

**What analysts are not asking.** Nobody on any of these five calls asked management to reconcile its "well below industry average" cost positioning against its own revised guidance versus peers. That question is available and unasked.

## 6. RED FLAGS / GREEN FLAGS

🔴 **Two straight years of double-digit production misses against original guidance** (2025: −10%; 2026 guided: −12%), delivered by a team that describes itself as conservative forecasters (Q3 2025).

🔴 **Cost inflation that management itself calls structural.** About $120 per ounce of the 2026 overrun — contractor inflation plus a June 2026 Canadian retention programme — is explicitly stated to persist into 2027 (Q2 2026).

🔴 **Gross margin rolled over in Q2 2026**, down 4.7 points from the Q1 2026 peak on flat revenue. The metal is no longer outrunning the cost base.

🔴 **Young-Davidson's productive capacity is being re-underwritten in public.** Guided to approximately 5,000 tonnes per day for the second half of 2026 versus 8,000 a year ago, with the deep extraction sequence under an unfinished review (Q2 2026).

🔴 **Capital intensity at 29.6% of revenue** — well above Agnico's 19.7% and Kinross's 16.4% — with 2026 guided spending of $885–975 million. Free-cash-flow yield of 2.25% is the lowest of the peer set at what may prove the peak of the gold cycle.

🔴 **Repeated one-year slippage** at Lynn Lake, plus an acknowledged but unrestated 15% capital-cost increase.

🔴 **Securities-fraud investigations opened by three plaintiff firms** after the June 18, 2026 disclosure. No complaint filed and no finding — but a live overhang and a fair proxy for lost forecasting credibility.

🟢 **Reserves grew 32% to 16 million ounces**, the seventh consecutive annual increase, including a near-doubling at Island Gold District to over 8 million ounces (Q4 2025). This is the real asset and it is getting bigger.

🟢 **The exploration results are specific and checkable** — 178 grams per tonne over 3.5 metres at Cline-Pick, a new 200-by-300-metre high-grade zone west of the Island Gold reserves, high-grade mineralisation defined to 1.6 kilometres depth and open at depth. Comparable Canadian Shield mines run past 3 kilometres.

🟢 **Genuine net cash of about $460 million** and $1.2 billion of liquidity. The growth is self-funded; there is no dilution risk and no maturity wall.

🟢 **Hedges are being eliminated rather than added.** Of the 330,000 ounces inherited from the Argonaut Gold acquisition, 280,000 have been retired, leaving roughly 50,000 ounces across 2027. Unusual discipline: most miners add hedges into a build.

🟢 **Insider buying is real and one-directional** — six open-market purchases totalling 65,000 shares against zero sales, executed during the drawdown.

🟢 **Quarterly guidance, once reset, is being met** (Q1 and Q2 2026 both in line with re-guided ranges). The near-term operating control is better than the annual planning suggests.

## 7. HIDDEN SIGNALS

**The vocabulary of cost has changed from "temporary" to "structural," and almost nobody remarked on it.** Through 2025, every cost overrun was attributed to a discrete event with an end date — a capacitor, a rope change, weather, share-price compensation. In Q2 2026 the chief financial officer used a different register: the retention programme "will be payable in future years," "will continue into 2027," "something that is impacting the cost structure at Island Gold." That is the language of a permanently reset cost base, delivered in the tone of a routine disclosure.

**The hedge buyouts are being presented as risk reduction and are functionally the opposite.** Retiring a $1,840-per-ounce forward sale when spot is above $4,400 costs real cash today — $92.3 million in the second quarter of 2026 alone — in exchange for greater exposure to a metal price that has already fallen about 21% from its high. Management frames this as "providing increased exposure to higher gold price." It is a leveraged directional view on gold, taken with shareholder cash, by a team that in the same quarter could not forecast its own tonnes.

**"At current gold prices" is doing enormous work.** The phrase appears in almost every forward cash-flow statement across all five calls — "at current gold prices, we expect our annual free cash flow to exceed $1 billion." Because "current" has been a moving and mostly rising number, the promise has escalated without the operating plan improving. In the Q2 2026 call McCluskey acknowledged the direction had reversed — "gold prices having pulled back so strongly over the last number of months" — while the billion-dollar framing survived unchanged.

**The expansion study's cost assumption predates the cost problem.** The February 2026 Island Gold District study assumes mine-site all-in sustaining costs of $1,025 per ounce from 2028. The Canadian labour retention programme and the contractor inflation that management now calls structural were implemented in June 2026, four months after that study was published. The study's central cost figure has not been refreshed for a cost shock the company has already booked.

**A new voice with a different job.** Scott Parsons, the exploration lead, was given materially more airtime from Q4 2025 onward, and the Q2 2026 call opens mid-sentence on him repeating "Cash flow. Cash flow. Cash flow." As the operating story deteriorated, the geology story was moved to the front of the presentation. That is a rational communications choice and also a tell about which part of the business management believes will carry the narrative.

## 8. CAPITAL-ALLOCATION VERDICT (for the director)

**Classification: PIVOTING.**

This is not a deteriorating business and it is not a stable one. It is a company deliberately converting a mid-cost, mid-life asset base into a larger, longer-life, genuinely lower-cost one — and it is currently in the worst part of that conversion, where all of the capital has been committed and none of the benefit has arrived. Reserves are up 32%. Costs are up 18%. Production is down 12%. All three facts are true at once and all three are consequences of the same decision.

**The single most important thing to weigh before sizing:** the entire investment case rests on figures produced by a management team whose twelve-month forecasts have failed five consecutive times. The Island Gold District study — 534,000 ounces a year at $1,025 per ounce, an after-tax present value of $8.2 billion at $3,200 gold and $12 billion at $4,500 — is the load-bearing number in any valuation of this company. It was authored by the same people who, in the four months after publishing it, discovered $120 per ounce of permanent cost inflation they had not budgeted, and cut the current year twice. The asset is probably real. The number attached to it should be discounted for the forecaster, not just for the discount rate. Any position sized as though that study were a fact rather than a management estimate is mis-sized.

The second consideration is timing, not quality. At about $33 the shares trade near nine times clean trailing operating earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation — above Agnico Eagle at roughly 8.6 times, despite Alamos costing $350 more per ounce to produce, converting less than half the free cash flow, and delivering worse against its own guidance. The market is not treating this as a broken company; it is still paying a growth multiple for a growth plan. The cheapness is in 2029, not in 2026.

**Economic moat: NARROW.** The moat type is a *scale-cost* advantage rooted in a specific, non-replicable geological endowment: the Michipicoten greenstone belt land package of 60,000 hectares, and in particular the ability to feed high-grade Island Gold underground ore into the much larger Magino mill seven kilometres away. That processing integration is a durable, physical, location-specific cost advantage that no competitor can copy, and it is the reason the 2028 cost target is credible in principle. But it is narrow, not wide, for two reasons. First, it applies to roughly half the company — Young-Davidson and Mulatos have no such advantage and Young-Davidson is actively getting harder and more expensive as it deepens. Second, gold is an undifferentiated commodity sold at a price the company cannot influence; there are no switching costs, no brand, no network, and no pricing power of any kind.

**Moat trend: ERODING, on the evidence available today.** The discipline required by the section-8 test is to read the returns and margins rather than the plan. Gross margin fell 4.7 points in the most recent quarter on flat revenue; the cost per ounce is guided 18% above the year's opening plan, with roughly $120 per ounce of that admitted to be permanent; and the company's cost ranking against its named peers has slipped from a marketed advantage to a measured disadvantage versus Agnico. Rising costs against a falling metal price is the definition of a compressing margin, and a high-but-falling return is an eroding moat, not a wide one. The counter-argument is entirely prospective: if the Magino mill reaches 20,000 tonnes per day in the first quarter of 2028 and the shaft is commissioned in the first quarter of 2027, the trend reverses decisively. But that is a forecast from a source with a poor forecasting record, and the rule here is to grade the evidence, not the promise.

**Secular threat: NONE.** This deserves to be stated plainly because it materially changes how the eroding-moat finding should be read. Gold mining faces no structural displacement risk. There is no artificial-intelligence substitution, no disintermediation, no channel shift, no reimbursement compression, no energy-transition obsolescence. Demand for gold is not a shrinking base — if anything the monetary-hedge bid has strengthened. Ore bodies deplete, but Alamos has replaced reserves for seven consecutive years and holds 16 million ounces, roughly 29 years at current output. The terminal value here is not melting; it is cyclical and it is levered to a metal price.

Therefore the cheapness question resolves cleanly: **this is a cyclical and execution-driven de-rating, not a value trap.** The shares are down about 40% from a 52-week high of $55.41 while gold has fallen about 21% from its own high — the stock has fallen roughly twice as hard as its commodity, which is what a beta of 1.77 to gold plus a credibility loss produces. Nothing structural is impairing the terminal value. What is impairing the near-term case is a management team that has spent two years being wrong about its own mine plan, at the exact moment it is spending the most money it has ever spent. The correct posture is not "avoid" and it is not "buy the discount." It is: wait for the cost base to be re-baselined by the December 2026 three-year guidance, and demand a price that pays you for the forecaster.
