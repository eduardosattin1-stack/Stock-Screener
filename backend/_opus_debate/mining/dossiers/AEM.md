# Forensic dossier — Agnico Eagle Mines Limited (AEM)

*Prepared 2026-08-11. Source material: five consecutive quarterly earnings calls (2025-07-31, 2025-10-30, 2026-02-13, 2026-05-01, 2026-07-30), the screener's financial block, quarterly income and cash-flow statements pulled from Financial Modeling Prep, and the company's own dated press releases. Price used throughout: $181.44 per share on the New York Stock Exchange, in United States dollars, as of 2026-08-11.*

---

## 1. Narrative arc

Across five calls the story has not changed shape — and in a sector where the story usually changes shape every time the commodity moves, that is itself the finding.

The opening frame in mid-2025 was a three-part message the chief executive, Ammar Al-Joundi, stated almost word for word each quarter: record financial results, a strengthening balance sheet with rising cash returns, and heavy reinvestment in an owned project pipeline. By the fourth-quarter 2025 call in February 2026 a fourth leg was added — a decade-long production target of "up to 20% to 30%" growth and "a path to over 4 million ounces of annual production by the early 2030s". By the first quarter of 2026 a fifth leg appeared: a Finnish platform, built by consolidating land around the Ikkari deposit, targeted at more than 500,000 ounces a year.

What matters is what was *not* dropped. The five named growth projects introduced in July 2025 — Detour Lake to a million ounces, Canadian Malartic to a million ounces, Upper Beaver, Hope Bay, and the San Nicolas copper project — are the same five discussed in July 2026, with the same headline figure attached to them ("approximately 1.3 million to 1.5 million ounces of potential production") repeated verbatim in four consecutive calls. Serial promoters escalate that number every quarter. Agnico held it flat and, when Finland was added in 2026, kept it *outside* the total rather than folding it in: "that target was before the Finland land consolidation" (second quarter 2026). That is the opposite of capacity inflation.

The one genuine expansion of ambition is the decade target itself. Going from 3.45 million ounces produced in 2025 to "over 4 million" by the early 2030s is roughly a 16% increase — the "20% to 30%" phrasing therefore requires the pipeline to overshoot the stated four-million-ounce marker. The arithmetic and the adjective do not perfectly agree, and that gap is worth holding onto.

Three of these deserve more than a row.

**The cost miss is the cleanest credibility test in the file, and they narrowly failed it.** In July 2025 management explicitly reaffirmed a full-year all-in sustaining cost range of $1,250–$1,300. They came in at $1,339. Their explanation is that royalties are contractually linked to the gold price, so a $1,000-an-ounce realised-price surprise mechanically inflated the cost line; excluding royalties, cash costs were $937, below the guidance midpoint. That explanation is arithmetically honest and they published the reconciliation rather than hiding it. But a miss framed as evidence of discipline is still a miss, and an investor should note that Agnico's reported cost line will always overshoot when gold surprises to the upside.

**Capital costs are creeping, exactly as the sector's history predicts.** Within twelve months: Detour Lake underground study spending tripled from $100M to $300M; Upper Beaver rose from $200M to $300M; Hope Bay went from "reasonable at $2 billion" (first quarter 2026 question), to "slightly over $2 billion" (same call), to $2.4 billion sanctioned three weeks later. Full-year 2026 capital expenditure guidance was revised *upward* to $2.6–$2.8 billion. Management's framing is that this is acceleration into high-return work, not inflation, and the Hope Bay disclosure supports that — the mill was redesigned to open at 6,000 tonnes per day rather than ramp in two stages. But the total programme is now $5–6 billion of growth spending through 2030, a figure the chief financial officer, Jamie Porter, confirmed directly when pressed in February 2026.

**Returns on capital contradict the cost-leadership story more than management admits.** Agnico's return on invested capital is 15.7% and return on equity 22.6%. Kinross Gold posts 25.2% and 35.7%; AngloGold Ashanti posts 31.3% and 45.8%. Agnico has the *lowest cost per ounce* of the seniors but not the best return on the capital employed to get it, because it carries a much heavier asset base (intangibles are 11.0% of total assets, a legacy of the Kirkland Lake merger) and is reinvesting 19.7% of revenue as capital expenditure against Kinross's 16.4%. That is a legitimate structural point, not an accounting quibble: the premium the market pays Agnico is for *durability and jurisdiction*, not for capital efficiency.

## 3. Tone and confidence trajectory

**Rising, then deliberately sobered.** The July 2025 call opens with "a pleasure when things are going well". October 2025: "we have never been stronger than we are now, and we have never had a better future than we have today". February 2026 escalates to a decade-long growth plan. The high-water mark of promotional language arrives in July 2026: "We are only in the 3rd or 4th inning here."

That phrase is a recognised promoter tell and it deserves the flag. But the same call, and the one before it, are structured so that the chief executive personally delivers bad news at the top. In May 2026 he opened by naming two fatalities and said "I recognize and I accept that the responsibility for the safety of our people rests ultimately with myself and with my team." In July 2026 he named the third worker who died, by name, along with his family, before any financial discussion. He also volunteered the Barnat pit wall failure and the guidance consequence unprompted.

Guidance has become *more* specific over time, not less. By the first quarter of 2026 the company was publishing diesel consumption at 108 litres per ounce, a $0.78-per-litre cost assumption, and a sensitivity of about $6 per ounce for a 10% diesel move. Promoters get vaguer as the story ages. This one got more granular.

There is no offensive-to-defensive shift. Language stayed on growth and reinvestment throughout, even as the gold price fell more than 20% from its January 2026 record.

## 4. Guidance credibility

**Reliability: High, with one recurring blemish.**

- *Production* — reliable and slightly conservative. 2025 guided at a 3.4-million-ounce midpoint; delivered 3.45 million. Through the third quarter of 2025 they had hit 77% of the annual range and said they were "confident in achieving the midpoint" — they beat it. In 2026, after a pit-wall failure at Barnat on 1 July, they reaffirmed the 3.3–3.5-million-ounce range within 24 hours while flagging the lower end, and delivered two consecutive above-budget quarters (825,000 and 856,000 ounces).
- *Costs* — the weak link. Missed the top of both 2025 ranges. For 2026 they widened the ranges considerably ($1,020–$1,120 cash cost, $1,400–$1,550 all-in sustaining) and are tracking inside them. Widening the band after a miss is a rational response, but it also means the 2026 "within guidance" claim is a lower bar than the 2025 one was.
- *Capital* — deteriorating. Every major project figure discussed has moved up, and the annual capital budget was revised upward mid-year.
- *Sandbagging pattern* — mild and on production only. Costs are not sandbagged; if anything they were optimistic in 2025.

## 5. Analyst pressure points

**The junior equity portfolio.** This is the single topic management most consistently deflects. In July 2025 John Tumazos asked whether, with the book at $1.063 billion, the company should "post every night on the Investor Relations page… the value of your total stockholdings, just like you're a hedge fund manager" and whether it was time to take half a billion off the table. He got a philosophy answer about learning-by-investing and no disclosure commitment. Tanya Jakusconek pressed the same ground in October 2025 and extracted a clearer principle — proceeds go back to the general pool, not into more equities. Yet through 2026 the company kept buying: C$22.4 million into Wallbridge Mining in May, C$60 million into Cadillac Mines in July, plus a $5 million royalty buy-back from Prism Resources in June. The stated principle and the observed behaviour are not aligned. This is a small position against a $92 billion market value, but it is the one place where a producer is behaving like a fund without fund-grade disclosure.

**Reserve pricing and cut-off grades.** Jakusconek pushed twice on whether record gold prices would tempt the company to lower cut-off grades and restate reserves at a higher price deck. Both times management refused: "we don't want to lower the cutoff grade… we don't want to change the mining sequence" (October 2025), with the price deck moved only for inflation. Reserves are stated near $1,450 an ounce and resources near $1,750 while gold trades at $4,445. That refusal is the most valuable answer in the entire transcript file — it means the record reserve base is *not* a gold-price artifact, and it means there is a large, unbooked option in the ground.

**The size of the capital programme.** Jakusconek asked directly whether $5 billion was the right number for the growth plan; Porter answered $5–6 billion through 2030 without hedging. Credit for the directness; note the size.

**Safety.** Pressed in both 2026 calls. The most revealing answer came from Carol-Ann Plummer-Theriault in July 2026 on the Canadian Malartic mill fatality: engineering controls had been designed in at the start of the mine, but "over the decade plus since those controls had been put in place, there had been an erosion of the controls. They were not working properly." That is an unusually candid admission of a control-system decay, and it cuts against the operational-excellence narrative in a way management did not attempt to soften.

## 6. Red flags and green flags

🔴 **Capital-cost escalation across every project simultaneously** (2026 calls) — Detour underground study spend ×3, Upper Beaver +50%, Hope Bay +20% inside three weeks, annual budget revised up to $2.6–$2.8 billion. This is the sector's besetting sin and it is present here, in a mild form and pre-announced.

🔴 **Three fatalities in twelve months** — Fosterville (December 2025), Canadian Malartic (April 2026), Upper Beaver (May 2026) — in a company with 23 fatalities across 70 years. Beyond the human cost, this is a regulatory, permitting and productivity risk in the exact jurisdictions the investment thesis depends on.

🔴 **A growing, unmanaged junior-equity book** — over $1 billion, still being added to, with no stated cap, no disclosed valuation cadence, and an answer-by-philosophy when challenged.

🔴 **Reported costs will always overshoot on a gold spike** — royalties are price-linked; the 2025 guidance miss was structural, not a one-off, and will repeat if gold runs.

🔴 **The "20% to 30% over a decade" target does not reconcile cleanly** with the "over 4 million ounces by the early 2030s" marker from a 3.45-million-ounce base.

🟢 **No dilution — the opposite.** Diluted shares outstanding were 502.1 million in the third quarter of 2024 and 502.9 million in the second quarter of 2026, *including* a share-funded acquisition of Rupert Resources. Every quarter shows net share repurchases. In a sector defined by serial equity issuance, this is the strongest single data point in the file.

🟢 **Buybacks executed in the lower half of the range.** The second-quarter 2026 repurchase of 2.24 million shares averaged $178.86 against a 52-week range of $130.04–$255.24 — the 39th percentile, and below today's $181.44. Management is not top-ticking its own stock.

🟢 **Deleveraging came first, buybacks second.** $1.6 billion of gross debt retired across 2024–25 *before* the $2 billion repurchase authorisation was raised. That is the correct order for a cyclical and it earned two credit-rating upgrades.

🟢 **Reserves stated at roughly a third of the spot gold price**, replaced fully net of depletion, with 47.1 million ounces of measured and indicated and 41.8 million ounces of inferred resources behind 55.4 million ounces of reserves.

🟢 **Cash-flow quality is clean.** Income quality (operating cash flow divided by net income) of 1.26 on a trailing basis; the depressed first-quarter 2026 operating cash flow is fully explained by a disclosed $1.8 billion cash-tax payment, half of it a 2025 catch-up.

🟢 **Structural cost insulation that is verifiable, not asserted.** Ontario and Quebec production runs on hydro and nuclear grid power; Nunavut diesel is pre-purchased through the barge season and hedged; the majority of production is underground and therefore less diesel-intensive. The published sensitivity — a 10% diesel move worth about $6 an ounce — is small enough to be checkable and was reaffirmed in a quarter when oil traded above $100 a barrel.

## 7. Hidden signals

**"We are only in the 3rd or 4th inning here"** (July 2026) is the first genuinely promotional flourish in five calls. It arrived in the same quarter as the company's highest-ever cash balance and its largest buyback. Watch whether the register keeps drifting.

**The Finland acquisition was justified by a discount-to-net-asset-value claim that cannot be checked.** Al-Joundi: "we acquired it effectively by our own internal models at quite a good discount to NAV… and we got the rest of the land package for free," followed immediately by "I can't get into all of the numbers." Tumazos had just computed roughly $1,268 an ounce of acquisition plus development capital against 4.22 million ounces of resource. Management neither confirmed nor corrected the arithmetic. An unverifiable value claim on a completed deal is a small thing, but it is exactly where a sceptic should keep a marker.

**A quiet shift from surface drilling to underground conversion drilling** at Canadian Malartic (February 2026, Guy Gosselin) is a genuine cost signal buried in a geology answer: converting inferred resource to reserve from underground development is far cheaper per ounce than long holes from surface. It implies future reserve additions come at a declining unit cost — a real margin tailwind nobody on the call priced.

**The pace of reserve conversion is self-capped at about 500,000 ounces a year.** Gosselin was explicit in February 2026. That is a hard ceiling on how fast the 41.8-million-ounce inferred pile can become bankable reserve, and it bounds how quickly the growth story can compound.

**Divestment of non-core assets has been hinted at for four consecutive quarters without a single announced sale.** "It's not a bad time to in some cases, sell those assets" (October 2025). Nothing has been sold. Treat any sum-of-the-parts credit for divestitures as speculative.

## 8. Capital-allocation verdict (for the director)

**Trajectory: STRENGTHENING.** The balance sheet, the reserve base, the cost position and the shareholder-return rate have all improved in the same direction across five consecutive quarters, and the improvements are corroborated by the statements, not just the script. This is the highest-quality operator in the peer set on every axis except return on capital employed.

**The single most important thing to weigh before sizing.** It is not management quality and it is not the balance sheet — both are as good as this sector offers. It is that **the entire question is price, and the price has already moved.** The stock is $181.44 after rising roughly 25% in a month, against a $130.04 low set when gold bottomed near $3,353. The commodity itself sits at $4,445, some 21% below its January 2026 record of $5,589 but 33% above its own 52-week low. Meanwhile the enterprise value is 8.6 times trailing EBITDA — the *top* of the senior peer group (Kinross 5.4×, AngloGold 6.8×, Alamos 7.8×, Newmont 8.2×). The premium is earned; the question is whether it is fully paid for. The director is not being asked to judge whether Agnico is a good company. It plainly is. The director is being asked to buy the best company in the sector at the most expensive multiple in the sector, after a 25% run, in a commodity that has already had its record print.

**Economic moat: NARROW, and widening.** The moat type is scale-cost combined with an irreplaceable-asset intangible. The Abitibi platform in Quebec and Ontario hosts five of ten operating mines including Canada's two largest gold mines, and delivered over a million ounces at roughly $850 an ounce cash cost with a 73% operating margin in the first half of 2025 — a regional density no competitor can replicate because the ground is not for sale. Add tier-one jurisdiction concentration (Canada, Finland, Australia, Mexico) with no African or high-expropriation-risk exposure, hydro and nuclear grid power in the core, and a 16-year reserve life at current production. The width judgement is *narrow* rather than *wide* because the output is an undifferentiated commodity with no pricing power and the assets deplete: this is a moat around a cost position, not around a customer. The *trend* is widening — EBITDA margin is 71.8% and rising, return on invested capital is 15.7% and rising, reserves grew net of depletion, and the underground-conversion shift lowers the future cost of reserve replacement.

**Secular threat: manageable.** There is no structural force eroding the terminal value of gold production — no technological displacement, no disintermediation, no channel shift. The genuine long-term risks are ordinary mining risks: depletion (mitigated by a replacement record and 89 million ounces of resource behind reserve), permitting and social licence (elevated by three fatalities in twelve months), and Arctic logistics at Nunavut and Hope Bay. The cheapness question does not arise, because the stock is not cheap; it is a quality asset at a full price. That makes the risk a *valuation* risk, not a value-trap risk — a distinction that matters, because a full price on a widening moat corrects through time and a lower entry, while a value trap never corrects at all.
