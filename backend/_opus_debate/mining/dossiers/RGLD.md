# Royal Gold, Inc. (RGLD) — Forensic Dossier

*Prepared 4 August 2026. Live price used throughout: $207.27 on the Nasdaq, in US dollars (quote taken 4 August 2026). Gold futures on the same day: $4,153.90 an ounce, against a 200-day average of $4,587 and a 52-week high of $5,626.80.*

Royal Gold is not a mine operator. It owns royalties and streams — contracts that entitle it to a slice of the gold, silver and copper produced at mines other companies build and run. A royalty pays a percentage of revenue with no cost attached. A stream is a prepaid purchase agreement: Royal Gold paid cash years ago and now buys metal at a fixed or heavily discounted price and sells it at the market price. That distinction governs everything below, because it means the company's unit cost is written into a contract rather than dug out of the ground.

Transcripts reviewed: five consecutive quarterly calls from 8 May 2025 through 7 May 2026, plus the company's preliminary second-quarter 2026 disclosure filed 21 July 2026.

---

## 1. Narrative arc

The story changes shape decisively at the midpoint of this window, and management tells you so directly.

**Opening posture (first quarter 2025, 8 May 2025) — a steady, mature royalty book.** The emphasis is on continuity: a "solid start," an adjusted EBITDA margin (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, as a share of revenue) of 82%, and the full repayment of the Rainy River advanced stream deposit, an investment made back in 2015. The new-business ambition is modest and explicitly bounded — chief executive Bill Heissenbuttel tells analyst Tanya Jakusconek that normal deal size is "$100 million to $300 million." The largest single new item that quarter is a $50 million top-up at Xavantina. This is a company describing itself as a compounder, not a consolidator.

**The pivot (second quarter 2025, 7 August 2025) — scale becomes the story.** In one quarter the company announces the acquisitions of Sandstorm Gold and Horizon Copper and buys a $1 billion gold stream on First Quantum's Kansanshi copper mine in Zambia. The language changes with it: Royal Gold is now positioning itself as "a premier growth company," and the specific promise made is diversification — "no asset accounting for more than about 12% of net asset value" once the deals close. Management is careful to frame this as scale, not a change of direction: "these transactions do not shift our strategic focus."

**Execution (third quarter 2025, 7 November 2025).** Sandstorm and Horizon close on 20 October 2025. The first Kansanshi gold delivery arrives in early October. Two pieces of genuinely good operating news land in the same quarter: Centerra extends Mount Milligan's mine life from 2036 to 2045, and Barrick's update on the Fourmile deposit describes a "multigenerational project" with the potential to produce 600,000 to 750,000 ounces a year over 25 years — ground Royal Gold covers with royalties bought in 2022 at an effective 1.6% gross rate.

**Digestion (fourth quarter and full year 2025, 19 February 2026).** 2025 revenue of $1.03 billion, operating cash flow of $705 million, net income of $466 million. Heissenbuttel calls it "a transformational year." The portfolio is now roughly 80 producing and 30 development interests, and the company changes its disclosure to group interests regionally. The dividend rises for the 25th consecutive year, to $1.90 a share.

**Consolidation and, for the first time, self-purchase (first quarter 2026, 7 May 2026).** Revenue of $469 million, up 143% year over year. $300 million repaid on the revolving credit facility. Two new tools announced: a $600 million accordion that would take total revolver capacity to $2 billion, and a $500 million share repurchase authorisation — the first in the company's history. The reasoning Heissenbuttel gives is the single most useful valuation sentence in the entire file, quoted in full in section 7.

The growth driver that was quietly dropped: the standalone development-equity model. Royal Gold spent this window unwinding exactly that. It restructured the Bear Creek debt and equity holdings into cash, Calibre Silver shares and royalties on Corani and Mercedes (first quarter 2026), sold the Highlander shares, and on 18 May 2026 cut its Hod Maden project interest from a joint-venture equity stake to a 15% interest plus a royalty. Each move trades capital-call exposure for contract exposure. That is a narrowing back toward the core model, and it is the right direction.

**Where the story is poorer than it looks.** The screener shows trailing free cash flow of **negative $244 million** and margin-of-safety readings of −100% on the discounted-cash-flow, earnings-power, owner-earnings and 15%-return models. Read plainly, that looks like a company burning cash. It is not. Operating cash flow was **positive $862 million**; the negative figure comes from **$1.107 billion of investing outflow that is portfolio purchase** — the $1 billion Kansanshi stream, the Warintza advance, the Hod Maden funding. For a royalty company, buying a royalty is the equivalent of an acquisition, not of maintenance spending. Any model that treats it as recurring capital expenditure will value this company at close to zero, and four of the five in our stack do exactly that. The "11.5 months of runway" figure in the same block is meaningless against $1.0 billion of undrawn revolver capacity.

**Where the story is richer than it looks.** Reported gross margin fell from 87.2% in the first quarter of 2025 to 67.1% in the first quarter of 2026 while revenue rose 143%. That reads as deteriorating economics. It is almost entirely non-cash: depreciation, depletion and amortisation tripled from $32.9 million to $90.9 million a quarter, and the company guided $95–99 million for the second quarter of 2026, because acquiring Sandstorm's assets reset their book values upward and depletion is charged against those higher values. The cash margin did not move.

## 3. Tone and confidence trajectory

**Rating: Rising through the first quarter of 2026, then steady — and consistently understated relative to results.**

- *First quarter 2025:* measured. "Our first quarter gave us a solid start to 2025." On the Pueblo Viejo silver that has been deferred for years: "I would just say to temper expectations when it comes to the timing of the deferred silver... it may come in over a few years." That is a chief executive talking down his own asset.
- *Second quarter 2025:* confident and expansionary — "another excellent quarter," "premier growth company." But when analyst Lawson Winder puts it to him that "the shares without question, have underperformed since you announced the Sandstorm transaction" and asks about a buyback, the answer is a flat no: "At this point, I don't think so... debt repayment, I think, is going to be a key focus before we ever get to consideration of a buyback."
- *Third quarter 2025:* the most revealing quarter for tone. With gold near $4,000 an ounce, Heissenbuttel volunteers that dealmaking is getting harder because "I don't think anybody in our sector is using $4,000 an ounce to value things. But at the same time, I'm not sure the seller is going to be accepting of a long-term consensus price of $3,000." A promoter would have used a $4,000 gold price as a marketing plank. He used it as a reason to be careful. Asked about joining the S&P 500 he says plainly, "I think we're still a bit of ways."
- *Fourth quarter 2025:* organisational, not triumphal. The emphasis is on getting one-off costs out of the way — "I want everything done by the end of this year that is nonrecurring... we need to start building this record of quarter-over-quarter."
- *First quarter 2026:* the most confident of the five, and for the first time the confidence is expressed in capital rather than adjectives: a bigger revolver, a repurchase authorisation, and $300 million of debt retired.

There is no shift from offensive to defensive language across the window. There is a shift from *acquisitive* to *integrative*, which is different and appropriate.

## 4. Guidance credibility

**Rating: High.**

Three independent pieces of evidence point the same way.

First, the delivery record. Full-year 2025 results came in inside every guidance range issued in March 2025 except other-metals revenue, which exceeded the top of its range (19 February 2026). That was achieved despite Centerra cutting Mount Milligan gold guidance mid-year on lower-than-expected grades and Ero cutting Xavantina guidance. The screener's own count is a beat in 5 of the last 7 quarters, or 71%.

Second, the method, which management set out unprompted in the second quarter of 2025 when pressed on how the ranges are built. Martin Raffield, who runs operations: "We don't take the guidance ranges supplied by the operators and just use those... We receive monthly budget data from the stream partners. We forecast each asset based on historical performance... We build in timing adjustments between production deliveries and sales." He then volunteers the weak point himself — that concentrate shipping lags can run "up to 5 to 6 months" and that "it's a pretty inexact process." Naming your own error bar is credibility-positive.

Third, and most telling, what they leave *out*. Twice — on the Mount Milligan cost-support ounces and the deferred gold — management explicitly excluded ounces from both the sales guidance and the gold-equivalent-ounce count, even though including them would have flattered the headline. Chief financial officer Paul Libner, second quarter 2025: "those ounces will not be part of our 2025 sales guidance, and they're not going to be reflected in our GEO calculations." That is the opposite of the capacity inflation this sector is known for.

There is no sandbagging pattern worth flagging. The ranges are wide enough to absorb operator variance and they are hit, not crushed.

## 5. Analyst pressure points

**The buyback, asked four times over four calls.** Lawson Winder raised it in the second quarter of 2025; Josh Wolfson of RBC opened the first quarter of 2026 by noting he had "also asked on the buyback as I have done historically." Management said no repeatedly and then reversed — but reversed on stated logic rather than on pressure, and only after the share price fell. This was analyst persistence that was eventually vindicated.

**The Pueblo Viejo silver deferral — the one genuine sore.** Tanya Jakusconek: "every day, keep thinking about the silver circuit." At the end of the first quarter of 2025 the deferral stood at **1.97 million ounces**, and silver recovery at the mine must exceed **52.5%** before Royal Gold receives any of it. Management's guidance a year on (fourth quarter 2025 call) was that Barrick is feeding highly weathered stockpile material that is hurting flotation and autoclave performance, and that "the stockpiles are going to be part of the ore feed for the next 5 years," as Jakusconek pointed out. This is the clearest case in the file of a stream promise not arriving on the original schedule, and it is not resolved.

**Deal size, asked every single quarter.** The answer drifted from "$100 million to $300 million" (first quarter 2025) to "we are seeing larger ones" (same call, under pressure) to "$100 million to $500 million" (fourth quarter 2025), with Heissenbuttel explaining the inflation honestly: "with metal prices where they are, every gold-equivalent ounce we buy is going to be more expensive." That is a straight answer to a question about capital discipline.

**Visibility on the acquired and net-profit-interest assets.** Brian MacArthur pressed on Antamina, which delivered $13 million in the first quarter of 2026. Heissenbuttel's answer — "$13 million was more than the annual revenue received in certain years in the past... NPIs are impossible to estimate" — and Raffield's "we have almost no visibility into that" are candid, but they confirm that a slice of the enlarged revenue base is genuinely unforecastable quarter to quarter.

**What analysts are not yet pressing on, and should be:** the return on the capital deployed in 2025. Nobody on these calls asked management to defend an 8.2% return on invested capital against Wheaton's 18.1%.

## 6. Concerning and positive signals

**Concerning**

- *Returns on the enlarged capital base are the worst in the peer group.* Return on invested capital of 8.2% and return on equity of 9.0% for 2025, against Wheaton at 18.1%, Franco-Nevada at 14.3%, OR Royalties at 13.9% and Triple Flag at 11.6%. Forward maths is better — first-quarter 2026 earnings annualised against period-end equity is roughly 15% — but the trailing number is the honest scoreboard on the 2025 deals, and it says Royal Gold paid a full price.
- *Management effectively conceded the point.* In the first quarter of 2026 Heissenbuttel said sector investments "are being priced at 1.7 times" net asset value while his own shares had traded "at a P/NAV of less than 1.4 times." Buying at 1.7 times with paper worth 1.4 times is value-dilutive to existing owners, and he knows it — that is precisely why the repurchase authorisation exists.
- *Insiders are selling, not buying.* Zero open-market purchases against 18 disposals totalling 18,048 shares. The company is buying its stock while the people running it are not. Both facts are true and they point in opposite directions.
- *The metal has turned.* Gold peaked at $5,366 on 1 March 2026 and trades at $4,153.90 today, roughly 23% lower and 9% below its own 200-day average. Royal Gold's measured sensitivity to gold is 1.25 times, and the deterministic torque estimate says a 10% move in the metal swings earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation by about 12.7% — in **both** directions.
- *The development book still calls for cash.* $70 million into Hod Maden and $50 million into Warintza in the second quarter of 2026 alone. The claim that a royalty company never writes cheques is true of the producing book and false of the pipeline.
- *Quantitative models turned negative.* Zacks moved the shares to its lowest rank on 22 June and again on 13 July 2026, consistent with falling gold cutting forward estimates.

**Positive**

- *The counterparty list is exceptional.* Barrick, Newmont, Teck, First Quantum, Vale, Glencore, Ivanhoe, MMG, Zijin and Centerra operate the assets. These are not juniors, and that is the single most durable fact in the file.
- *Reserve life is being extended, repeatedly and by others' money.* Mount Milligan from 2036 to 2045 (third quarter 2025). Pueblo Viejo to the mid-2040s. Kansanshi with "a 20-year production history and the potential to operate for a further 20-plus years." Xavantina extended four years to 2032. Fourmile described by Barrick as multigenerational at 600,000–750,000 ounces a year over 25 years, fully covered by Royal Gold's 1.6% gross royalty.
- *A royalty rate step-up nobody is talking about.* At the Cortez complex the blended royalty rate rises to 3.5–4% in 2026 from 2.6% in 2025 (fourth quarter 2025 call), against operator guidance of 700,000–780,000 ounces.
- *Debt is coming down faster than promised.* Gross borrowings of $965.6 million at 31 December 2025, $595.7 million at 31 March 2026, and $400 million drawn at 30 June 2026, with $1.0 billion undrawn.
- *The first buyback in company history was executed low, not high.* **147,205 shares at an average of $203.80, for $30 million**, during the second quarter of 2026 — within about 2% of today's price, and after the shares had fallen roughly a third from their high. Counter-cyclical repurchase is rare in this sector; the usual pattern is buying at the top.
- *Twenty-five consecutive annual dividend increases,* to $1.90 a share for 2026, and the only precious-metals company in the S&P High Yield Dividend Aristocrats index.
- *Complexity is being removed, not added.* Bear Creek converted to cash, shares and royalties; Highlander sold; Hod Maden reduced from a joint-venture stake to 15% plus a royalty.

## 7. Hidden signals

**The valuation sentence.** In the first quarter of 2026 call, asked what changed on the buyback, Heissenbuttel gave the clearest window into management's own net-asset-value work anywhere in these transcripts: *"if you can buy back your shares at 1.4 times, and investments in the sector are being priced at 1.7 times, we should know our assets better than anything new we are going to add to the portfolio. So if we are going to use capital, is that a better use of capital?"* Two things follow. First, this is a management team that has internalised the discipline of comparing acquisitions against its own share price — the discipline this sector most often lacks. Second, it is a soft disclosure that the shares were trading roughly 18% below where the market prices comparable assets.

**A ring-fence most managements would not build.** In the same call: *"we do not plan to use expanded revolver capacity to buy back shares."* Borrowing to repurchase stock at a cyclical high in the underlying commodity is one of the classic ways mining balance sheets are destroyed. Ruling it out unprompted is a meaningful governance signal.

**Terminology shift: from assets to record.** The fourth-quarter 2025 language — "we need to start building this record of quarter-over-quarter of sort of recurring business where we can show the revenue, we can show the cash flow" — marks the moment management stopped selling the transaction and started selling the run rate. The corresponding disclosure change (regional grouping with the largest interests broken out) is consistent with that.

**They decline to front-run their own operators.** Asked about Cortez in the first quarter of 2026, Raffield: "we should just wait for Barrick to come out on Monday. I would not like to front-run anything that they are going to say at this stage." Small, but it is the behaviour of a company that does not selectively leak good news.

**No new voices, and that matters.** The same four executives — Heissenbuttel, Libner, Raffield and Alistair Baker in investor relations — run every call in the window, through a transformational acquisition. There is no chief financial officer turnover, no restatement, no auditor change. In a sector where management churn often precedes bad news, continuity through integration is a positive.

## 8. Capital-allocation verdict

**Classification: Strengthening.**

The business is materially larger, more diversified and lower-risk per asset than it was fifteen months ago, cash margins never broke, guidance was delivered through two operator disappointments, debt is being retired ahead of schedule, and the company initiated its first-ever repurchase near a cyclical low rather than a high. Very little in the transcripts triggers the mining kill list: there is no serial equity issuance beyond one disclosed, shareholder-approved acquisition; no capacity or backlog inflation — in fact management repeatedly excluded ounces that would have flattered the headline; and the tone is conspicuously unpromotional at exactly the moments a promoter would reach for the megaphone.

**The single thing to weigh before sizing.** Not whether the business is good — it is — but **what metal price the price you pay assumes.** Royal Gold's earnings are struck against gold at $4,154 an ounce today, having realised roughly $4,871 in the first quarter of 2026 and $4,506 in the second. The metal is 23% below its March 2026 peak and below its own 200-day average, and the company's measured sensitivity is 1.25 times. An apparently cheap multiple on earnings generated at a metal price near an all-time high is not the same thing as a cheap multiple on normalised earnings. That, and not credibility, is the live question.

**Economic moat: narrow, and stable.** The moat type is a **regulatory and contractual licence** — perpetual land-based royalties and long-dated stream agreements that cannot be competed away, whose unit cost ($871 per ounce) is written into a contract and therefore does not inflate with diesel, labour or grade decline the way a miner's costs do. That is genuinely durable. But the width test has to be run on the returns evidence, and the returns evidence is not yet flattering: 8.2% return on invested capital and 9.0% on equity for 2025 are the lowest in a peer group where Wheaton earns 18.1%. The contracts are wide-moat; the returns on the enlarged capital base have not yet proved it. The trend is stable rather than eroding, because the dilution is a one-off step-up in the capital base from the 2025 acquisitions, not a decay in contract economics — cash margins held at 83% throughout.

**Secular threat: none.** Nothing in the structural-force list touches this business. There is no software substitute for a gold royalty, no disintermediation path, no reimbursement regime and no channel shift. The threat here is entirely cyclical — the gold price — which by definition does not impair terminal value, only the path to it. Where the shares are cheap, the cheapness is a fear print about the metal and about a recently digested acquisition, not a structurally shrinking base. The honest caveat is that a cyclical peak in the commodity can look identical to a cheap multiple, and telling those apart is the work of the valuation, not of this dossier.
