# Alamos Gold Inc. (AGI) — Forensic Interrogator Dossier

**Prepared 2026-08-04. Price used: $28.91 per share, US dollars, NYSE listing (Alamos is dual-listed on the Toronto Stock Exchange under the same ticker; every figure in this note is in US dollars, which is also the company's own reporting currency).**

A note on the ticker before anything else: "AGI" is shared by two unrelated companies. Alamos Gold Inc. trades under AGI on both the New York and Toronto exchanges; Ag Growth International, a Winnipeg farm-equipment maker, is also nicknamed AGI but trades under AFN. Several news items filed under this ticker belong to the farm-equipment company, including a June 2026 strategic-review announcement. None of that belongs to Alamos, and none of it is used here.

---

## 1. Narrative arc

Across the five calls in this record — August 2025 through July 2026 — Alamos has told one story with remarkable consistency, and that consistency is itself the thing to examine.

The story is: *we own one of the best undeveloped growth pipelines in gold, we can fund it entirely out of our own cash flow, and production roughly doubles by the end of the decade while unit costs fall.* In August 2025 the chief executive, John McCluskey, framed it as "one of the strongest outlooks in our sector" and put a number on it — "over 900,000 ounces per year" by late 2028 and "approximately 1 million ounces per year" longer term. By February 2026 the same story had been upgraded, not revised: mineral reserves (the ore the company has proven it can economically mine) rose 32% to 16 million ounces, reserves at the flagship Island Gold District nearly doubled to over 8 million ounces, and the expansion study was enlarged from a 12,400-tonne-per-day plan to 20,000 tonnes per day. Management put an after-tax net present value (the discounted worth of the future cash flows) of $8.2 billion on the Island Gold District at a $3,200 gold price, rising to $12 billion at $4,500.

What has changed is not the long-term story. It is the near-term delivery, which has deteriorated in every single one of these five quarters.

- **August 2025:** 2025 cost guidance raised 12%. Management attributed roughly 40% to external factors.
- **October 2025:** 2025 production guidance cut 6% after a mill capacitor failure at Magino and a seismic event at Island Gold.
- **February 2026:** full-year 2025 production came in at 545,000 ounces — *below even the reduced guidance* — and all-in sustaining costs of $1,524 per ounce landed above the revised cost guidance too.
- **June 2026 (disclosed 18 June, outside this transcript set):** two further seismic events at Young-Davidson, one at an active mining front, plus three days of storm-related power downtime. Second-quarter production guidance cut to 130,000–135,000 ounces. The shares fell about 12% on 22 June 2026.
- **July 2026:** full-year 2026 guidance cut a second time — production from 570,000–650,000 ounces to 510,000–560,000, and all-in sustaining costs raised from $1,500–1,600 to $1,775–1,875 per ounce.

The single most important arithmetic in this dossier: in February 2026 management guided to a **12% production increase** for the year at costs of $1,500–1,600 per ounce. Five months later that had become a **roughly 2% production decrease** versus 2025 at costs of $1,775–1,875 per ounce. The growth year was not merely trimmed. It was erased, and the cost base moved 17% above a 2025 figure that had itself already missed.

Two growth drivers have also quietly slipped. Lynn Lake, the new Manitoba mine, moved from "the latter part of 2028" (August 2025) to "initial production in 2029" (October 2025), with the chief financial officer, Greg Fisher, conceding on the same call that "we can expect a 15% increase in our capital that we put out in the feasibility study." And La Yaqui Grande in Mexico is now taking longer to leach than planned, deferring ounces into 2027 — management is clear no ounces are lost, only delayed, and the mine is "right at the very end of its life" in any case.

The critical distinction the numbers force: **every financial record Alamos has set in this period was delivered by the gold price, not by the mines.** Gold's average realised price rose from $3,223 per ounce in the second quarter of 2025 to $4,504 in the second quarter of 2026 — a 40% increase. Over the same window production *fell* from 137,000 ounces to 130,600, and all-in sustaining costs *rose* from $1,475 to $1,728 per ounce. Revenue grew 46.5% year-on-year and gross margin (revenue less direct mining costs, as a share of revenue) improved from 54.2% to 59.2% — but sequentially, from the first quarter of 2026 to the second, gross margin *fell* from 63.9% to 59.2%. The metal is doing the work, and in the most recent quarter it stopped covering for the cost line.

One deterministic warning about the machine-generated metrics in this file. The screening proxy reports a trailing-twelve-month operating margin of 79.3%, which would place Alamos among the most profitable producers in the industry. That figure is inflated: it absorbs the $227M after-tax gain on the sale of the Turkish development assets booked in the fourth quarter of 2025. Cleaned of that one-off, the second-quarter 2026 operating margin is 68.5%. **The proxy and the company's own reported unit costs disagree, and the company's figures are the honest ones.**

## 3. Tone and confidence trajectory

**Declining, with the decline carefully managed.**

- *August 2025:* offensive and specific. "We've been providing guidance like this for a very long time. We take it very seriously... we're very confident in our forecast." Growth language dominates.
- *October 2025:* the first defensive turn, and notably self-aware. "This has been far from a typical production year for Alamos... we've taken the prudent course and lowered guidance." Management immediately reasserted the long-term frame — "they in no way take away from the quality of our mines."
- *February 2026:* confidence restored by balance-sheet actions rather than operations. The dividend was raised 60%, reserves rose 32%, the expansion was enlarged. Production for 2025 had missed, and that fact occupied two sentences of a long call.
- *April 2026:* mixed. Genuine operating progress at Island Gold (record underground mining rate of 1,423 tonnes per day), offset by the first mention of "ongoing inflationary pressures across our cost structure, including higher labour, contractor, diesel, and electricity costs."
- *July 2026:* the most defensive call in the set, and the most candid. The cost bridge was itemised without evasion: roughly $90 per ounce from labour and contractors, of which about $30 per ounce is a new Canadian retention programme implemented in June "in response to the competitive market environment." Mr McCluskey volunteered that "gold prices having pulled back so strongly over the last number of months" makes the market attractive — an unusually direct acknowledgement of a falling tape.

Specificity has actually *increased* as results have worsened, which is a credit signal rather than a warning one. This management does not hide behind aggregates; it names the tonnes, the grades, the dollars per ounce, and the reason.

## 4. Guidance credibility

**Low, and unambiguously so — but for reasons of forecasting optimism, not concealment.**

The record over eight quarters: 2025 cost guidance raised 12% in July; 2025 production guidance cut 6% in October; full-year 2025 delivered *below* the reduced production guidance and *above* the revised cost guidance; 2026 production guidance cut in June; 2026 production and cost guidance both cut again in July. That is five downward revisions and two outright misses of already-revised targets, in two years.

There is no sandbagging pattern here — the opposite. Alamos consistently guides to what the mine plan says under good conditions, and mining rarely offers good conditions. Management explicitly claims the conservative-forecaster mantle ("we pride ourselves on providing consistently accurate guidance," October 2025) while the evidence does not support it. The mismatch between the claim and the record is the single sharpest forensic finding in this dossier.

Two mitigating facts belong on the record. First, the causes have genuinely been physical and largely unforeseeable — a capacitor failure, seismic events, forest fires, a winter storm that shut an underground mine for three days. Second, the *reserve* forecasting has been excellent: mineral reserves have grown for seven consecutive years, and the mineral inventory at Island Gold has grown for ten. Alamos is much better at finding ore than at scheduling its extraction. For an investor, that is a meaningful distinction — it argues the long-run asset value is real even where the annual numbers are not reliable.

Reliability score: **Low on annual operating guidance. High on geology and reserves.**

## 5. Analyst pressure points

Analysts have circled three subjects persistently, and management's answers have been full but have not always aged well.

**Seismicity at Young-Davidson.** Don DeMarco of National Bank has asked about it directly in multiple quarters. In October 2025 the answer was that seismicity "is a normal part of operating an underground mine" with "no personnel or equipment impacted." In July 2026 — after two further events, one at an active mining front — Mr DeMarco pressed on frequency and trend as the mine deepens. Mr McCluskey's answer: "it is not that we are seeing more events overall or higher events overall." Yet the operational consequence has escalated each time, from a delayed mining front in 2025 to a mining rate cut from roughly 7,500 to about 5,000 tonnes per day in 2026, with enhanced ground support now becoming "the standard going forward in the lower mine" at an added $10–15M a year of sustaining capital. **This is the topic analysts pushed on that later became bad news, and it is the one to keep pushing.**

**Buyback cadence.** Asked repeatedly (Bank of America, October 2025 and April 2026) for a target run rate or a minimum cash balance. Mr Fisher declines to give a target — "we've never put targets in place... we always want to be opportunistic." That is a legitimate answer, but it makes the buyback unmodellable, and the actual execution deserves scrutiny (see section 6).

**The cost path to 2028.** Sathish Kasinathan of Bank of America asked the right question in July 2026: how much of the cost increase is *structural* going into 2027? Mr Fisher answered honestly that the retention programme "will continue into 2027." Nobody on any of these calls has yet forced management to bridge, line by line, from $1,825 per ounce today to the roughly $1,250 target for 2028. That bridge is the whole investment case and it remains unaudited by the street.

## 6. Red flags and green flags

**Concerning**

- **The guidance record versus the guidance claim.** Five downward revisions in eight quarters while asserting a reputation for accuracy (October 2025).
- **Securities-fraud investigations opened.** Pomerantz LLP, the Schall Law Firm and Bronstein, Gewirtz & Grossman all announced investigations of Alamos between late June and July 2026, following the 18 June disclosure of the Young-Davidson seismic events and the roughly 12% share-price fall on 22 June. These are law-firm-initiated investigations, not filed complaints or regulatory actions, and no wrongdoing has been established. They nonetheless create a headline and legal-cost overhang.
- **Pro-cyclical capital allocation — buying high.** In the second quarter of 2026 Alamos repurchased $50M of stock at an average $35.70 per share; the stock is now $28.91, roughly 19% lower. In the same quarter it spent $92.3M eliminating 35,000 ounces of legacy hedges at $4,458 per ounce; spot gold is now near $4,100, leaving that buyout underwater by roughly $12M at today's price. Both decisions were defensible when made and both were made near the top.
- **Costs rising into a falling gold price.** The 2026 cost guide rose 18% at the same moment the realised price began falling — $4,829 in the first quarter, $4,504 in the second, spot near $4,100 today. Margin is compressing from both ends in the second half.
- **Growth capital committed into a weaker tape.** Roughly $500M a year of growth spending continues, with Lynn Lake a $920M project not producing until 2029 and about $542M of Magino mill expansion still to spend, which management concedes carries "normal-course inflationary pressures of around 4% to 5%."

**Positive**

- **A genuine balance sheet.** Net cash of about $420M at 30 June 2026 — cash and short-term investments of $675.9M against $216M of total debt. No maturity wall; long-term debt is $200M.
- **Reserve growth is real and audited.** Reserves up 32% to 16 million ounces at year-end 2025, seven consecutive years of growth, Island Gold reserves nearly doubling to over 8 million ounces including 1.6 million ounces grading 15 grams per tonne in the Lower Island East area.
- **Insider behaviour is aligned.** Six open-market purchases totalling 65,000 shares against zero sales — the clean version of the signal, excluding grants.
- **The Turkish sale was well executed.** $470M total consideration for assets written off in 2021, with $160M received on closing in October 2025 and $310M staged over two years.
- **The hedge clean-up was the right call.** 245,000 of the 330,000 ounces of below-market forward sales inherited with Argonaut Gold have been eliminated, increasing exposure to gold. Approximately 50,000 ounces remain, maturing in 2027.
- **Island Gold is operationally delivering** even as the rest slips: record quarterly production of 67,500 ounces in the second quarter of 2026, underground mining rates at a record 1,423 tonnes per day in the first quarter rising to 1,550 in the second, on plan for 2,000 by year-end. The Phase 3+ shaft reached its planned depth of 1,381 metres and commissioning is still targeted for the first quarter of 2027.

## 7. Hidden signals

**"Mine-site" is doing quiet work.** The expansion study headline is 534,000 ounces a year at "mine site all-in sustaining costs of $1,025 per ounce." Mine-site costs exclude corporate overhead and, typically, some royalties. The consolidated number an investor actually receives will be meaningfully higher. Every impressive cost figure in this story carries that qualifier; every disappointing one does not.

**A change in cost reporting mid-stream.** In October 2025 Alamos began reporting cash costs and all-in sustaining costs *excluding* mark-to-market adjustments on previously issued share-based instruments, describing this as a "better representation." The logic is defensible — those adjustments move with the share price, not with mining. But the change was introduced in the same quarter the company was cutting guidance, and it removes from the headline cost figure an item that had been running against them.

**The retention programme is the most informative disclosure on the July 2026 call.** A mid-year, unbudgeted retention scheme for all Canadian operations, worth about $30 per ounce and continuing into 2027, tells you the labour market in northern Ontario and Manitoba is tighter than the plan assumed — and Alamos is building two new mines in exactly that labour market. This is a structural cost, not a one-off.

**Management appetite has turned inward.** Asked in July 2026 whether the Young-Davidson disruption had increased the appetite for acquisitions, Mr McCluskey was blunt: "We are not really that focused on M&A right now... we have got plenty of things to focus on." Given the sector's history of buying at cycle tops, this is the right answer and a mildly positive signal on discipline — the one place where the pro-cyclical pattern above does *not* apply.

**A leadership refresh is under way.** Multiple management appointments were announced on 21 May 2026. New voices have not yet had time to change the message.

## 8. Capital-allocation verdict (for the director)

**Trajectory classification: DETERIORATING** — on operations and forecasting credibility, not on assets or solvency. This is the important nuance. Alamos is not a company running out of money or losing its ore body. It is a company whose ability to convert an excellent ore body into the ounces it promises, on the schedule it promises, at the cost it promises, has failed five times in eight quarters and failed most severely in the most recent quarter.

**The single thing to weigh before sizing:** the entire valuation case rests on a cost bridge nobody has audited. The stock is priced for 800,000 ounces in 2028 at roughly $1,250 per ounce of all-in sustaining cost. Today's guidance is 535,000 ounces at $1,825. That is a 31% unit-cost reduction to be delivered against management's own admission of 4–6% labour and contractor inflation, a new permanent retention cost, and permanently higher ground support at Young-Davidson. The mine plan supports the direction — higher-grade Island Gold ore displacing lower-grade open-pit feed genuinely does lower unit costs. The track record does not support the timing. Size for the possibility that 2028 arrives in 2030.

**Economic moat: NARROW.** A gold producer is a price taker with no pricing power, no switching costs and no network effect. The only durable advantage available is a **scale-cost** one: a long-life, high-grade ore body in a stable jurisdiction. Alamos genuinely has one — Island Gold at 9.4 grams per tonne underground grade, over 8 million ounces of reserves, in Ontario, open laterally and at depth to 1.6 kilometres. That is a real and narrow moat.

**Moat trend: STABLE.** The evidence cuts both ways and roughly cancels. Widening: reserves up 32%, Island Gold reserves nearly doubled, and the expansion genuinely lowers Island Gold's cost curve. Eroding: consolidated unit costs rose 17% in a single guidance cycle, return on invested capital of about 14.8% sits below Agnico Eagle's 15.7% and far below Kinross's 25.2%, and Young-Davidson is becoming structurally more expensive as it deepens. The company is getting better at the flagship and worse at the rest.

**Secular threat: NONE.** Gold demand faces no structural displacement — no technology substitutes for a monetary metal, and central-bank and investment demand are if anything strengthening. The risk here is entirely cyclical (the gold price) and operational (execution). This is emphatically not a melting-ice-cube.

**Is the cheapness a fear-print or a value trap?** Neither, and that is the uncomfortable answer. The shares are down roughly 48% from a 52-week high of $55.41, which *looks* like a fear-print. But measured against peers rather than against its own past, Alamos is not cheap: it trades near 6.7 times trailing operating profit on a figure flattered by the Turkish asset-sale gain — closer to 8 times on clean forward numbers — with a free-cash-flow yield of about 2.6%, against Kinross at 4.7 times and a 10.9% free-cash-flow yield with a comparable cost structure, and Agnico Eagle at 7.1 times and 5.8% with a materially *better* one. Investors are paying a premium multiple for a growth pipeline that has now slipped twice. The share price has fallen a long way; the valuation has not fallen with it.
