# Forensic Dossier — Agnico Eagle Mines Limited (AEM)

**Prepared:** 4 August 2026 · **Transcripts reviewed:** five consecutive quarterly calls, 31 July 2025 through 30 July 2026 · **Price used:** $152.14 on the New York listing (the company reports in U.S. dollars; the shares also trade in Toronto)

Agnico Eagle is a senior gold producer — it digs up, processes and sells gold bullion, with small amounts of silver, copper and zinc as by-products, from ten operating mines in Canada, Finland, Mexico and Australia. Gold is roughly 95% of what it sells. Everything below tests what management said against what the accounts and the filings actually show.

---

## 1. Narrative arc

The story across these five calls is remarkably stable, which by itself is informative. The same three-part message opens every call: record financial results, a stronger balance sheet with more cash returned to shareholders, and heavy reinvestment in an owned growth pipeline. Management has not swapped growth drivers, quietly retired a promise, or introduced a new one to paper over a stale one. In a sector where the story usually changes when the numbers do, this consistency is the single strongest credibility signal in the file.

What *did* evolve is the emphasis, and it moved in one direction: from harvesting to building.

- **Mid-2025** the pitch was margin capture. The chief executive framed it as pass-through: gold rose about $400 an ounce in the quarter while cash costs rose about $30, so "we're delivering 93% of this remarkable gold price increase to our owners" (Q2 2025). Growth was a list of five projects described as "potential."
- **February 2026** the pitch became a decade-long production plan. Management put a number on it — production up "20% to 30% over the next decade" with "a path to over 4 million ounces of annual production by the early 2030s" — and told the market the capital bill: "$5 billion to $6 billion of growth spending over the course of '26 through 2030" (chief financial officer James Porter, Q4 2025 call).
- **May 2026 onward** the plan started converting into committed spending. The Hope Bay project received a final go-ahead on 19 May 2026, the Finnish land package was consolidated for $578 million of cash in the second quarter of 2026, and 2026 capital spending guidance was lifted from $2.2–2.4 billion to $2.605–2.825 billion.

A fourth theme was added, unprompted, in late 2025 and has stayed: productivity. And a fifth, tragically, in 2026: safety, after three workplace fatalities in roughly twelve months.

The honest summary of the arc is that the company is deliberately trading near-term free cash flow for production a decade out. That is defensible. It is also the reason the stock's cash-flow yield now looks ordinary next to peers that are harvesting rather than building.

**Where the story is richer than the trajectory justifies.** Two places. First, the word "record" opens every call, and the driver is the gold price rather than the business — a point management concedes: "one should expect record financial results when we have record gold prices" (Q2 2025). That self-awareness is a credit to them, but it does not change the fact that the headline is a price headline. Second, and more concretely, the record broke in the most recent quarter and the framing did not. Second-quarter 2026 revenue was $3.71 billion against $4.10 billion in the first quarter — down 9.6% — *even though gold production rose* from 825,100 to 855,816 ounces. Realised revenue per ounce fell roughly 13% quarter on quarter. Gross margin went from 66.4% to 62.2%. The call still opened with "record." Free cash flow was indeed a record $1,335 million, but that record owes a great deal to the timing of tax payments: the company front-loaded $1.8 billion of 2026 cash taxes into the first quarter, flattering the second.

## 3. Tone and confidence trajectory

**Rating: Stable, with a late shift from confidence to accountability.**

- *Q2 2025* — buoyant and specific. "It's always a pleasure to speak to all of you and particularly a pleasure when things are going well."
- *Q3 2025* — the most expansive tone in the file: "With almost 70 years of history behind us, we have never been stronger than we are now, and we have never had a better future than we have today."
- *Q4 2025 (February 2026)* — the tone turns strategic and long-dated. The excitement is explicitly redirected away from current results: "the real excitement this morning is not in looking back or even the next 3 years."
- *Q1 2026* — first crack. Two fatalities disclosed, and the chief executive takes personal ownership: "I recognize and I accept that the responsibility for the safety of our people rests ultimately with myself and with my team."
- *Q2 2026* — a third fatality, named, with a precise historical frame: 23 fatalities in almost 70 years, three of them in the last year. Alongside it, the first genuinely defensive operational disclosure of the file — a rock-mass movement in the wall of the Barnat pit on 1 July 2026.

Guidance has become *more* specific over time, not less — the tell of a management team that is comfortable being measured. The February 2026 call volunteered a five-year capital number ($5–6 billion) that nobody would have forced them to give. The first-quarter 2026 call volunteered a diesel sensitivity precise enough to be checked: 108 litres per ounce, about 7% of the cost base, a 10% diesel move worth roughly $6 an ounce. That is not the language of a team managing perception.

There is one clear tonal shift from offence to defence, and it is confined to safety. Operationally and financially, the register has not changed.

## 4. Guidance credibility

**Reliability: High, with one documented cost miss.**

*Production.* Delivered. Full-year 2025 production of 3.45 million ounces beat the 3.4 million midpoint of guidance. 2026 guidance of 3.3–3.5 million ounces has been reiterated on every call; after the Barnat pit event the company moved to "near the lower end" within twenty-four hours of the incident rather than waiting for the quarter. That is fast, unflattering disclosure — a credibility asset.

*Costs.* The one real blemish. In July 2025 management said it expected to land "within the guided range of $915 to $965 per ounce." In October 2025 that softened to "at or near the top end." The year finished at $979, above the range, with all-in sustaining cost at $1,339 against a $1,250–1,300 guide. Every quarter the explanation was royalties and the Canadian dollar, and every quarter an adjusted figure was offered that would have been inside the range. The mechanic is genuine — royalties are contractually tied to the gold price, so a $1,000-an-ounce price beat mechanically breaks a cost guide. But a company that guides costs without a price-linked royalty adjustment is choosing a guide it can miss. For 2026 the ranges were widened ($1,020–1,120 cash cost, $1,400–1,550 all-in sustaining) and the second quarter landed comfortably inside at $1,054 and $1,459.

*Capital.* 2026 capital spending was raised from $2.2–2.4 billion to $2.605–2.825 billion — an 18% increase mid-year. This was telegraphed. In July 2025 the chief financial officer said plainly, "there is the potential for an increase in our overall capital spending"; in February 2026 he gave the $5–6 billion five-year figure. The Hope Bay capital estimate itself drifted from "$2 billion" (February 2026) to "slightly over $2 billion… below 2.5" (May 2026), with the reason given as a scope change — building the mill at 6,000 tonnes per day in one stage instead of two — rather than inflation. Disclosed, explained, and small. But it is drift.

*Sandbagging pattern.* None detectable. If anything the opposite: production guides are set at levels they beat, cost guides at levels that break when the gold price runs.

## 5. Analyst pressure points

Three topics recur, and management engages all three rather than deflecting.

**The equity investment portfolio.** The most persistent line of attack, and the least satisfactorily answered. John Tumazos in July 2025: the portfolio "was up to $1.063 billion at June 30, should we post every night on the Investor Relations page… the value of your total stockholdings, just like you're a hedge fund manager?… is this a good opportunity to take $0.5 billion off the table?" Tanya Jakusconek pressed the same point in October 2025. Management's defence is coherent — small early stakes are how they learn about assets before committing real capital, and "the money belongs to our owners… we do not simply take that gain and allocate it to future equity investments." They also acted on it, selling $400 million of equity investments in the third quarter of 2025. But they then committed C$60 million to a pre-listing private placement in Cadillac Mines on 23 July 2026. A senior producer running a venture book is a governance oddity, and analysts keep saying so.

**Selling non-core assets at a cyclical high.** Asked directly and repeatedly in 2025 whether high gold prices were an opportunity to monetise the long tail of early-stage options. The answer each time was a soft yes that never converted: "it's not an unreasonable question" (July 2025); "there are some projects… we will be disposing of them. And frankly… at these current gold prices, it's not a bad time to in some cases, sell those assets" (October 2025). Nothing material has been sold. Gold is now 26% off its high. The window analysts flagged has narrowed.

**Reserve pricing and cut-off grade.** Jakusconek pushed twice on whether the company would raise its reserve gold-price assumption. The answer was a firm no beyond inflation: reserves are booked near $1,450 an ounce and resources near $1,750, and the intention is "to keep the cut-off rate stable while… offsetting inflation." This is the *conservative* answer and it is a credibility asset — but it also means the reserve figure understates what is economically mineable at a $4,155 gold price, which matters for how the asset base is valued.

**What analysts are not asking enough about.** The 2027 and 2028 production hole from the Barnat redesign — up to 150,000 ounces a year in both years, roughly 4% of output — landed alongside a record-cash-flow headline and drew far less questioning than the equity portfolio did.

## 6. Red flags and green flags

**Concerning**

- **Buying back stock near the cycle high.** In the second quarter of 2026 the company repurchased 2,235,947 shares at an average of $178.86, spending $400 million. At $152.14 today that position is marked down about 15%. Repurchasing equity at the top of a commodity cycle is the sector's oldest sin. Mitigating: this is a rules-based programme (a stated target of returning roughly 40% of free cash flow), not an opportunistic bet, and it was funded from $1,335 million of quarterly free cash flow with net cash still rising.
- **Three fatalities in twelve months** (Fosterville, December 2025; Canadian Malartic, April 2026; Upper Beaver, May 2026), against 23 in the company's entire seventy-year history. The root-cause disclosure in the July 2026 call is unusually candid — including the admission at Canadian Malartic that engineering controls had been in place for over a decade and there had been "an erosion of the controls." Candour is a credit; a control-erosion finding at a company whose whole pitch is operational excellence is not.
- **Barnat pit wall movement, 1 July 2026.** Reduces production by 60,000–80,000 ounces in the second half of 2026 and up to 150,000 ounces in each of 2027 and 2028.
- **Capital guidance raised 18% mid-year**, from $2.2–2.4 billion to $2.605–2.825 billion, on top of a $525 million exploration budget and $578 million spent on Finnish properties in one quarter.
- **A small spoken-versus-filed discrepancy on Hope Bay.** The chief executive described Hope Bay on the July 2026 call as "producing between 400 and 450 thousand ounces a year"; the filed release says 400,000–435,000 ounces. A 15,000-ounce upward rounding in the spoken version. Minor, but it is exactly the kind of drift this exercise looks for, and it is worth noting that it moved up, not down.
- **Margin rolled over sequentially** in the second quarter of 2026 — gross margin 62.2% against 66.4% — while the narrative stayed on "record."

**Positive**

- **No dilution.** Diluted share count went from 504.4 million (second quarter 2025) to 502.9 million (second quarter 2026) — down, not up, despite an acquisition. In a sector where serial equity issuance is the default funding model, this is the single most important clean-hands finding in the file.
- **Genuine deleveraging into net cash.** About $1.6 billion of gross debt repaid over eighteen months, ending at $3.267 billion of net cash, with independent confirmation from two ratings agencies (Moody's to A3, Fitch to A−).
- **Reserves replaced with growth**, at a deliberately conservative $1,450-an-ounce assumption, while mining 3.0 million ounces.
- **Fast, unflattering disclosure.** The Barnat impact was quantified and guidance re-framed within twenty-four hours.
- **Cost claims that survive third-party checking.** The $1,459 all-in sustaining cost is genuinely the lowest among the senior producers.
- **Structural cost advantages that are specific and checkable**: hydro and nuclear grid power in Ontario and Quebec, hedged and pre-barged diesel in Nunavut, a majority-underground production mix that uses less diesel than open-pit peers.

## 7. Hidden signals

**The reserve price is the buried asset.** Reserves are stated at roughly $1,450 an ounce against a $4,155 spot price. Management has explicitly refused to lower cut-off grades to chase ounces: "we don't want to lower the cutoff grade. We don't want to change the mining sequence. We are committed to… send the right ton first at the mill" (Q3 2025). That discipline protects the mine plan and it also means the stated 55.4 million ounces is a floor, not an estimate. The conversion pace is disclosed too — about 500,000 ounces a year from inferred resource to reserve, moving increasingly to cheaper underground drilling.

**"We are only in the 3rd or 4th inning."** New language in July 2026. Combined with the shift of excitement to "the early 2030s," this is a management team asking to be judged on a horizon longer than most shareholders hold. That is either admirable or a way of deferring accountability, and only execution will separate the two.

**A new regional voice.** Jussi was introduced in July 2026 to run the Northern European business, with a mandate to "more than double that business to over 500 thousand ounces." Note the careful hedging elsewhere on the same asset: an executive said of Ikkari, "we will be careful before we will provide any number," and the exploration head clarified that the 500,000-ounce figure is Kittila *and* Ikkari combined, not Ikkari alone. Someone in the room is policing the numbers. That is a healthy sign.

**Treasury detail as a confidence marker.** "We sold gold at an average price of $3,476 per ounce… a full $20 per ounce higher than the spot average in the quarter, well done to the treasury team" (Q3 2025). A company willing to be measured on twenty dollars an ounce is not managing to the headline.

## 8. Capital-allocation verdict

**Trajectory: Strengthening.** Net cash of $3.267 billion where there was net debt eighteen months ago, two credit upgrades, record reserves, a flat-to-falling share count, and a fully self-funded pipeline. Return on equity of 22.6% and return on invested capital of 15.7% on a trailing basis, both rising.

**The single thing to weigh before sizing.** Not the quality of the company — that is established and verifiable. It is that the market has already re-rated *for* the quality. Agnico trades at 7.13 times trailing enterprise value to EBITDA against Newmont at 6.90 times and Alamos at 6.70 times: effectively parity with a peer whose costs run $162 an ounce higher, whose reserve life is shorter, and whose assets sit in harder jurisdictions. Historically Agnico commanded a wide premium for exactly those differences. The premium has closed. And the stock's 40% fall from its 52-week high of $255.24 is not a mispricing to exploit — gold itself is down 26% from its own high, and 40% divided by 26% is 1.54, almost exactly the stock's measured two-year sensitivity to the gold price of 1.48. The shares have fallen precisely as much as the metal move warranted, no more. There is no washout here to buy, only a business fairly priced against a lower gold price.

**Economic moat: narrow, and widening.** A gold miner is a price-taker; it cannot have a wide moat in the pricing-power sense. What it can have is a durable cost and jurisdiction advantage over the companies it competes with for capital, and Agnico has the best version of it in the senior tier: the lowest all-in sustaining cost, a roughly sixteen-year reserve life (55.4 million ounces against about 3.4 million mined a year), assets concentrated in Canada, Finland, Australia and Mexico rather than politically fragile jurisdictions, structural power and fuel advantages, and an A− credit rating. That edge is widening, not narrowing — the cost gap to peers grew over these five calls while reserves grew and share count fell.

**Secular threat: none.** Gold is not being disrupted by technology, regulation or substitution. The risk here is entirely cyclical — the price of the metal — and cyclical risk is not terminal-value impairment. The current cheapness-versus-its-own-history is a price-of-gold print, not a structurally shrinking base. Equally, it is not a bargain: a fair price for a very good business.
